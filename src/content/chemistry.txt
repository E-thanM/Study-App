# 1a States of matter
Describe the arrangement and movement of particles in a solid. :: Close together in a regular pattern; vibrate about fixed positions
Describe the arrangement and movement of particles in a liquid. :: Close together, random arrangement; move around each other
Describe the arrangement and movement of particles in a gas. :: Far apart, random; move quickly in all directions
Name the state changes: solid→liquid, liquid→gas, gas→liquid, liquid→solid, solid→gas. :: Melting, boiling/evaporating, condensing, freezing, sublimation
What happens to particles when a substance is heated and melts? :: They gain energy, vibrate more, overcome some forces of attraction and start to move around each other
What is diffusion? :: The random movement of particles from high to low concentration, so they spread out and mix
Describe an experiment showing diffusion of gases. :: Cotton wool soaked in ammonia at one end of a tube and conc. hydrochloric acid at the other; white ring of ammonium chloride forms nearer the HCl end because ammonia particles are lighter and move faster
What is the difference between dilute and concentrated solution? :: Dilute – small amount of solute per volume of solvent; concentrated – large amount
What does a solubility curve show? :: How the mass of solute that dissolves in 100 g of water varies with temperature
How do you investigate the solubility of a solid at different temperatures? :: Dissolve solute in a known mass of water at a set temperature until saturated, evaporate a sample to dryness, weigh the solid; repeat at different temperatures
C: Particles in a solid have the {{c1::least}} energy and particles in a gas have the {{c2::most}} energy.
What happens to particles when a liquid boils? :: They gain enough energy to overcome the forces of attraction between them and move far apart as a gas
What happens to particles when a gas condenses? :: They lose energy and slow down; forces of attraction pull them close together into a liquid
What happens to particles when a liquid freezes? :: They lose energy, slow down and settle into fixed positions in a regular arrangement
What is the difference between evaporation and boiling? :: Evaporation happens only at the surface, at temperatures below the boiling point ;; Boiling happens throughout the liquid at the boiling point
C: {{c1::Sublimation}} is when a solid turns directly into a gas without becoming a liquid (e.g. iodine, solid carbon dioxide).
Why does the temperature stay constant while a substance is melting or boiling? :: The energy supplied is used to overcome forces of attraction between particles instead of raising the temperature
Describe the potassium manganate(VII) dilution experiment. :: Dissolve a few purple crystals in water; take a small volume and dilute it with water; repeat many times – the purple colour fades but is still visible after several dilutions
What does the potassium manganate(VII) dilution experiment show about particles? :: A tiny crystal contains a very large number of very small particles, which spread out evenly through the water
Why does a crystal of potassium manganate(VII) colour all the water in a beaker even without stirring? :: Diffusion – the random movement of the particles spreads them from high to low concentration until evenly mixed
Why is diffusion faster in gases than in liquids? :: Gas particles move faster and there is more space between them
Why is diffusion faster at a higher temperature? :: Particles have more kinetic energy so move faster
Write the equation for the reaction in the ammonia and hydrogen chloride diffusion tube. :: NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s)
Explain why the white ring of ammonium chloride forms closer to the hydrochloric acid end. :: Ammonia (Mr 17) has a lower relative formula mass than HCl (Mr 36.5) ;; so ammonia particles move faster and travel further in the same time
Describe the diffusion of bromine vapour in a gas jar. :: Brown bromine vapour spreads into the jar of air above until the colour is the same throughout; it is almost instant into a vacuum because there are no air particles to collide with
C: A {{c1::solvent}} is the liquid in which a solute dissolves.
C: A {{c1::solute}} is the substance that dissolves in a solvent.
C: A {{c1::solution}} is a mixture formed when a solute dissolves in a solvent.
C: A {{c1::saturated solution}} is a solution in which no more solute can dissolve at that temperature.
What is meant by solubility? :: The mass of solute that dissolves in 100 g of solvent to form a saturated solution at a particular temperature
What are the units of solubility? :: g per 100 g of solvent (e.g. g/100 g water)
How does the solubility of most solids in water change as temperature increases? :: It increases
How does the solubility of a gas in water change as temperature increases? :: It decreases
Name a salt whose solubility changes very little with temperature. :: Sodium chloride
How do you calculate solubility from experimental masses? :: Solubility = (mass of dissolved solid ÷ mass of water) × 100 ;; units g per 100 g water
Worked: 12.0 g of a salt dissolves in 40.0 g of water to make a saturated solution. What is its solubility? :: 12.0 ÷ 40.0 × 100 = 30 g per 100 g water
How do you use a solubility curve to find the mass of crystals formed when a saturated solution is cooled? :: Read the solubility at the starting temperature and at the final temperature; mass of crystals = difference (for 100 g of water – scale up or down for other masses)
Worked: solubility of KNO₃ is 110 g/100 g water at 60 °C and 30 g/100 g water at 20 °C. Mass of crystals when a saturated solution in 100 g water is cooled from 60 °C to 20 °C? :: 110 − 30 = 80 g
How do you use a solubility curve to find the temperature at which crystals first appear? :: Find the solubility value equal to the mass dissolved per 100 g water and read across to the curve and down to the temperature
Describe how to find the solubility of a solid in water at a specific temperature. :: Add excess solid to water in a water bath at the set temperature; stir until no more dissolves (solid remains); pour some solution (no solid) into a weighed evaporating basin and weigh; evaporate to dryness and reweigh ;; mass of water = mass of solution − mass of dry solid
In the solubility practical, how do you know the solution is saturated? :: Undissolved solid remains even after stirring
In the solubility practical, why is the residue heated to constant mass? :: To make sure all the water has evaporated, so the mass of solid is correct
In the solubility practical, why must no undissolved solid be transferred to the evaporating basin? :: It would increase the mass of solid and give a solubility that is too high
On a solubility curve graph, which variable goes on each axis? :: x-axis – temperature (independent variable) ;; y-axis – solubility in g per 100 g water (dependent variable)
Explain, in terms of particles, why a gas can be compressed but a solid cannot. :: Gas particles are far apart with lots of space between them ;; solid particles are already touching

# 1b Elements, compounds and mixtures
How can you tell if a substance is pure using melting point? :: A pure substance melts at a specific temperature; an impure substance melts over a range (and at a lower temperature)
Name the separation technique for an insoluble solid from a liquid. :: Filtration
Name the separation technique for a soluble solid from a solution. :: Crystallisation (evaporate some solvent, leave to cool and crystallise)
Name the separation technique for a liquid from a solution. :: Simple distillation
Name the separation technique for a mixture of liquids with different boiling points. :: Fractional distillation
Name the separation technique for different dyes in ink. :: Paper chromatography
How do you calculate an Rf value? :: Rf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent front
In chromatography, what does a single spot show? :: The substance is pure (one compound)
Why is the baseline drawn in pencil in chromatography? :: Pencil is insoluble in the solvent so it won't run
Why must the baseline be above the solvent level? :: So the spots don't dissolve into the solvent in the beaker
C: An {{c1::element}} is a substance made of only one type of atom.
C: A {{c1::compound}} is a substance containing two or more elements chemically combined.
C: A {{c1::mixture}} contains two or more substances that are not chemically combined.
What is a pure substance in chemistry? :: A single element or a single compound, not mixed with any other substance
Describe how to carry out filtration. :: Fold filter paper into a cone in a funnel; pour the mixture through; the insoluble solid stays in the paper and the liquid passes through
C: In filtration, the solid left in the filter paper is the {{c1::residue}} and the liquid that passes through is the {{c2::filtrate}}.
Describe how to obtain crystals of a soluble solid from its solution. :: Heat the solution to evaporate some of the water until the crystallisation point; leave to cool so crystals form; filter off the crystals; dry them between filter papers or in a warm oven
How do you know a solution has reached its crystallisation point? :: Crystals form on a glass rod dipped in the solution and taken out to cool (or crystals appear at the edge)
Why is a solution not heated to dryness when making hydrated salt crystals? :: Heating to dryness can remove the water of crystallisation (or decompose the salt) and gives a powder instead of crystals
Describe simple distillation. :: Heat the solution in a flask; the solvent boils and its vapour passes into a condenser, where it cools and condenses; the pure liquid (distillate) is collected; the solute stays in the flask
How can pure water be obtained from sea water? :: Simple distillation
What is the job of the condenser in distillation? :: To cool the vapour so that it condenses back into a liquid
Why does cooling water enter a Liebig condenser at the bottom? :: So the condenser fills completely with cold water, giving efficient cooling
Where should the thermometer bulb be in distillation and why? :: Level with the side arm leading to the condenser – to measure the temperature of the vapour being distilled
Describe how to separate ethanol from water by fractional distillation. :: Heat the mixture; vapour rises up a fractionating column (hottest at the bottom); ethanol (lower boiling point, 78 °C) reaches the top and passes into the condenser first; collect it while the temperature stays at 78 °C; water (100 °C) distils later
What is the purpose of the fractionating column? :: It gives a temperature gradient and a large surface area so vapours repeatedly condense and re-evaporate, separating liquids with similar boiling points
Explain how paper chromatography separates the dyes in an ink. :: Dyes have different solubilities in the solvent and different attractions to the paper ;; more soluble dyes travel further up the paper with the solvent
C: In paper chromatography the solvent is the {{c1::mobile}} phase and the paper is the {{c2::stationary}} phase.
What does a chromatogram with three spots from one sample show? :: The sample is a mixture of at least three substances
How can chromatography identify the substances in a mixture? :: Run known reference substances on the same paper with the same solvent; a spot at the same height (same Rf value) as a reference is likely to be the same substance
Why might a spot stay on the baseline? :: The substance is insoluble in the solvent used
What range of values can an Rf value have? :: Between 0 and 1 (it has no units)
Worked: a spot moves 3.6 cm and the solvent front moves 8.0 cm. What is the Rf value? :: 3.6 ÷ 8.0 = 0.45
Why must the solvent front be marked as soon as the paper is removed? :: The solvent evaporates quickly, so the front would no longer be visible
Why is a lid put on the beaker in chromatography? :: To stop the solvent evaporating from the paper (keeps the air saturated with solvent vapour)
Describe the paper chromatography practical for inks or food colourings. :: Draw a pencil baseline about 1 cm from the bottom; put small spots of each ink on it; stand the paper in a beaker with solvent below the baseline; cover; remove when the solvent is near the top; mark the solvent front; dry; measure distances and calculate Rf values
Why should the spots of ink be small? :: So they don't spread into each other and the dyes separate clearly
How would you get salt from a mixture of salt and sand? :: Add water and stir to dissolve the salt; filter to remove the sand; evaporate/crystallise the filtrate to get salt
Why can sand be separated from salt by dissolving and filtering? :: Salt is soluble in water but sand is insoluble

# 1c Atomic structure
Give the relative mass and charge of a proton, neutron and electron. :: Proton: mass 1, charge +1 ;; Neutron: mass 1, charge 0 ;; Electron: mass 1/1836 (negligible), charge −1
What is the atomic number? :: The number of protons in an atom
What is the mass number? :: The total number of protons and neutrons in an atom
What are isotopes? :: Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons
What is relative atomic mass (Ar)? :: The average mass of an atom of an element compared to 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom (weighted mean of its isotopes)
How do you calculate relative atomic mass from isotope abundances? :: Ar = Σ(isotope mass × % abundance) ÷ 100
What is the maximum number of electrons in the first three shells? :: 2, 8, 8
Write the electron configuration of sodium. :: 2.8.1
What does the group number tell you about electrons? :: The number of electrons in the outer shell
What does the period number tell you? :: The number of occupied electron shells
What is an atom? :: The smallest particle of an element that can exist (with no overall charge)
What is a molecule? :: Two or more atoms chemically (covalently) bonded together
Describe the structure of an atom. :: A tiny central nucleus containing protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons arranged in shells
C: Almost all the mass of an atom is concentrated in the {{c1::nucleus}}.
Why does an atom have no overall charge? :: It contains equal numbers of protons (+) and electrons (−)
How do you work out the number of neutrons in an atom? :: Mass number − atomic number
How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in an atom of sodium-23 (atomic number 11)? :: 11 protons, 12 neutrons, 11 electrons
How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in a chloride ion ³⁵Cl⁻ (atomic number 17)? :: 17 protons, 18 neutrons, 18 electrons
Why do isotopes of the same element have the same chemical properties? :: They have the same number of electrons (same electronic configuration)
Why is relative atomic mass often not a whole number? :: It is a weighted mean (average) of the masses of the element's isotopes
Worked: chlorine is 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37. Calculate its Ar. :: (35 × 75 + 37 × 25) ÷ 100 = 35.5

# 1d The Periodic Table
How are elements arranged in the Periodic Table? :: In order of increasing atomic number, in groups (columns) with similar properties and periods (rows)
Where are metals and non-metals in the Periodic Table? :: Metals on the left and centre; non-metals on the right
How can you classify an element as a metal or non-metal using its oxide? :: Metal oxides are basic; non-metal oxides are acidic
Why do elements in the same group have similar reactions? :: They have the same number of outer electrons
How do you deduce the electronic configuration of an element from its position in the Periodic Table? :: Number of shells = period number; electrons in the outer shell = group number (group 0 has a full outer shell)
Electronic configuration of carbon (atomic number 6)? :: 2.4
Electronic configuration of nitrogen (atomic number 7)? :: 2.5
Electronic configuration of oxygen (atomic number 8)? :: 2.6
Electronic configuration of fluorine (atomic number 9)? :: 2.7
Electronic configuration of magnesium (atomic number 12)? :: 2.8.2
Electronic configuration of aluminium (atomic number 13)? :: 2.8.3
Electronic configuration of silicon (atomic number 14)? :: 2.8.4
Electronic configuration of phosphorus (atomic number 15)? :: 2.8.5
Electronic configuration of sulfur (atomic number 16)? :: 2.8.6
Electronic configuration of chlorine (atomic number 17)? :: 2.8.7
Electronic configuration of argon (atomic number 18)? :: 2.8.8
Electronic configuration of potassium (atomic number 19)? :: 2.8.8.1
Electronic configuration of calcium (atomic number 20)? :: 2.8.8.2
An element has electronic configuration 2.8.6. Which group and period is it in? :: Group 6, period 3 (sulfur)
How does electrical conductivity distinguish metals from non-metals? :: Metals conduct electricity; non-metals do not (except graphite)
Is magnesium oxide acidic or basic? :: Basic – it is a metal oxide (reacts with acids; any solution is alkaline)
Is sulfur dioxide acidic or basic? :: Acidic – it is a non-metal oxide; it dissolves in water to form an acidic solution
Explain why the noble gases do not readily react. :: Their atoms have full outer shells (2 for helium, 8 for the others) ;; so they do not easily lose, gain or share electrons
Explain why lithium, sodium and potassium have similar chemical properties. :: They all have one electron in their outer shell, so they react in the same way (losing one electron to form 1+ ions)

# 1e Chemical formulae, equations and calculations
What is relative formula mass (Mr)? :: The sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in the formula
What is a mole? :: The amount of substance containing 6.02 × 10²³ particles (the Avogadro constant); one mole of a substance has a mass in grams equal to its Mr
Write the equation linking moles, mass and Mr. :: moles = mass ÷ Mr
Write the equation linking concentration, moles and volume. :: concentration (mol/dm³) = moles ÷ volume (dm³)
What volume does one mole of any gas occupy at room temperature and pressure? :: 24 dm³ (24 000 cm³)
How do you calculate percentage yield? :: % yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100
How do you find an empirical formula? :: Divide the mass (or %) of each element by its Ar, divide by the smallest answer to get a whole-number ratio
What is an empirical formula? :: The simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound
What is a molecular formula? :: The actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule
How do you find the molecular formula from the empirical formula? :: Divide Mr by the empirical formula mass, then multiply the empirical formula by that number
Describe an experiment to find the formula of magnesium oxide. :: Weigh crucible + Mg; heat strongly with lid lifted occasionally until no more change; reweigh; calculate masses of Mg and O; find moles and ratio
What is water of crystallisation? :: Water molecules chemically bonded within a crystal (e.g. CuSO₄·5H₂O)
How do you calculate the percentage of water of crystallisation? :: Heat the hydrated salt to constant mass; mass lost = water ;; **% water** = mass of water lost ÷ mass of hydrated salt × 100 ;; (to find x in ·xH₂O, compare moles of anhydrous salt and water instead)
How do you convert cm³ to dm³? :: Divide by 1000
C: The {{c1::mole (mol)}} is the unit for amount of substance.
What do the state symbols (s), (l), (g) and (aq) mean? :: Solid, liquid, gas, aqueous (dissolved in water)
Why must chemical equations be balanced? :: Atoms are not created or destroyed, so there must be the same number of each type of atom on both sides (mass is conserved)
Balance: Mg + O₂ → MgO :: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
Balance: CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O :: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
Balance: Na + H₂O → NaOH + H₂ :: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂
Balance: Al + O₂ → Al₂O₃ :: 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃
Balance: Fe₂O₃ + CO → Fe + CO₂ :: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂
What is an ionic equation? :: An equation showing only the ions/particles that take part in the reaction, leaving out spectator ions
What are spectator ions? :: Ions present in the reaction mixture that do not change during the reaction
Write the ionic equation for the precipitation of silver chloride. :: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)
Write the ionic equation for the precipitation of barium sulfate. :: Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)
Write the ionic equation for magnesium displacing copper from copper(II) sulfate solution. :: Mg(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)
Worked: calculate the Mr of CaCO₃ (Ca 40, C 12, O 16). :: 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100
Worked: calculate the Mr of CuSO₄·5H₂O (Cu 63.5, S 32, O 16, H 1). :: 63.5 + 32 + 64 + (5 × 18) = 249.5
How do you calculate mass from moles? :: mass (g) = moles × Mr
Worked: how many moles are in 8.0 g of sodium hydroxide, NaOH (Mr 40)? :: 8.0 ÷ 40 = 0.20 mol
Worked: what is the mass of 0.25 mol of carbon dioxide (Mr 44)? :: 0.25 × 44 = 11 g
How do you calculate a reacting mass from an equation? :: 1. Moles of known substance = mass ÷ Mr ;; 2. Use the equation's mole ratio to find moles of the other substance ;; 3. Mass = moles × Mr
Worked: what mass of calcium oxide forms when 50 g of CaCO₃ is heated? (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂; Mr CaCO₃ 100, CaO 56) :: 50 ÷ 100 = 0.50 mol CaCO₃ → 0.50 mol CaO ;; 0.50 × 56 = 28 g
What is the limiting reactant? :: The reactant that is completely used up, so it limits the amount of product formed (the other reactant is in excess)
What is the theoretical yield? :: The maximum mass of product calculated from the equation, assuming all the limiting reactant reacts and nothing is lost
Give reasons why the percentage yield of a reaction is less than 100%. :: Reaction may be incomplete or reversible ;; some product lost during separation (filtering, transferring) ;; side reactions produce other products
Worked: the theoretical yield is 28 g but only 21 g is obtained. What is the percentage yield? :: 21 ÷ 28 × 100 = 75%
How do you calculate the percentage by mass of an element in a compound? :: (Ar × number of atoms of that element ÷ Mr) × 100
Worked: percentage by mass of nitrogen in ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃ (Mr 80; N 14). :: (2 × 14) ÷ 80 × 100 = 35%
How do you calculate atom economy? :: Atom economy = (Mr of desired product ÷ total Mr of all reactants) × 100
Worked: 2.4 g of carbon combines with 0.6 g of hydrogen. Find the empirical formula (C 12, H 1). :: C: 2.4 ÷ 12 = 0.2 ;; H: 0.6 ÷ 1 = 0.6 ;; ratio 1 : 3 → CH₃
Worked: a compound is 40% Ca, 12% C and 48% O by mass. Find its empirical formula (Ca 40, C 12, O 16). :: Ca 40 ÷ 40 = 1; C 12 ÷ 12 = 1; O 48 ÷ 16 = 3 → CaCO₃
Worked: a compound has empirical formula CH₂ and Mr 56. Find its molecular formula. :: Mass of CH₂ = 14; 56 ÷ 14 = 4 → C₄H₈
Worked: 1.2 g of magnesium forms 2.0 g of magnesium oxide. Find the formula (Mg 24, O 16). :: Mass of O = 0.8 g ;; Mg 1.2 ÷ 24 = 0.05; O 0.8 ÷ 16 = 0.05 ;; ratio 1 : 1 → MgO
In the magnesium oxide experiment, why is the crucible lid lifted from time to time? :: To let oxygen in to react with the magnesium, while stopping magnesium oxide smoke escaping
In the magnesium oxide experiment, why is the crucible heated to constant mass? :: To make sure all the magnesium has reacted
Why might the mass of magnesium oxide obtained be lower than expected? :: Some magnesium oxide smoke escaped when the lid was lifted, or not all the magnesium reacted
Describe how to find the formula of copper(II) oxide by reduction. :: Weigh a tube containing copper(II) oxide; pass hydrogen or methane over it and heat strongly (burn off excess gas at the end); heat to constant mass; let it cool in the gas stream; reweigh ;; mass lost = mass of oxygen; work out mole ratio Cu : O
In the reduction of copper(II) oxide, what colour change is seen? :: Black to pink-brown (copper)
In the reduction of copper(II) oxide, why is the copper left to cool in the gas stream? :: To stop the hot copper reacting with oxygen in the air and being re-oxidised
In the reduction of copper(II) oxide, why is the excess hydrogen burned off? :: So unburned hydrogen does not escape into the room, where it could form an explosive mixture with air
Worked: 2.50 g of hydrated copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄·xH₂O, is heated to constant mass leaving 1.60 g. Find x (CuSO₄ = 159.5, H₂O = 18). :: Water = 0.90 g = 0.05 mol ;; CuSO₄ = 1.60 ÷ 159.5 = 0.01 mol ;; ratio 1 : 5 → x = 5
How do you know all the water of crystallisation has been removed? :: The mass stays the same on repeated heating and weighing (constant mass)
C: Concentration in g/dm³ = {{c1::mass of solute (g)}} ÷ {{c2::volume of solution (dm³)}}
How do you convert a concentration in mol/dm³ to g/dm³? :: Multiply by the Mr
Worked: 4.0 g of NaOH (Mr 40) is dissolved to make 250 cm³ of solution. Concentration in mol/dm³? :: 4.0 ÷ 40 = 0.10 mol; 250 cm³ = 0.250 dm³ ;; 0.10 ÷ 0.250 = 0.40 mol/dm³
Worked: express 0.40 mol/dm³ NaOH (Mr 40) in g/dm³. :: 0.40 × 40 = 16 g/dm³
Worked: how many moles of HCl are in 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ solution? :: 0.100 × (25.0 ÷ 1000) = 0.00250 mol
How do you work out an unknown concentration from titration results? :: 1. Moles of known solution = concentration × volume (dm³) ;; 2. Use the equation ratio to find moles of the other ;; 3. Concentration = moles ÷ volume (dm³)
Worked: 25.0 cm³ of NaOH is neutralised by 20.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ HCl. Find the NaOH concentration. :: HCl = 0.100 × 0.0200 = 0.00200 mol ;; 1 : 1 ratio → 0.00200 mol NaOH ;; 0.00200 ÷ 0.0250 = 0.0800 mol/dm³
Worked: 25.0 cm³ of NaOH is neutralised by 12.5 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ H₂SO₄ (2NaOH + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O). Find the NaOH concentration. :: H₂SO₄ = 0.00125 mol ;; NaOH = 2 × 0.00125 = 0.00250 mol ;; 0.00250 ÷ 0.0250 = 0.100 mol/dm³
C: Volume of gas at rtp (dm³) = moles × {{c1::24}}
How do you calculate the moles of a gas from its volume in cm³ at rtp? :: moles = volume (cm³) ÷ 24 000
What does rtp stand for? :: Room temperature and pressure
Worked: what volume does 0.50 mol of oxygen occupy at rtp? :: 0.50 × 24 = 12 dm³
Worked: what volume of CO₂ at rtp is made when 10 g of CaCO₃ (Mr 100) reacts with excess acid? :: 10 ÷ 100 = 0.10 mol CaCO₃ → 0.10 mol CO₂ ;; 0.10 × 24 = 2.4 dm³
Worked: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. What volume of oxygen reacts with 40 cm³ of hydrogen (same conditions)? :: 20 cm³ – for gases, volume ratio = mole ratio
Worked: how many molecules are in 0.50 mol of water? :: 0.50 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 3.01 × 10²³ molecules
How do you identify the limiting reactant from masses? :: Work out the moles of each reactant ;; divide each by its coefficient in the balanced equation ;; the smaller answer is the limiting reactant
Worked: 2.4 g of Mg (Ar 24) is added to 0.10 mol of HCl (Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂). Which reactant is limiting? :: Mg = 2.4 ÷ 24 = 0.10 mol, which would need 0.20 mol HCl ;; only 0.10 mol HCl is present, so **HCl is limiting** (Mg in excess)
Worked: how many moles are in 60 cm³ of a gas at rtp? :: 60 ÷ 24 000 = 0.0025 mol
Worked: what volume of hydrogen at rtp forms when 0.65 g of zinc (Ar 65) reacts with excess acid? (Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂) :: 0.65 ÷ 65 = 0.010 mol Zn → 0.010 mol H₂ ;; 0.010 × 24 000 = 240 cm³
Worked: what mass of NaOH (Mr 40) is needed to make 250 cm³ of 0.200 mol/dm³ solution? :: moles = 0.200 × 0.250 = 0.0500 mol ;; mass = 0.0500 × 40 = 2.0 g
Worked: calculate the atom economy for making CaO by heating CaCO₃ (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂; Mr CaCO₃ 100, CaO 56). :: 56 ÷ 100 × 100 = 56%
Why is a high atom economy desirable? :: Less waste product, fewer raw materials used, so more sustainable and cheaper
What is the difference between a hydrated and an anhydrous salt? :: Hydrated – contains water of crystallisation ;; Anhydrous – contains no water of crystallisation
How do you write a word equation? :: Reactants (names) on the left, an arrow, products on the right, e.g. magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide
What does the coefficient in front of a formula (e.g. 2H₂O) mean? :: The number of particles (molecules or moles) of that substance; it multiplies everything in the formula

# 1f Ionic bonding
What is an ion? :: An atom or group of atoms with a charge, formed by losing or gaining electrons
How are ionic bonds formed? :: Electrons are transferred from metal atoms to non-metal atoms, forming oppositely charged ions held by strong electrostatic attraction
What charge do ions of group 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7 elements have? :: +1, +2, +3, −3, −2, −1
Give the formulae of hydroxide, nitrate, carbonate, sulfate and ammonium ions. :: OH⁻, NO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻, SO₄²⁻, NH₄⁺
Describe the structure of an ionic compound. :: Giant ionic lattice – a regular arrangement of oppositely charged ions held by strong electrostatic forces
Why do ionic compounds have high melting points? :: Lots of energy is needed to overcome the strong electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions in the giant lattice
When do ionic compounds conduct electricity? :: When molten or dissolved in water – the ions are free to move and carry charge
C: Metal atoms {{c1::lose}} electrons to form positive ions; non-metal atoms {{c2::gain}} electrons to form negative ions.
What are the charges on silver, copper(II), iron(II), iron(III), lead(II) and zinc ions? :: Ag⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺, Pb²⁺, Zn²⁺
What is the formula of the hydrogen ion? :: H⁺
What does the Roman numeral in a name like iron(III) chloride tell you? :: The charge on the metal ion (Fe³⁺)
What does the ending -ate usually tell you about a compound? :: It contains oxygen as well, e.g. sulfate (SO₄²⁻), nitrate (NO₃⁻), carbonate (CO₃²⁻)
How do you work out the formula of an ionic compound? :: Use enough of each ion so the total positive charge equals the total negative charge (e.g. Al³⁺ and O²⁻ → Al₂O₃)
magnesium chloride <> MgCl₂
aluminium oxide <> Al₂O₃
calcium hydroxide <> Ca(OH)₂
ammonium sulfate <> (NH₄)₂SO₄
sodium carbonate <> Na₂CO₃
iron(III) nitrate <> Fe(NO₃)₃
lead(II) nitrate <> Pb(NO₃)₂
silver nitrate <> AgNO₃
magnesium nitride <> Mg₃N₂
iron(II) sulfate <> FeSO₄
zinc chloride <> ZnCl₂

Describe the formation of sodium chloride in terms of electrons. :: The sodium atom (2.8.1) transfers its outer electron to a chlorine atom (2.8.7) ;; forming Na⁺ [2.8] and Cl⁻ [2.8.8] ions with full outer shells, which attract each other

# 1g Covalent bonding
What is a covalent bond? :: A shared pair of electrons between two non-metal atoms (strong electrostatic attraction between the shared pair and the nuclei)
Why do simple molecular substances have low melting and boiling points? :: Only weak intermolecular forces need to be overcome, not the strong covalent bonds
How does the size of a molecule affect boiling point? :: Larger molecules (higher Mr) have stronger intermolecular forces, so higher boiling points
Why don't simple molecular substances conduct electricity? :: There are no charged particles (ions or delocalised electrons) free to move
Describe the structure of diamond and its properties. :: Each carbon bonded covalently to 4 others in a giant lattice – very hard, very high melting point, doesn't conduct electricity – used in cutting tools
Describe the structure of graphite and its properties. :: Each carbon bonded to 3 others in layers; weak forces between layers so they slide (soft, lubricant); one delocalised electron per atom so it conducts electricity (electrodes)
Describe the structure of C₆₀ fullerene. :: Simple molecular – a hollow sphere of 60 carbon atoms; weak intermolecular forces so it is soft and slippery, low sublimation point
Why do giant covalent substances have high melting points? :: Many strong covalent bonds must be broken
Describe the bonding in a hydrogen molecule, H₂. :: One shared pair of electrons – a single covalent bond (H–H)
Describe the bonding in a chlorine molecule, Cl₂. :: One shared pair (single bond); each chlorine atom also has three non-bonding (lone) pairs
Describe the bonding in hydrogen chloride, HCl. :: One shared pair between H and Cl; chlorine has three lone pairs
Describe the bonding in an oxygen molecule, O₂. :: Two shared pairs – a double bond (O=O); each oxygen has two lone pairs
Describe the bonding in a nitrogen molecule, N₂. :: Three shared pairs – a triple bond (N≡N); each nitrogen has one lone pair
Describe the bonding in water, H₂O. :: Two single O–H covalent bonds; oxygen has two lone pairs
Describe the bonding in ammonia, NH₃. :: Three single N–H covalent bonds; nitrogen has one lone pair
Describe the bonding in methane, CH₄. :: Four single C–H covalent bonds; no lone pairs
Describe the bonding in carbon dioxide, CO₂. :: Two C=O double bonds (O=C=O); each oxygen has two lone pairs
Describe the bonding in ethane, C₂H₆. :: One C–C single bond and six C–H single bonds
Describe the bonding in ethene, C₂H₄. :: One C=C double bond (two shared pairs) and four C–H single bonds
Describe the bonding in chloromethane, CH₃Cl. :: Three C–H and one C–Cl single bonds; chlorine has three lone pairs
In a covalent dot-and-cross diagram, where are the shared electrons drawn? :: In the overlapping area of the two atoms' outer shells

Why are simple molecular substances usually gases or liquids at room temperature? :: Intermolecular forces are weak, so little energy is needed to separate the molecules
Explain why diamond is very hard. :: Each carbon atom is covalently bonded to four others in a rigid giant 3D lattice of strong bonds
Explain why diamond does not conduct electricity. :: All four outer electrons of each carbon are used in covalent bonds – there are no delocalised electrons

# 1h Metallic bonding
Describe metallic bonding. :: A giant lattice of positive metal ions in a sea of delocalised electrons, held by strong electrostatic attraction
Why do metals conduct electricity? :: Delocalised electrons are free to move through the structure
Why are metals malleable? :: The layers of positive ions can slide over each other without breaking the metallic bonds
How do you draw metallic bonding? :: Rows of positive ions (circles with +) in a regular lattice, with electrons (e⁻) drawn between them
Why do metals usually have high melting points? :: The strong electrostatic attraction between positive ions and delocalised electrons needs a lot of energy to overcome
Why are metals ductile (can be drawn into wires)? :: The layers of positive ions can slide over each other while the delocalised electrons keep holding them together

# 3a Energetics
What is an exothermic reaction? :: A reaction that releases heat energy to the surroundings – temperature rises; ΔH is negative
What is an endothermic reaction? :: A reaction that takes in heat energy from the surroundings – temperature falls; ΔH is positive
Give examples of exothermic and endothermic reactions. :: Exothermic: combustion, neutralisation, displacement, respiration ;; Endothermic: thermal decomposition, photosynthesis, dissolving ammonium nitrate
How do you calculate heat energy change? :: Q = m × c × ΔT (c for water = 4.2 J/g/°C)
How do you calculate molar enthalpy change? :: ΔH = −Q ÷ moles (convert J to kJ)
Explain energy changes in terms of bonds. :: Bond breaking is endothermic; bond making is exothermic ;; ΔH = energy to break bonds − energy released making bonds
What does an energy level (profile) diagram show? :: Energy of reactants and products, activation energy and ΔH; exothermic – products lower than reactants
What is activation energy? :: The minimum energy needed for particles to react
Why is a polystyrene cup used in calorimetry? :: Insulates – reduces heat loss to the surroundings
C: The units of enthalpy change (ΔH) are {{c1::kJ/mol}}.
Describe a calorimetry experiment to find the enthalpy change of neutralisation. :: Measure 25 cm³ of acid into a polystyrene cup (in a beaker) and record its temperature; add 25 cm³ of alkali at the same temperature; stir and record the highest temperature reached
Describe a calorimetry experiment to find the enthalpy change of a displacement reaction. :: Put a measured volume of copper(II) sulfate solution in a polystyrene cup; record the temperature; add excess zinc (or magnesium) powder; stir and record the highest temperature
Describe a calorimetry experiment to find the temperature change when a salt dissolves. :: Measure a volume of water into a polystyrene cup and record its temperature; add a weighed mass of solid (e.g. ammonium nitrate); stir until dissolved and record the lowest (or highest) temperature
Describe a calorimetry experiment to find the enthalpy change of combustion of a fuel. :: Weigh a spirit burner of fuel; put a measured volume of water in a copper can and record its temperature; heat the water with the burner until the temperature rises by about 20 °C; record the final temperature; reweigh the burner
In combustion calorimetry, why is a copper can used? :: Copper is a good conductor of heat, so heat passes quickly to the water
Give reasons why the measured enthalpy of combustion is less exothermic than the data book value. :: Heat lost to the surroundings (air, can) ;; incomplete combustion (soot) ;; fuel evaporates from the wick ;; not standard conditions
How can heat losses be reduced in combustion calorimetry? :: Use a draught shield, put a lid on the can, keep the flame close to the can
In solution calorimetry, why is the mixture stirred? :: So the temperature is the same throughout and all reactants react
In solution calorimetry, what mass is used for m in Q = mcΔT? :: The mass of the solution (water), assuming 1 cm³ has a mass of 1 g – not the mass of the solid
Worked: the temperature of 50 g of water rises by 6.0 °C. Calculate Q (c = 4.2 J/g/°C). :: Q = 50 × 4.2 × 6.0 = 1260 J (1.26 kJ)
Worked: 25 cm³ of 1.0 mol/dm³ HCl is mixed with 25 cm³ of 1.0 mol/dm³ NaOH; temperature rises 6.8 °C. Calculate ΔH. :: Q = 50 × 4.2 × 6.8 = 1428 J ;; moles = 1.0 × 0.025 = 0.025 mol ;; ΔH = −1428 ÷ 0.025 = −57 120 J/mol = **−57 kJ/mol**
Worked: burning 0.46 g of ethanol (Mr 46) heats 100 g of water by 20 °C. Calculate ΔH of combustion. :: Q = 100 × 4.2 × 20 = 8400 J = 8.4 kJ ;; moles = 0.46 ÷ 46 = 0.010 mol ;; ΔH = −8.4 ÷ 0.010 = **−840 kJ/mol**
Why is ΔH given a negative sign for a reaction where the temperature rises? :: The reaction is exothermic – the chemicals lose energy to the surroundings
Describe the energy level diagram for an exothermic reaction. :: Products drawn lower than reactants; downward arrow labelled ΔH (negative)
Describe the energy level diagram for an endothermic reaction. :: Products drawn higher than reactants; upward arrow labelled ΔH (positive)
On a reaction profile, how is the activation energy shown? :: An arrow from the reactants' energy level up to the top of the curve (peak)
Worked: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl. Bond energies (kJ/mol): H–H 436, Cl–Cl 242, H–Cl 431. Calculate ΔH. :: Broken: 436 + 242 = 678 ;; Made: 2 × 431 = 862 ;; ΔH = 678 − 862 = **−184 kJ/mol**
Explain, in terms of bonds, why a reaction is exothermic. :: More energy is released when new bonds are made in the products than is taken in to break bonds in the reactants
Explain, in terms of bonds, why a reaction is endothermic. :: More energy is taken in to break bonds in the reactants than is released when bonds are made in the products
Why might ΔH calculated from bond energies differ from an experimental value? :: Bond energies are average values – the actual energy of a bond varies between different molecules
Is dissolving ammonium nitrate in water exothermic or endothermic? :: Endothermic – the temperature falls

# 3b Rates of reaction
What must happen for particles to react (collision theory)? :: Particles must collide with energy greater than or equal to the activation energy
How does increasing concentration (or pressure) affect rate? :: More particles per unit volume, so more frequent collisions – faster rate
How does increasing surface area affect rate? :: More particles exposed, so more frequent collisions
How does a catalyst work? :: Provides an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy; it is not used up
Describe the sodium thiosulfate 'disappearing cross' experiment. :: Mix sodium thiosulfate with HCl over a cross; time until the cross disappears (sulfur precipitate); repeat at different temperatures/concentrations
How do you calculate rate from a graph? :: Gradient of the curve (tangent at a point) = change in amount ÷ time
C: The rate of reaction depends on the frequency of {{c1::successful collisions}} (collisions with at least the activation energy).
Describe how to investigate the effect of marble chip size on rate using a balance. :: Put acid in a conical flask on a balance with a cotton wool plug; add a known mass of marble chips and start the timer; record the mass every 30 s; repeat with the same mass of smaller chips (or powder), same volume and concentration of acid
In the marble chips experiment, why is cotton wool put in the neck of the flask? :: It lets carbon dioxide escape but stops acid spraying out
Write the equation for marble chips reacting with hydrochloric acid. :: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂
Describe how to investigate the effect of acid concentration on rate using a gas syringe. :: Add marble chips to acid in a flask connected to a gas syringe; record the volume of gas every 30 s; repeat with different concentrations, keeping the volume of acid, mass and size of chips and temperature the same
Give three ways of measuring the rate of a reaction. :: Volume of gas given off over time (gas syringe) ;; mass lost over time (balance) ;; time for a precipitate to hide a cross (or colour change)
On a graph of volume of gas against time, what does a steeper gradient show? :: A faster rate of reaction
Why does a rate graph level off? :: One of the reactants has been used up, so the reaction has stopped
Why is the rate fastest at the start of a reaction? :: The concentration of reactants is highest, so collisions are most frequent
Why do two experiments give the same final volume of gas even though one is faster? :: The same amount of the limiting reactant was used, so the same amount of product forms
How do you calculate the mean rate of a reaction? :: Mean rate = amount of product formed (or reactant used) ÷ time taken
Worked: 48 cm³ of gas is collected in 30 s. Mean rate? :: 48 ÷ 30 = 1.6 cm³/s
Give the units of rate when measuring gas volume and mass. :: cm³/s and g/s
Write the equation for the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide. :: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂
Describe how to investigate the effect of different solid catalysts on the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide. :: Add the same mass of each solid (e.g. MnO₂, CuO) to the same volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide; measure the volume of oxygen in a gas syringe every 10–30 s; compare rates
How can you show that a solid is a catalyst and not used up? :: Filter it off at the end, wash, dry and reweigh – the mass is unchanged (and it still speeds up a fresh reaction)
What is a catalyst? :: A substance that speeds up a reaction but is chemically unchanged at the end
On a reaction profile, how does a catalyst change the diagram? :: The peak (activation energy) is lower; ΔH and the energies of reactants and products are unchanged
Explain why increasing temperature increases the rate of reaction. (4 marks) :: Particles have more kinetic energy and move faster ;; collide more frequently ;; a greater proportion of collisions have energy ≥ activation energy ;; so more successful collisions per second
Explain why powdered calcium carbonate reacts faster than lumps with the same mass. :: Powder has a larger surface area ;; more particles exposed to the acid ;; more frequent collisions, so more successful collisions per second
Explain why increasing the concentration of acid increases the rate. :: More acid particles in the same volume ;; collisions are more frequent ;; more successful collisions per second
How does increasing the pressure of a gas reaction affect rate? :: Same number of particles in a smaller volume, so more frequent collisions – faster rate
In the disappearing cross experiment, how is rate related to the time measured? :: Rate is proportional to 1 ÷ time – a shorter time means a faster rate
Write the equation for sodium thiosulfate reacting with hydrochloric acid. :: Na₂S₂O₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + S + SO₂ + H₂O

# 3c Reversible reactions and equilibria
What symbol shows a reversible reaction? :: ⇌
Give an example of a reversible reaction. :: Heating hydrated copper(II) sulfate (blue → white anhydrous) and adding water back; or ammonium chloride ⇌ ammonia + hydrogen chloride
What is dynamic equilibrium? :: In a closed system, the forward and backward reactions occur at the same rate, so concentrations stay constant
What happens to the equilibrium if temperature is increased? :: It shifts in the endothermic direction
What happens to the equilibrium if pressure is increased? :: It shifts towards the side with fewer gas molecules
What happens to the equilibrium if a catalyst is added? :: No change in position – equilibrium is reached faster
Write the equation and conditions for the Haber process. :: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ ;; 450 °C, 200 atm, iron catalyst
Why is a compromise temperature used in the Haber process? :: Lower temperature gives a higher yield (forward reaction is exothermic) but the rate would be too slow
Write the equation and conditions for the Contact process. :: 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ ;; 450 °C, 1–2 atm, vanadium(V) oxide catalyst
What are the uses of ammonia and sulfuric acid? :: Ammonia – making fertilisers (ammonium nitrate) and nitric acid ;; Sulfuric acid – fertilisers, detergents, paints
C: A reversible reaction is one where the {{c1::products can react to re-form the reactants}}.
Describe what happens when ammonium chloride is heated in a test tube. :: The white solid decomposes into ammonia and hydrogen chloride gases at the hot end; these recombine to form white solid ammonium chloride at the cooler top of the tube
Write the equation for the effect of heat on ammonium chloride. :: NH₄Cl(s) ⇌ NH₃(g) + HCl(g)
Write the equation for the dehydration of hydrated copper(II) sulfate. :: CuSO₄·5H₂O ⇌ CuSO₄ + 5H₂O
What do you see when water is added to anhydrous copper(II) sulfate? :: White solid turns blue and gets hot (exothermic)
If the forward reaction is exothermic, what is the reverse reaction? :: Endothermic, with the same amount of energy transferred
Why must a reaction be in a sealed (closed) container to reach equilibrium? :: So no reactants or products can escape
Explain why a catalyst does not affect the position of equilibrium. :: It speeds up the forward and reverse reactions by the same amount
What happens to the equilibrium if temperature is decreased? :: It shifts in the exothermic direction
What happens to the equilibrium if pressure is decreased? :: It shifts towards the side with more gas molecules
N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ (forward exothermic). Explain the effect of increasing pressure on the yield of ammonia. :: 4 molecules of gas on the left, 2 on the right ;; increasing pressure shifts the equilibrium to the side with fewer gas molecules ;; so to the right – yield of ammonia increases
N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ (forward exothermic). Explain the effect of increasing temperature on the yield of ammonia. :: Equilibrium shifts in the endothermic direction ;; the reverse reaction is endothermic ;; so it shifts left – yield of ammonia decreases
2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ (forward exothermic). What is the effect of increasing pressure? :: Shifts to the right (3 gas molecules → 2) – more SO₃
H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI (all gases). What is the effect of increasing pressure on the position of equilibrium? :: No effect – there are equal numbers of gas molecules on each side
If the forward reaction is endothermic, what happens to the yield when temperature is increased? :: The yield increases – equilibrium shifts to the right (endothermic direction)
How do you decide which way pressure shifts an equilibrium? :: Count the moles of gas on each side of the equation; higher pressure favours the side with fewer gas molecules
Why is a very high pressure not used in the Haber process even though it increases yield? :: It is expensive to build and run equipment that withstands it, and it is dangerous
Do the reactions stop at dynamic equilibrium? :: No – both forward and reverse reactions continue, at equal rates

# 4a Introduction to organic chemistry
What is a hydrocarbon? :: A compound containing only hydrogen and carbon
What is a homologous series? :: A family of compounds with the same general formula, same functional group, similar chemical properties and a trend in physical properties; each member differs by CH₂
What are structural isomers? :: Compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural formulae
What is a functional group? :: The atom or group of atoms that determines the chemical reactions of a compound
Define saturated and unsaturated. :: Saturated – only single C–C bonds ;; Unsaturated – contains at least one C=C double bond
Name prefixes for 1–5 carbons. :: Meth- (1), eth- (2), prop- (3), but- (4), pent- (5)
What is an addition reaction? :: Two molecules join to form one product (e.g. alkene + bromine)
What is a substitution reaction? :: An atom is replaced by another atom (e.g. alkane + halogen in UV light)
What is a general formula? :: A formula that applies to every member of a homologous series, e.g. CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ for alkanes
What is a structural formula? :: Shows how the atoms are arranged without drawing all the bonds, e.g. CH₃CH₂CH₃
What is a displayed formula? :: Shows every atom and every bond in the molecule
What is the empirical formula of ethene, C₂H₄? :: CH₂
What is the empirical formula of butane, C₄H₁₀? :: C₂H₅
Name the suffixes for alkanes, alkenes, alcohols and carboxylic acids. :: -ane, -ene, -ol, -oic acid
What do the numbers in names like but-2-ene and propan-1-ol show? :: The carbon atom where the functional group (C=C or –OH) is, counting from the end that gives the lowest number
What is the prefix for a chain of 6 carbon atoms? :: Hex-
Name the two structural isomers of C₄H₁₀. :: Butane and methylpropane
Name the three structural isomers of C₅H₁₂. :: Pentane, methylbutane and dimethylpropane

Classify: methane + chlorine → chloromethane + hydrogen chloride (UV). :: Substitution
Classify: methane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water. :: Combustion

# 4b Crude oil
What is crude oil? :: A mixture of hydrocarbons (mostly alkanes) formed from ancient marine organisms
Name the main fractions of crude oil from top to bottom. :: Refinery gases, gasoline (petrol), kerosene, diesel oil, fuel oil, bitumen
Give uses of the fractions. :: Refinery gases – bottled gas/heating ;; Gasoline – petrol for cars ;; Kerosene – aircraft fuel ;; Diesel – lorries/trains ;; Fuel oil – ships, power stations ;; Bitumen – roads, roofing
How do properties change with chain length? :: Longer chains: higher boiling point, more viscous, darker colour, harder to ignite
What is produced in complete and incomplete combustion of hydrocarbons? :: Complete: CO₂ + H₂O ;; Incomplete: CO and/or C (soot) + H₂O
Why is carbon monoxide poisonous? :: It binds to haemoglobin, reducing the blood's ability to carry oxygen
How are nitrogen oxides formed in engines? :: High temperatures cause nitrogen and oxygen from the air to react
How is sulfur dioxide pollution formed and what does it cause? :: Sulfur impurities in fuels burn to SO₂; it dissolves in rain water to form acid rain
What is cracking? :: Breaking long-chain alkanes into shorter, more useful alkanes and alkenes using a catalyst (silica/alumina) at 600–700 °C
What is a fuel? :: A substance that releases heat energy when burned
What is a fraction? :: A mixture of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points (similar chain lengths)
Explain how fractional distillation separates crude oil. Why do long-chain hydrocarbons have higher boiling points? :: They have stronger intermolecular forces, which need more energy to overcome
Write the equation for the complete combustion of propane. :: C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

When does incomplete combustion happen? :: When there is not enough oxygen (limited air supply)
Why is carbon monoxide especially dangerous? :: It is colourless and odourless, so it cannot be detected without an alarm
Give problems caused by soot (carbon) from incomplete combustion. :: Blackens buildings; causes breathing problems; less energy released from the fuel
Write the equation for nitrogen and oxygen reacting in a car engine. :: N₂ + O₂ → 2NO (then 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂)
How do sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen cause acid rain? :: They dissolve in water in clouds/rain to form acidic solutions (sulfuric and nitric acids)
Give three effects of acid rain. :: Kills fish and aquatic life (acidifies lakes) ;; damages trees ;; erodes limestone buildings and corrodes metal structures
How can sulfur dioxide emissions be reduced? :: Remove sulfur from fuels before burning them (or remove SO₂ from waste gases)
Write an equation for cracking decane, C₁₀H₂₂, to octane and ethene. :: C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄
Worked: complete the cracking equation C₁₂H₂₆ → C₈H₁₈ + ? :: C₄H₈ (balance the C and H atoms)
Describe how to crack liquid paraffin in the lab. :: Heat paraffin soaked into mineral wool so vapour passes over hot broken porcelain (or aluminium oxide); collect the gas over water; test it with bromine water (decolourised – alkene)
In lab cracking, why is the delivery tube removed from the water before heating stops? :: To prevent suck-back of cold water into the hot tube, which could crack it
Explain why cracking is necessary. :: Crude oil contains more long-chain fractions than are needed and too little of the short-chain fractions (e.g. petrol) in high demand ;; cracking converts surplus long chains into useful short chains and alkenes

# 4c Alkanes
What is the general formula of alkanes? :: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂
How do alkanes react with bromine? :: Substitution in UV light: e.g. CH₄ + Br₂ → CH₃Br + HBr
Explain why alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons. :: They contain only carbon and hydrogen, and only single C–C bonds (no C=C double bonds)
methane <> CH₄

Give the structural formula of butane. :: CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃
Write the equation for methane reacting with chlorine in UV light. :: CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl
Write the equation for ethane reacting with bromine in UV light. :: C₂H₆ + Br₂ → C₂H₅Br + HBr (bromoethane)
What condition is needed for an alkane to react with a halogen? :: Ultraviolet (UV) light
What is meant by monosubstitution? :: Only one hydrogen atom in the alkane is replaced by a halogen atom
What happens when bromine water is added to an alkane (in the dark)? :: No reaction – it stays orange
Why are alkanes fairly unreactive? :: They contain only strong single C–C and C–H bonds (apart from combustion they react only in UV with halogens)

How do you test for an alkene? :: Add bromine water – orange to colourless

Write the equation for propene reacting with bromine. :: C₃H₆ + Br₂ → C₃H₆Br₂ (1,2-dibromopropane)
How can bromine water distinguish between an alkane and an alkene? :: Shake each with bromine water ;; alkene: orange → colourless ;; alkane: stays orange
Why does an alkene decolourise bromine water? :: Bromine adds across the C=C double bond, forming a colourless dibromoalkane
What is the name of the product of but-1-ene reacting with bromine? :: 1,2-dibromobutane

# 4e Alcohols
What is the functional group of alcohols? :: –OH
How is ethanol made by fermentation? :: Glucose (sugar) with yeast at about 30 °C, without oxygen: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂
How is ethanol made from ethene? :: Hydration with steam: C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH ;; 300 °C, 60–70 atm, phosphoric acid catalyst
Compare fermentation and hydration of ethene. :: Fermentation: renewable, low energy, slow, batch, impure product ;; Hydration: non-renewable (crude oil), fast, continuous, pure product, high energy
How is ethanol oxidised? :: Combustion to CO₂ + H₂O; or oxidised to ethanoic acid by potassium dichromate (orange → green) or by microbes (wine turns to vinegar)
How can ethanol be dehydrated? :: Pass vapour over hot aluminium oxide to make ethene and water
What is the general formula of alcohols? :: CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH
methanol <> CH₃OH
ethanol <> C₂H₅OH (CH₃CH₂OH)
propan-1-ol <> CH₃CH₂CH₂OH
butan-1-ol <> CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH
Write the equation for the complete combustion of ethanol. :: C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O
Write the equation for the microbial oxidation of ethanol. :: C₂H₅OH + O₂ → CH₃COOH + H₂O
What reagent and conditions oxidise ethanol to ethanoic acid in the lab? :: Potassium dichromate(VI) in dilute sulfuric acid, heated ;; colour change orange → green
Why is fermentation carried out in the absence of air? :: To stop the ethanol being oxidised to ethanoic acid (and so yeast respires anaerobically)
Why is fermentation carried out at about 30 °C? :: It is the optimum temperature for the yeast enzymes – lower is too slow; higher denatures the enzymes
Why does fermentation stop at about 15% ethanol? :: The ethanol concentration becomes high enough to kill the yeast
How is ethanol separated from the fermentation mixture? :: Fractional distillation
What type of reaction is the hydration of ethene? :: Addition
Why might a country with lots of sugar cane but no oil choose fermentation? :: Sugar is a cheap, renewable raw material available locally; crude oil would have to be imported

Why does ethanoic acid fizz less vigorously with magnesium than hydrochloric acid of the same concentration? :: It is a weak acid – only partly ionised, so fewer H⁺ ions

# 4g Esters
What is the functional group of esters? :: –COO–
How are esters made? :: Alcohol + carboxylic acid with a conc. sulfuric acid catalyst, heated: e.g. ethanol + ethanoic acid → ethyl ethanoate + water
What are esters used for? :: Food flavourings and perfumes (sweet fruity smells)
Give the structural formula of ethyl ethanoate. :: CH₃COOCH₂CH₃
Write the equation for making ethyl ethanoate. :: CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O
How are esters named? :: First part from the alcohol (-yl), second part from the acid (-oate), e.g. ethanol + ethanoic acid → ethyl ethanoate
Name the ester made from methanol and propanoic acid. :: Methyl propanoate (CH₃CH₂COOCH₃)
Name the ester made from propan-1-ol and ethanoic acid. :: Propyl ethanoate (CH₃COOCH₂CH₂CH₃)
Name the ester made from ethanol and methanoic acid. :: Ethyl methanoate (HCOOCH₂CH₃)
Which alcohol and acid make methyl ethanoate? :: Methanol and ethanoic acid
Which alcohol and acid make ethyl propanoate? :: Ethanol and propanoic acid
C: Esters are {{c1::volatile}} compounds with distinctive (fruity) smells.
Describe how to prepare a sample of ethyl ethanoate. :: Mix ethanol and ethanoic acid in a test tube with a few drops of conc. sulfuric acid; warm in a hot water bath for a few minutes; pour into sodium carbonate solution; carefully waft to smell the fruity ester
In the ester preparation, why is a water bath used instead of a Bunsen flame? :: Ethanol (and the ester) are flammable
In the ester preparation, why is the mixture poured into sodium carbonate solution? :: To neutralise the remaining acids so the smell of the ester can be detected (the ester forms a layer on top)
What is the role of concentrated sulfuric acid in making an ester? :: Catalyst

# 4h Synthetic polymers
What is addition polymerisation? :: Many small unsaturated monomers (alkenes) join together to form a long chain polymer; the C=C opens
Name the polymers made from ethene, propene and chloroethene. :: Poly(ethene), poly(propene), poly(chloroethene) (PVC)
What is condensation polymerisation? :: Monomers with two functional groups join, releasing a small molecule (often water) for each bond made
How are polyesters formed? :: A dicarboxylic acid and a diol undergo condensation polymerisation, eliminating water
Why are some polyesters biodegradable? :: The ester links can be broken down (hydrolysed) by microorganisms
C: Small molecules that join together to make a polymer are called {{c1::monomers}}.
How do you draw the repeat unit of an addition polymer? :: Draw the two carbons from the C=C joined by a single bond, with their side groups; add bonds extending out through brackets on each side; write n after the bracket
Give the repeat unit of poly(ethene). :: –[CH₂–CH₂]–ₙ
Give the repeat unit of poly(propene). :: –[CH₂–CH(CH₃)]–ₙ
Give the repeat unit of poly(chloroethene). :: –[CH₂–CHCl]–ₙ
Give the monomer and repeat unit of poly(tetrafluoroethene) (PTFE). :: Monomer CF₂=CF₂ ;; repeat unit –[CF₂–CF₂]–ₙ
How do you deduce the monomer from the repeat unit of an addition polymer? :: Take the two carbons of the repeat unit, change the C–C to C=C, and remove the extension bonds
Give uses of poly(ethene), poly(propene), PVC and PTFE. :: Poly(ethene) – plastic bags, bottles ;; poly(propene) – crates, ropes ;; PVC – water pipes, window frames, cable insulation ;; PTFE – non-stick pan coatings
Explain why addition polymers are not biodegradable. :: They are inert – strong C–C and C–H bonds make them unreactive, and microorganisms cannot break them down
Give the problems of burning addition polymers. :: Produces toxic gases, e.g. carbon monoxide (incomplete combustion) and hydrogen chloride from PVC; also releases CO₂
Give the formulae of ethanedioic acid and ethanediol. :: HOOC–COOH ;; HO–CH₂–CH₂–OH
Give the repeat unit of the polyester made from ethanedioic acid and ethanediol. :: –[OC–COO–CH₂–CH₂–O]–ₙ (water is eliminated)
How many water molecules form when n molecules of a dicarboxylic acid react with n molecules of a diol? :: 2n
What is the linkage in a polyester? :: Ester link –COO–
Compare addition and condensation polymerisation. :: Addition – monomers with C=C; polymer is the only product ;; Condensation – monomers with two functional groups each; a small molecule (water) is also formed
What are biopolyesters? :: Polyesters that are biodegradable (broken down by microorganisms)

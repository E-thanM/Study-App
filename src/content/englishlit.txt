# Macbeth – key quotes
"Fair is foul, and foul is fair" – who says it, when, and why is it important? :: **Who/where:** The Witches, Act 1 Scene 1 ;; **Analysis:** Paradox/chiasmus – appearances deceive and moral order is inverted; trochaic rhythm makes it chant-like and unnatural, unlike the iambic pentameter of humans; Macbeth echoes it in his first line ("So foul and fair a day"), linking him to the witches ;; **Context:** Jacobean fear of witchcraft – James I wrote Daemonologie (1597) and believed witches tried to kill him
"unseamed him from the nave to th' chops" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** The Captain, Act 1 Scene 2 ;; **Analysis:** Macbeth is introduced as a heroic but extremely violent warrior; "unseamed" is brutal imagery; his capacity for bloodshed is admired when used for the king, foreshadowing violence turned against the king ;; **Context:** Loyalty to the king was a sacred duty; Jacobean ideas of masculinity linked to bravery in battle
"Let not light see my black and deep desires" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Macbeth (aside), Act 1 Scene 4 ;; **Analysis:** Light/dark imagery – he wants darkness to hide his ambition, showing guilt before any crime; rhyming couplet emphasises his resolve; "black and deep" suggests evil and hidden depths ;; **Context:** Malcolm has just been named Prince of Cumberland (heir), blocking Macbeth; regicide was the worst crime against the Divine Right of Kings
"too full o' th' milk of human kindness" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Lady Macbeth (soliloquy), Act 1 Scene 5 ;; **Analysis:** "Milk" connotes nurturing, femininity and innocence – she sees compassion as weakness; she dominates her husband and reverses gender roles ;; **Context:** Women were expected to be submissive and maternal; Lady Macbeth subverts the Jacobean ideal
"That tend on mortal thoughts, unsex me here" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Lady Macbeth, Act 1 Scene 5 ;; **Analysis:** Imperative verbs – she summons evil spirits like a witch; "unsex me" rejects femininity to gain the cruelty she associates with men; later asks for her "milk" to be turned to "gall" ;; **Context:** Calling on spirits would horrify a Jacobean audience fearful of witchcraft; challenges the patriarchal Great Chain of Being
"innocent flower, / But be the serpent under 't" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Lady Macbeth, Act 1 Scene 5 ;; **Analysis:** Appearance vs reality; the serpent alludes to Satan in the Garden of Eden, presenting her as a temptress and Macbeth as Adam ;; **Context:** Gunpowder Plot (1605) medal showed a snake among flowers – link to treason against James I
"Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Macbeth (soliloquy), Act 1 Scene 7 ;; **Analysis:** Horse-riding metaphor – ambition is his only motive and will make him fall; shows he knows killing Duncan is wrong (Duncan's "virtues / Will plead like angels") ;; **Context:** Ambition beyond one's place in the Great Chain of Being was seen as sinful and dangerous
"When you durst do it … you were a man" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Lady Macbeth, Act 1 Scene 7 ;; **Analysis:** She manipulates Macbeth by questioning his masculinity; links manhood with violence; she also uses the shocking image of dashing her baby's brains out ;; **Context:** Jacobean masculinity was tied to courage and honour – she exploits this
"Is this a dagger which I see before me" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Macbeth (soliloquy), Act 2 Scene 1 ;; **Analysis:** Hallucination shows his disturbed mind and guilt; rhetorical question shows uncertainty; the dagger leads him towards murder – is it supernatural or his own ambition? ;; **Context:** Belief in the supernatural and visions as omens
"Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Macbeth, Act 2 Scene 2 ;; **Analysis:** Hyperbole – his guilt is so great an ocean couldn't clean it; blood imagery for guilt runs through the play; he says it would rather turn the seas red ("The multitudinous seas incarnadine") ;; **Context:** Regicide was a sin against God, so his guilt is also religious; contrast with Lady Macbeth's later hand-washing
"A little water clears us of this deed" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Lady Macbeth, Act 2 Scene 2 ;; **Analysis:** Dismissive, practical tone contrasts with Macbeth's guilt; dramatic irony – later she sleepwalks trying to wash invisible blood away ;; **Context:** Audience would expect divine punishment for the murder of an anointed king
"O, full of scorpions is my mind, dear wife!" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Macbeth, Act 3 Scene 2 ;; **Analysis:** Metaphor – scorpions suggest poisonous, tormenting thoughts (paranoia about Banquo and Fleance); "dear wife" shows affection, but he no longer shares plans with her – their relationship is shifting ;; **Context:** Tyrants were thought to suffer mental torment – a punishment for disrupting natural order
"I am in blood / Stepped in so far" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Macbeth, Act 3 Scene 4 (after the banquet and Banquo's ghost) ;; **Analysis:** Extended metaphor of a river of blood – he feels it's as easy to keep killing as to stop; shows his moral decline and loss of conscience ;; **Context:** Shows the tyrant's descent; links to James I's belief that tyranny and usurpation lead to chaos
"Double, double toil and trouble" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** The Witches, Act 4 Scene 1 ;; **Analysis:** Trochaic tetrameter and rhyming couplets make a spell-like chant; repetition and plosives create menace; the apparitions they show give Macbeth false confidence ;; **Context:** James I's obsession with witchcraft; the 1604 Witchcraft Act made it a capital crime
"The castle of Macduff I will surprise" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Macbeth, Act 4 Scene 1 ;; **Analysis:** Murdering innocent women and children shows he has become a complete tyrant – violence now has no purpose beyond fear; contrasts with the heroic warrior of Act 1 ;; **Context:** Audience would see this as the ultimate sign of tyranny, justifying his overthrow
"But I must also feel it as a man" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Macduff, Act 4 Scene 3 (on hearing his family has been murdered) ;; **Analysis:** Redefines masculinity – a real man can show emotion and grief; contrasts with Lady Macbeth's idea that manhood = violence ;; **Context:** Shakespeare challenges narrow Jacobean ideas of masculinity
"Out, damned spot! Out, I say!" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Lady Macbeth (sleepwalking), Act 5 Scene 1 ;; **Analysis:** She speaks in prose, showing her mental collapse; obsessive hand-washing shows guilt she once dismissed; "damned" suggests fear of hell ;; **Context:** Sleepwalking was seen as a sign of a troubled soul; reversal of power – she is now the weaker one
"Here's the smell of the blood still" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Lady Macbeth, Act 5 Scene 1 ;; **Analysis:** Hyperbole echoes Macbeth's "Neptune's ocean"; "little hand" reveals her vulnerability and femininity; guilt can't be removed ;; **Context:** Arabia was associated with luxury and exotic perfumes; guilt as divine punishment
"full of sound and fury, / Signifying nothing" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Macbeth, Act 5 Scene 5 (after Lady Macbeth's death) ;; **Analysis:** Nihilistic metaphor – life is meaningless and theatrical; his ambition has left him empty; numbness rather than grief ("She should have died hereafter") ;; **Context:** Theatre metaphor (Shakespeare's Globe); the tragic hero's downfall leads to despair
"this dead butcher and his fiend-like queen" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Malcolm, final scene (Act 5) ;; **Analysis:** Final judgement – "butcher" reduces Macbeth to a brutal killer, "fiend-like" links Lady Macbeth to the devil; order is restored under the rightful king ;; **Context:** Restoration of the Divine Right of Kings; James I descended from Banquo, so the play flatters him

# Macbeth – themes and context
What is the Divine Right of Kings and why does it matter in Macbeth? :: The belief that kings are chosen by God; killing a king (regicide) is a sin against God and disrupts the natural order – nature reacts (storms, horses eating each other, darkness in the day)
What is the Great Chain of Being? :: A God-given hierarchy: God → angels → king → nobles → commoners → animals; breaking it causes chaos – Macbeth does this through regicide
Why was Macbeth written to please James I? :: James was descended from Banquo (shown positively); interest in witchcraft (Daemonologie); written after the Gunpowder Plot (1605) – shows treason is punished
What are the main themes of Macbeth? :: Ambition, guilt, the supernatural, kingship and tyranny, appearance vs reality, masculinity and gender, violence, order and disorder
How does Macbeth fit the conventions of a tragedy? :: A noble hero with a fatal flaw (hamartia – ambition) falls from greatness, causing his own death; the audience feels pity and fear (catharsis)
How does Lady Macbeth's character change? :: Starts dominant and ruthless (summons spirits, manipulates Macbeth) → excluded from plans (Act 3) → guilt-ridden and mad (sleepwalking, prose) → suicide ;; reverse of Macbeth's arc
How is Banquo a foil to Macbeth? :: Both hear prophecies but Banquo resists temptation ("instruments of darkness tell us truths… to betray's"), remains loyal and noble; his descendants become kings
When was Macbeth written and first performed? :: c.1606, early in the reign of James I (James VI of Scotland, king of England from 1603); Shakespeare's company became the King's Men under James's patronage
What was Shakespeare's main source for Macbeth, and what did he change? :: Holinshed's Chronicles (1587) ;; the real Macbeth ruled Scotland fairly successfully for 17 years (1040–1057) ;; Shakespeare made Duncan old and saintly, Macbeth a tyrant, and Banquo innocent (in Holinshed he helps kill Duncan) – to flatter James I
What was the Gunpowder Plot and how is it reflected in Macbeth? :: A 1605 Catholic plot to blow up James I and Parliament ;; the play shows treason and regicide punished; Lady Macbeth's 'serpent' echoes the plot medal; the Porter's 'equivocator' refers to Father Garnet, who defended lying under oath at the plotters' trial
What is equivocation and why does it matter in Macbeth? :: Using ambiguous language to deceive while technically telling the truth ;; the witches equivocate ('none of woman born', Birnam Wood) – their prophecies come true in unexpected ways, and Macbeth realises too late 'the equivocation of the fiend'
What is 'the king's evil' scene and why is it in Macbeth? :: In 4.3 the saintly English king Edward the Confessor is described as curing the sick ('the king's evil', scrofula) by touch ;; it contrasts a holy king with the tyrant Macbeth, and flatters James I, who also practised touching for the evil
How does nature react to the murder of Duncan? :: In 2.4 darkness covers the day, an owl kills a falcon, and Duncan's horses turn wild and eat each other ;; nature mirrors the disruption of the Great Chain of Being caused by regicide (pathetic fallacy)
How does Macbeth present the theme of ambition? :: Ambition is Macbeth's hamartia ('vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself') ;; Lady Macbeth's ambition is for her husband; Banquo has ambition but controls it ;; unchecked ambition brings guilt, tyranny, isolation and death – it is self-destroying
How does Macbeth present the theme of guilt? :: Through hallucinations (dagger, Banquo's ghost), sleeplessness ('Macbeth does murder sleep'), blood imagery and Lady Macbeth's sleepwalking ;; Macbeth's guilt comes first but fades into numbness; Lady Macbeth's is suppressed then overwhelms her
How does Macbeth present the supernatural? :: The witches' prophecies, apparitions, visions (dagger, ghost) and unnatural events ;; it is ambiguous whether the supernatural controls Macbeth or reveals his own desires – fate vs free will ;; for a Jacobean audience witchcraft was real and terrifying
How does Macbeth present kingship and tyranny? :: Contrasts good kings (Duncan, Edward the Confessor, Malcolm with his 'king-becoming graces') with the tyrant Macbeth, who rules by fear and murder ;; Scotland under Macbeth is a sick, bleeding country; order returns only with the rightful king
How does Macbeth present appearance vs reality? :: Characters hide evil behind innocent faces ('False face must hide what the false heart doth know'; 'There's daggers in men's smiles') ;; prophecies mislead; Duncan cannot read 'the mind's construction in the face'
How does Macbeth present masculinity and gender? :: Lady Macbeth links manhood to violence ('When you durst do it, then you were a man'); the murderers are goaded the same way ;; Macduff offers a different model – a man can 'feel it as a man' ;; the witches have beards, blurring gender
What is the significance of blood imagery in Macbeth? :: Blood first means honour in battle ('bloody execution'), then guilt (Neptune's ocean, 'damned spot') and the cycle of violence ('blood will have blood') ;; it tracks Macbeth's change from hero to butcher
What is the significance of sleep imagery in Macbeth? :: Sleep represents innocence, peace and a clear conscience ;; Macbeth 'does murder sleep' – he kills Duncan as he sleeps and can no longer rest ;; Lady Macbeth sleepwalks – guilt invades her sleep
What is the significance of darkness and light imagery in Macbeth? :: Darkness hides evil ('Stars, hide your fires'; 'Come, thick night') and goodness is associated with light ;; after Duncan's murder the day stays dark; Lady Macbeth later 'has light by her continually' – she now fears the dark
What is the significance of clothing imagery in Macbeth? :: Macbeth wears 'borrowed robes' (Cawdor's title); at the end his title hangs 'like a giant's robe / Upon a dwarfish thief' ;; clothing shows he is not fit for the role he has stolen
What dramatic techniques does Shakespeare use in Macbeth? :: **Soliloquies** reveal inner thoughts ;; **asides** show hidden plans ;; **dramatic irony** (Duncan praising the castle) ;; **offstage violence** (Duncan's murder) creates horror through imagination ;; **comic relief** (the Porter) ;; **pathetic fallacy** (storms, darkness) ;; **motifs** of blood, sleep, darkness, clothing
How does Shakespeare use verse and prose in Macbeth? :: Noble characters speak in **blank verse** (unrhymed iambic pentameter) ;; low-status characters and madness use **prose** (the Porter, sleepwalking Lady Macbeth – her mind has broken down) ;; **rhyming couplets** end scenes or signal resolve ;; the witches use **trochaic tetrameter** – unnatural, chant-like
What is a soliloquy and why are Macbeth's soliloquies important? :: A speech alone on stage revealing a character's true thoughts to the audience ;; Macbeth's soliloquies (1.7, 2.1, 5.5) chart his decline from conscience-stricken doubt to murderous resolve to empty despair – the audience shares his mind, so feels pity as well as horror
What is dramatic irony? Give two examples from Macbeth. :: When the audience knows something the characters don't ;; Duncan says 'There's no art / To find the mind's construction in the face' just as Macbeth enters ;; Duncan praises the 'pleasant seat' of the castle where he will be murdered ;; Lady Macbeth's 'A little water clears us' before her sleepwalking
How does Shakespeare use fate and free will in Macbeth? :: The witches predict Macbeth will be king but don't say how; Macbeth chooses murder ;; Banquo hears prophecies too and does nothing ;; the ambiguity lets the audience debate whether Macbeth is a puppet of fate or responsible for his crimes
How is Macbeth a tragedy in Aristotle's terms? :: **Hamartia** – fatal flaw (ambition) ;; **peripeteia** – reversal of fortune ;; **anagnorisis** – recognition (he realises the witches' equivocation) ;; **catharsis** – the audience's release of pity and fear ;; the hero is high-status and admirable at first, and his fall is caused by his own choices

# An Inspector Calls – key quotes
"substantial and heavily comfortable, but not cosy and homelike" / lighting "pink and intimate" until the Inspector arrives, then "brighter and harder" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Stage directions, Act 1 ;; **Analysis:** The house reflects the Birlings – wealthy but lacking warmth; pink lighting suggests rose-tinted complacency; the harsher light with the Inspector exposes their secrets ;; **Context:** Set in 1912 (Edwardian, before WW1) but written in 1945 – audience knows what comes next
"a hard-headed, practical man of business" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Mr Birling describing himself, Act 1 ;; **Analysis:** Shows his capitalist values – profit first; self-satisfied and arrogant; repeated "hard-headed" makes him seem stubborn and unfeeling ;; **Context:** Priestley was a socialist and attacks selfish capitalism through Birling
"The Germans don't want war" / the Titanic is "unsinkable, absolutely unsinkable" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Mr Birling, Act 1 ;; **Analysis:** Dramatic irony – the 1945 audience knows the Titanic sank (April 1912) and two world wars followed; makes Birling look foolish so his views on responsibility are also undermined ;; **Context:** Written after WW2; Priestley wants the audience to reject Birling's outdated, selfish ideas
"a man has to mind his own business" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Mr Birling, Act 1 (just before the doorbell rings) ;; **Analysis:** Sums up his individualist, capitalist philosophy; the Inspector's arrival interrupts him – a symbolic challenge to this view ;; **Context:** Contrasts with Priestley's socialist message of collective responsibility
"community and all that nonsense" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Mr Birling, Act 1 ;; **Analysis:** Dismissive tone shows contempt for socialism and shared responsibility; mocks writers like Shaw and Wells ;; **Context:** Priestley supported the post-war welfare state – the audience is encouraged to reject Birling's attitude
"an impression of massiveness, solidity and purposefulness" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Stage directions describing Inspector Goole, Act 1 ;; **Analysis:** He is a commanding, powerful presence despite not being physically large; controls the pace ("one line of enquiry at a time"); possibly supernatural ("Goole" = ghoul) ;; **Context:** Acts as Priestley's mouthpiece for socialist ideas
"But these girls aren't cheap labour – they're people." – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Sheila, Act 1 ;; **Analysis:** Sheila begins to show empathy and challenges her father's view of workers as a commodity; the dash emphasises her realisation ;; **Context:** Young people represent hope for change; women's rights and workers' rights were growing issues in 1912
"Why shouldn't they try for higher wages?" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Eric, Act 1 ;; **Analysis:** Eric points out his father's hypocrisy; shows the younger generation questioning capitalism ;; **Context:** Strikes and unions were growing in 1912; Priestley sympathises with workers
"Girls of that class –" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Mrs Birling, Act 2 ;; **Analysis:** Snobbish, prejudiced dismissal of Eva – she sees the working class as morally inferior; the broken sentence shows she sees no need to finish – her contempt is assumed ;; **Context:** Rigid Edwardian class system; Priestley attacks upper-class prejudice
"a kind of wall between us and that girl" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Sheila, Act 2 ;; **Analysis:** Wall metaphor – the barrier the upper classes put between themselves and the poor; Sheila now speaks like the Inspector ;; **Context:** Class divisions; Priestley hoped the war had broken down such barriers
"Public men … have responsibilities as well as privileges" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** The Inspector, Act 2 ;; **Analysis:** Challenges Birling's sense of entitlement – status brings duty to others ;; **Context:** Birling is a former Lord Mayor and magistrate hoping for a knighthood
"didn't feel about her as she felt about me" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Gerald, Act 2 ;; **Analysis:** Gerald admits his affair with Daisy Renton was unequal – he had the power; shows some guilt, but he later tries to escape responsibility ;; **Context:** Upper-class men exploiting working-class women was common
"Go and look for the father of the child" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Mrs Birling, Act 2 ;; **Analysis:** Dramatic irony – she unknowingly condemns her own son, Eric; shows her hypocrisy and lack of compassion ;; **Context:** She chaired the Brumley Women's Charity Organisation yet refused Eva help – Priestley criticises charity driven by prejudice
"the kind of father a chap could go to" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Eric, Act 3 ;; **Analysis:** Reveals the broken relationship between father and son; Birling cares more about reputation than family ;; **Context:** Generational divide – the young are more open to change
"We are members of one body" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** The Inspector's final speech, Act 3 ;; **Analysis:** Central message of collective social responsibility; "one body" has religious connotations (Christian community) ;; **Context:** Priestley's socialism; supports the welfare state and NHS that Labour created after 1945
"taught it in fire and blood and anguish" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** The Inspector, Act 3 ;; **Analysis:** Prophetic warning – the tricolon and imagery refer to the two World Wars; the play itself is a warning to the 1945 audience ;; **Context:** Written in 1945 after WW2 – Priestley urges the audience to build a fairer society
"millions and millions and millions of Eva Smiths" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** The Inspector, Act 3 ;; **Analysis:** Eva represents all exploited working-class people; repetition emphasises the scale; "Smith" is a common name – everyone ;; **Context:** Priestley's concern for the poor and working class
"It frightens me the way you talk" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Sheila, Act 3 ;; **Analysis:** Sheila has changed and accepts responsibility, unlike her parents who return to complacency once they think the Inspector was a hoax ;; **Context:** Hope rests with the young generation (Sheila and Eric)
"a police inspector is on his way here" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Mr Birling, final lines, Act 3 ;; **Analysis:** Cyclical structure – the Birlings must face the inquiry again because they failed to learn; dashes show shock; cliffhanger ending ;; **Context:** Priestley suggests society will be punished until it learns collective responsibility

# An Inspector Calls – themes and context
When was An Inspector Calls set and written, and why does it matter? :: Set in 1912, written 1945 (first performed in Moscow 1945, London 1946) ;; dramatic irony – the audience knows about the Titanic, WW1, WW2; Priestley urges post-war change
What were Priestley's political views? :: Socialist; co-founded the Common Wealth Party; hosted 'Postscripts' radio broadcasts in WW2; supported the welfare state
What are the main themes of An Inspector Calls? :: Social responsibility, class, gender, age/generations, capitalism vs socialism, guilt and blame, hypocrisy
What is the significance of the name 'Goole'? :: Sounds like 'ghoul' – suggests something supernatural; he seems to know events in advance and may be Priestley's mouthpiece or a conscience figure
What dramatic devices does Priestley use? :: Dramatic irony, the well-made play structure, unity of time and place, cliffhangers at the end of each act, stage directions/lighting, the Inspector as mouthpiece, cyclical ending
Summarise each character's role in Eva's death. :: Mr Birling – sacked her for striking ;; Sheila – had her sacked from Milwards out of jealousy ;; Gerald – kept her as mistress then ended it ;; Eric – got her pregnant, stole money ;; Mrs Birling – refused her charity
How does Priestley present social responsibility? :: Through the contrast between Birling's individualism ('look after himself and his own') and the Inspector's collectivism ('We are members of one body') ;; each character's small selfish act forms a chain that destroys Eva ;; the audience is challenged to accept responsibility for others
How does Priestley present class? :: The upper/middle-class Birlings and Gerald exploit and judge working-class Eva ('Girls of that class') ;; Mrs Birling is socially superior to her husband; Birling craves a knighthood ;; Priestley shows class privilege as morally empty and dangerous
How does Priestley present capitalism vs socialism? :: Birling embodies selfish capitalism (profit, low wages, 'community and all that nonsense'); the Inspector voices socialism (shared responsibility, care for workers) ;; dramatic irony discredits Birling, so the audience is pushed towards socialism
How does Priestley present hypocrisy? :: The Birlings appear respectable but are guilty: Mrs Birling runs a charity yet refuses help; Birling is a magistrate yet exploits workers; Gerald seems honourable yet keeps a mistress ;; their concern for reputation over morality is exposed
What is the Inspector's dramatic function? :: Drives the plot and controls the pace ('one line of enquiry at a time'); reveals secrets; acts as Priestley's mouthpiece; judges each character; links the 1912 story to the 1945 audience through his final speech
What does the ending of An Inspector Calls suggest? :: The final phone call means the Birlings must face the truth again – those who did not learn will be tested again ;; it suggests the Inspector was supernatural or a warning, and that society will keep suffering ('fire and blood and anguish') until it learns

# Of Mice and Men – key quotes
"the Salinas River drops in close to the hillside" – why is the setting significant? :: **Who/where:** Narrator, opening of Chapter 1 ;; **Analysis:** 'Soledad' means solitude/loneliness in Spanish – foreshadows the theme; the idyllic natural setting of the clearing is where the novella also ends (cyclical structure) ;; **Context:** Set in California during the Great Depression of the 1930s
"the way a bear drags his paws" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Narrator describing Lennie, Chapter 1 ;; **Analysis:** Animal imagery presents Lennie as powerful but innocent and instinctive – like a creature that doesn't know its own strength; foreshadows harm ;; **Context:** People with learning disabilities were poorly understood and often institutionalised in the 1930s
"Guys like us … loneliest guys in the world" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** George, Chapter 1 ;; **Analysis:** Introduces loneliness as the central theme; George and Lennie's friendship is unusual ("With us it ain't like that") ;; **Context:** Itinerant migrant workers moved from ranch to ranch with no family or home during the Depression
"I got you to look after me" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Lennie (and George), Chapter 1 ;; **Analysis:** Shows their mutual dependence and companionship – the dream and the friendship protect them from loneliness; repeated like a ritual ;; **Context:** Rare loyalty in a harsh, competitive world
"live off the fatta the lan'" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Lennie, Chapter 1 (and repeated) ;; **Analysis:** The dream of owning a farm represents freedom, security and independence; colloquial dialect shows his simplicity ;; **Context:** The American Dream – Steinbeck shows it is impossible for the poor in the Depression
"I could get along so easy and so nice" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** George, Chapter 1 ;; **Analysis:** Shows George's frustration and the burden of caring for Lennie – but he stays, showing loyalty; foreshadows the ending ;; **Context:** Hard economic times made companionship a luxury
"his word was taken on any subject" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Narrator describing Slim, Chapter 2 ;; **Analysis:** Slim is the 'prince of the ranch' – wise, respected, calm; an almost god-like figure; he understands George and Lennie ;; **Context:** Contrasts with Curley, whose power comes only from his father's ownership
"full, rouged lips and wide-spaced eyes, heavily made up" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Narrator describing Curley's wife, Chapter 2 ;; **Analysis:** Red symbolises danger and sexuality – the men see her as a temptress ("jail bait"); she is never named, showing she is seen as Curley's possession ;; **Context:** Women in the 1930s had few rights; a ranch was a male world
"I ought to of shot that dog myself, George" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Candy, Chapter 3 ;; **Analysis:** Foreshadows George shooting Lennie – George takes responsibility for his friend's death himself; shows how the weak are discarded ;; **Context:** Old and disabled workers were seen as useless in the Depression – no welfare state
"they can me here I wisht somebody'd shoot me" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Candy, Chapter 3 ;; **Analysis:** Candy fears he will end up like his dog – useless and discarded; the dream gives him hope ;; **Context:** No pensions or social security for ageing workers in 1930s America
"I can't play because I'm black" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Crooks, Chapter 4 ;; **Analysis:** Crooks is isolated by racism – forced to live alone in the harness room; his bitterness comes from loneliness ;; **Context:** Racial segregation and Jim Crow laws in 1930s America
"a guy gets too lonely an' he gets sick" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Crooks, Chapter 4 ;; **Analysis:** Reveals the psychological damage of loneliness; the dash shows emotion breaking through his defensive manner ;; **Context:** Segregation left black workers profoundly isolated
"nobody gets no land. It's just in their head" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Crooks, Chapter 4 ;; **Analysis:** Crooks crushes the dream – double negatives emphasise his cynicism; he has seen hundreds of men with the same dream fail ;; **Context:** The American Dream was unattainable for most during the Depression
"I could get you strung up on a tree" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Curley's wife to Crooks, Chapter 4 ;; **Analysis:** She uses the only power she has – her race – to threaten Crooks with lynching; shows a hierarchy of the powerless ;; **Context:** Lynching of black Americans was common; a white woman's word would be believed over a black man's
"I can't talk to nobody but Curley" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Curley's wife, Chapter 5 ;; **Analysis:** Reveals her loneliness and vulnerability – she is a victim, not just a temptress; she also dreamed of being "in the movies" ;; **Context:** Women's limited opportunities; Hollywood glamour as the dream for 1930s women
"the ache for attention were all gone" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Narrator, Chapter 5 (after Lennie kills her) ;; **Analysis:** Polysyndeton lists her troubles; in death she becomes innocent and peaceful – sympathetic portrayal; only free when dead ;; **Context:** Women trapped by marriage and male power
"I done another bad thing." – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Lennie, Chapter 5 ;; **Analysis:** Childlike language shows he doesn't fully understand the seriousness; echoes the mouse and puppy – pattern of accidental killing ;; **Context:** Lack of understanding of disability – no protection for vulnerable people
"Le's do it now. Le's get that place now." – analyse the ending. :: **Who/where:** Lennie, Chapter 6, moments before George shoots him ;; **Analysis:** George tells him to look across the river and retells the dream as he shoots Lennie – a mercy killing to save him from Curley's lynch mob; the dream dies with Lennie; cyclical structure returns to the clearing ;; **Context:** The impossibility of the American Dream; George's sacrifice parallels Candy's dog
"You hadda, George. I swear you hadda." – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Slim, Chapter 6 ;; **Analysis:** Slim understands George acted out of love; repetition offers comfort ;; **Context:** Shows true friendship in a harsh world
"ya suppose is eatin' them two guys" – analyse. :: **Who/where:** Carlson, final line, Chapter 6 ;; **Analysis:** Carlson's insensitivity shows most men can't understand friendship or grief – loneliness and lack of empathy prevail ;; **Context:** Itinerant workers' tough, isolated lives

# Of Mice and Men – themes and context
Where does the title Of Mice and Men come from? :: Robert Burns' poem 'To a Mouse': "The best-laid schemes o' mice an' men / Gang aft agley" – plans often go wrong; foreshadows the failure of the dream
What was the Great Depression? :: Economic collapse after the 1929 Wall Street Crash – mass unemployment in the USA; the Dust Bowl drove migrants to California looking for work
What are the main themes of Of Mice and Men? :: Loneliness/isolation, friendship, dreams (American Dream), power and powerlessness, prejudice (race, gender, age, disability), violence, fate
How is Of Mice and Men structured? :: Six chapters like scenes of a play (Steinbeck called it a 'play-novelette'); each opens with setting; cyclical – begins and ends at the pool; foreshadowing (mouse, puppy, Candy's dog)
Which characters represent different kinds of prejudice? :: Crooks – race ;; Curley's wife – gender ;; Candy – age and disability ;; Lennie – learning disability
What was Steinbeck's purpose? :: To show the harsh lives of migrant workers and criticise the unfairness of 1930s society; to build sympathy for the powerless
Who was John Steinbeck? :: American writer (1902–1968), born in Salinas, California; worked on ranches alongside migrant workers as a young man ;; Of Mice and Men was published in 1937; he also wrote The Grapes of Wrath (1939) and won the Nobel Prize in Literature (1962)
What was the American Dream and how does Steinbeck present it? :: The belief that anyone in America can achieve success and freedom through hard work ;; George and Lennie's farm, Candy's hope, Crooks's brief wish and Curley's wife's movie dream all fail – Steinbeck suggests the dream is an illusion for the poor during the Depression
What was life like for migrant workers in 1930s California? :: Itinerant 'bindle stiffs' moved from ranch to ranch for low wages, sleeping in bunkhouses, with no families, security or savings; they often spent wages on drink and brothels; many had fled the Dust Bowl
How were black Americans treated in the 1930s? :: Racism was widespread; in the South Jim Crow laws enforced segregation, and across the USA black workers faced discrimination and the threat of lynching ;; Crooks is isolated in the harness room and powerless against Curley's wife's threat
How were women treated in 1930s America? :: Women had few opportunities, were expected to marry and be dependent on husbands ;; Curley's wife is lonely, unnamed and seen as a 'tramp' – the only woman on a male ranch ;; Hollywood offered a glamorous but mostly unreachable dream
How does Steinbeck present loneliness? :: Almost every character is isolated: Crooks (race), Candy (age, disability, losing his dog), Curley's wife (gender), the ranch workers (itinerant life) ;; George and Lennie's friendship is the exception – and it is destroyed, leaving George alone
How does Steinbeck present friendship? :: George and Lennie's mutual care ('I got you to look after me') is rare and envied ('Ain't many guys travel around together') ;; Slim understands it; Candy's bond with his dog mirrors it ;; the mercy killing is presented as the final act of friendship
How does Steinbeck present power and powerlessness? :: Power comes from status (the boss, Curley), respect (Slim), race (Curley's wife over Crooks) or physical strength (Lennie, unaware of it) ;; the weak turn on the weaker – a hierarchy of the powerless
How does Steinbeck present violence? :: Violence runs through the novella: Curley's fights, the shooting of Candy's dog, Lennie's accidental killings, the lynch mob, George shooting Lennie ;; nature is also violent (the heron and the snake) – a harsh, predatory world
How does Steinbeck present fate in Of Mice and Men? :: The title (from Burns) and heavy foreshadowing suggest the dream is doomed from the start ;; the cyclical structure and repeated patterns (Weed, the mouse, the puppy) make the tragedy feel inevitable
What is the significance of the rabbits? :: Lennie's dream of tending the rabbits symbolises safety, innocence and the dream itself; George repeats it to calm him ;; in Chapter 6 a giant rabbit hallucination tells Lennie George will leave him – the dream turning against him
What is the significance of Candy's dog? :: Old, smelly and useless, the dog is shot by Carlson – just as society discards the old and weak ;; Candy regrets not doing it himself, so George shoots Lennie himself, in the same way (back of the head), as an act of mercy
What other symbols does Steinbeck use? :: **The heron and snake** – the strong preying on the weak ;; **Curley's glove 'fulla vaseline'** – vanity and sexual possessiveness ;; **George's solitaire** – loneliness ;; **the dream farm** – Eden, freedom ;; **the river/clearing** – a safe haven that becomes a place of death
What writing techniques does Steinbeck use? :: **Descriptive openings** to each chapter like stage directions ;; **naturalistic dialogue** and dialect ('fatta the lan'') ;; **animal imagery** for Lennie ;; **foreshadowing** ;; **symbolism** ;; **cyclical structure** ;; **pathetic fallacy and light** (fading light in Chapter 6) ;; a third-person narrator who rarely comments, so readers judge for themselves
Why did Steinbeck write Of Mice and Men like a play? :: He intended it as a 'play-novelette' that could be staged: six scenes, each in one location, mostly dialogue, with opening descriptions like stage directions ;; it was adapted for Broadway in 1937

# Macbeth – points, quotes and analysis
Macbeth – Point: The play opens by warning that appearances cannot be trusted. :: **Quote:** "Fair is foul, and foul is fair" (Witches, Act 1 Scene 1) ;; **Analysis:** Paradox and chiasmus – good and evil swap places. The chant-like trochaic rhythm sounds unnatural, unlike the iambic pentameter of the human characters. Macbeth echoes it ("So foul and fair a day I have not seen"), linking him to the witches before he has met them ;; **Context:** Jacobean fear of witchcraft – James I wrote Daemonologie and believed witches plotted against him
Macbeth – Point: Macbeth is first presented as a brave but brutal warrior. :: **Quote:** "unseamed him from the nave to th' chops" (Captain, Act 1 Scene 2) ;; **Analysis:** "Brave" and "deserves" show the king's side admiring him, but "unseamed" and "bloody execution" are graphically violent. His capacity for bloodshed is praised when used for Duncan, foreshadowing violence turned on the king ;; **Context:** Loyalty to the king was sacred; Jacobean masculinity was tied to courage in battle
Macbeth – Point: Banquo, unlike Macbeth, is suspicious of the witches' prophecies. :: **Quote:** "instruments of darkness tell us truths" (Banquo, Act 1 Scene 3) ;; **Analysis:** Banquo sees that evil forces may tell small truths to lead people into disaster. Metaphor of "instruments of darkness" links the witches to evil. He is a foil – he resists temptation while Macbeth gives in ;; **Context:** King James believed he descended from Banquo, so Banquo is shown as noble
Macbeth – Point: Macbeth's ambition makes him want to hide his thoughts from God. :: **Quote:** "Let not light see my black and deep desires" (Macbeth, aside, Act 1 Scene 4) ;; **Analysis:** Light and dark imagery – he wants darkness to hide his guilt before he has done anything. Imperative "hide" and the rhyming couplet show resolve. "Black and deep" suggests evil that goes right down inside him ;; **Context:** Malcolm has just been named heir; regicide was the worst sin against the Divine Right of Kings
Macbeth – Point: Lady Macbeth sees her husband's kindness as a weakness. :: **Quote:** "too full o' th' milk of human kindness" (Lady Macbeth, Act 1 Scene 5) ;; **Analysis:** The metaphor of "milk" suggests nurturing and femininity – she treats compassion as a flaw. She sets out to dominate him, reversing the traditional gender roles ;; **Context:** Women were expected to be gentle and obedient; Lady Macbeth subverts this ideal
Macbeth – Point: Lady Macbeth rejects femininity so she can commit murder. :: **Quote:** "That tend on mortal thoughts, unsex me here" (Lady Macbeth, Act 1 Scene 5) ;; **Analysis:** Imperative verbs – she commands evil spirits like a witch. "Unsex me" shows she sees cruelty as masculine, and she later asks for her "milk" to be turned to "gall" ;; **Context:** Calling on spirits would horrify a Jacobean audience fearful of witchcraft; it challenges the Great Chain of Being
Macbeth – Point: Deception is central to the Macbeths' plan to kill Duncan. :: **Quote:** "innocent flower, / But be the serpent under 't" (Lady Macbeth, Act 1 Scene 5) ;; **Analysis:** Imperative advice to hide evil behind a welcoming face. The serpent alludes to Satan in the Garden of Eden, making Lady Macbeth a temptress and Macbeth her Adam ;; **Context:** A medal made after the Gunpowder Plot (1605) showed a snake among flowers – treason hidden behind a friendly face
Macbeth – Point: Macbeth knows that ambition is his only reason to kill Duncan. :: **Quote:** "Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself" (Macbeth, Act 1 Scene 7) ;; **Analysis:** Horse-riding metaphor – ambition is a rider who jumps too far and falls. It foreshadows his downfall and shows he knows the murder is wrong (Duncan's virtues "will plead like angels") ;; **Context:** Ambition beyond one's place in the Great Chain of Being was seen as sinful and dangerous
Macbeth – Point: Lady Macbeth manipulates her husband by attacking his masculinity. :: **Quote:** "When you durst do it … you were a man" (Lady Macbeth, Act 1 Scene 7) ;; **Analysis:** She ties manhood to violence and courage, so refusing the murder becomes cowardice. She also uses the shocking image of dashing her own baby's brains out to prove her resolve ;; **Context:** Jacobean masculinity was linked to honour and bravery – she exploits this
Macbeth – Point: Guilt and imagination disturb Macbeth even before the murder. :: **Quote:** "Is this a dagger which I see before me" (Macbeth, Act 2 Scene 1) ;; **Analysis:** The hallucination shows his troubled mind. The rhetorical question shows uncertainty – is it supernatural or the product of his ambition? The dagger leads him towards Duncan's chamber ;; **Context:** Belief in the supernatural and in visions as omens
Macbeth – Point: Macbeth is overwhelmed by guilt immediately after killing Duncan. :: **Quote:** "Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood" (Macbeth, Act 2 Scene 2) ;; **Analysis:** Hyperbole – his guilt is so great that an ocean could not clean it. He imagines his hand turning the seas red. Blood is the play's symbol of guilt ;; **Context:** Regicide was a sin against God, so his guilt is religious as well as personal
Macbeth – Point: At first Lady Macbeth is dismissive of guilt. :: **Quote:** "A little water clears us of this deed" (Lady Macbeth, Act 2 Scene 2) ;; **Analysis:** Her practical, almost casual tone contrasts with Macbeth's horror. The line creates dramatic irony – later she sleepwalks trying to wash away imaginary blood ;; **Context:** A Jacobean audience would expect divine punishment for killing an anointed king
Macbeth – Point: Once he is king, Macbeth becomes paranoid and tormented. :: **Quote:** "O, full of scorpions is my mind, dear wife!" (Macbeth, Act 3 Scene 2) ;; **Analysis:** Metaphor – scorpions suggest poisonous, tormenting thoughts about Banquo and Fleance. "Dear wife" shows affection, but he no longer shares his plans with her – their relationship is breaking down ;; **Context:** Tyrants were believed to suffer mentally for disturbing the natural order
Macbeth – Point: Macbeth feels trapped in a cycle of violence. :: **Quote:** "I am in blood / Stepped in so far" (Macbeth, Act 3 Scene 4) ;; **Analysis:** Extended river-of-blood metaphor – going back is as hard as going forward, so he keeps killing. It shows his conscience fading and his moral decline ;; **Context:** The tyrant's downfall – a warning to Jacobean audiences about the cost of usurping a king
Macbeth – Point: The witches create chaos and tempt Macbeth with false confidence. :: **Quote:** "Double, double toil and trouble" (Witches, Act 4 Scene 1) ;; **Analysis:** Chant-like trochaic tetrameter and rhyming couplets sound like a spell. Repetition and plosive sounds are menacing. The apparitions that follow give Macbeth a false sense of safety ;; **Context:** The 1604 Witchcraft Act made witchcraft a capital crime
Macbeth – Point: By Act 4 Macbeth has become a complete tyrant. :: **Quote:** "The castle of Macduff I will surprise" (Macbeth, Act 4 Scene 1) ;; **Analysis:** Killing innocent women and children serves no purpose except to spread fear. "Unfortunate souls" shows he has no more empathy. It contrasts with the heroic warrior of Act 1 ;; **Context:** Audiences saw this as the mark of a tyrant, justifying his overthrow
Macbeth – Point: Shakespeare challenges narrow ideas of what it means to be a man. :: **Quote:** "But I must also feel it as a man" (Macduff, Act 4 Scene 3) ;; **Analysis:** Macduff says a real man can grieve. This contrasts with Lady Macbeth's idea that manhood means violence, and with Malcolm's call to "dispute it like a man" ;; **Context:** Shakespeare questions the Jacobean ideal of masculinity as toughness
Macbeth – Point: Lady Macbeth is destroyed by guilt in the end. :: **Quote:** "Out, damned spot! Out, I say!" (Lady Macbeth, sleepwalking, Act 5 Scene 1) ;; **Analysis:** She speaks in prose, showing her broken mind. The obsessive hand-washing shows the guilt she once dismissed ("a little water clears us"). "Damned" suggests she fears hell ;; **Context:** Sleepwalking was seen as a sign of a troubled soul; the power balance of the marriage has reversed
Macbeth – Point: Guilt cannot be washed away. :: **Quote:** "Here's the smell of the blood still" (Lady Macbeth, Act 5 Scene 1) ;; **Analysis:** Hyperbole echoes Macbeth's "Neptune's ocean". "Little hand" reveals her vulnerability and femininity. The smell of blood suggests her crime lingers ;; **Context:** Arabia was linked with luxury and exotic perfumes – even the finest cannot cleanse a sin
Macbeth – Point: Macbeth ends in despair, believing life is meaningless. :: **Quote:** "full of sound and fury, / Signifying nothing" (Macbeth, Act 5 Scene 5) ;; **Analysis:** Metaphors of a shadow and a poor actor show life as empty and theatrical. His numbness at his wife's death ("She should have died hereafter") shows how far ambition has hollowed him out ;; **Context:** Theatre metaphor for Shakespeare's Globe; the tragic hero falls into despair
Macbeth – Point: At the end, order is restored and the Macbeths are condemned. :: **Quote:** "this dead butcher and his fiend-like queen" (Malcolm, Act 5 Scene 8 – the final scene) ;; **Analysis:** "Butcher" reduces the former hero to a brutal killer and "fiend-like" links Lady Macbeth to the devil. The rightful king restores order ;; **Context:** The restoration of the Divine Right of Kings would please James I, who traced his line to Banquo
Macbeth – Point: Macbeth's first words link him to the witches. :: **Quote:** "So foul and fair a day" (Macbeth, Act 1 Scene 3) ;; **Analysis:** He unknowingly echoes the witches' paradox 'Fair is foul', suggesting a connection between them before they meet; the day is 'foul' (weather) yet 'fair' (victory) ;; **Context:** Jacobean belief that witches sought out those open to evil
Macbeth – Point: The witches' prophecy plants the idea of kingship. :: **Quote:** "All hail, Macbeth, that shalt be king hereafter!" (Third Witch, Act 1 Scene 3) ;; **Analysis:** The triple greeting builds to the climactic title of king; 'hereafter' is vague, leaving Macbeth to decide how it happens ;; **Context:** Fate vs free will – the witches predict but do not command
Macbeth – Point: Banquo's prophecy is a threat to Macbeth. :: **Quote:** "Thou shalt get kings, though thou be none" (Third Witch, Act 1 Scene 3) ;; **Analysis:** The paradox promises Banquo a dynasty; it later drives Macbeth's jealousy and his order to kill Banquo and Fleance ;; **Context:** James I claimed descent from Banquo, so this flatters the king
Macbeth – Point: At first Macbeth hopes fate will crown him without crime. :: **Quote:** "chance may crown me / Without my stir" (Macbeth, Act 1 Scene 3) ;; **Analysis:** The full line ('If chance will have me king, why, chance…') repeats 'chance', showing him trying to leave it to fate; this hesitation contrasts with his later violent decisions ;; **Context:** Shows Macbeth's initial conscience and loyalty
Macbeth – Point: Duncan's trust shows how appearances deceive. :: **Quote:** "To find the mind's construction in the face" (Duncan, Act 1 Scene 4) ;; **Analysis:** Duncan says 'There's no art' to reading faces – dramatic irony, as he says this about the traitor Cawdor just as Macbeth, the next traitor, enters ;; **Context:** The good king's trusting nature makes his murder more shocking
Macbeth – Point: Lady Macbeth is determined to make the prophecy come true. :: **Quote:** "Glamis thou art, and Cawdor, and shalt be" (Lady Macbeth, Act 1 Scene 5) ;; **Analysis:** The future tense 'shalt be' shows her certainty; she echoes the witches' language, linking her with them ;; **Context:** Women had no direct power; she seeks it through her husband
Macbeth – Point: Lady Macbeth recognises her husband's ambition but doubts his ruthlessness. :: **Quote:** "Art not without ambition, but without / The illness" (Lady Macbeth, Act 1 Scene 5) ;; **Analysis:** She sees ruthlessness as necessary – 'illness' means the wickedness that should accompany ambition; she views morality as weakness ;; **Context:** Ambition beyond one's station was considered sinful
Macbeth – Point: Lady Macbeth calls on darkness to hide the murder. :: **Quote:** "pall thee in the dunnest smoke of hell" (Lady Macbeth, Act 1 Scene 5) ;; **Analysis:** Imperative 'Come, thick night' and darkness imagery; 'pall' suggests a funeral shroud and 'smoke of hell' links the murder to damnation ;; **Context:** Light and darkness symbolise good and evil
Macbeth – Point: Macbeth fears the consequences of killing a good king. :: **Quote:** "pity, like a naked new-born babe" (Macbeth, Act 1 Scene 7) ;; **Analysis:** The simile shows pity as innocent and vulnerable yet powerful enough to spread news of the deed; his imagination reveals his conscience ;; **Context:** Regicide as a crime against God and nature
Macbeth – Point: Macbeth briefly decides not to kill Duncan. :: **Quote:** "We will proceed no further in this business" (Macbeth, Act 1 Scene 7) ;; **Analysis:** The firm declarative and euphemism 'business' show him trying to distance himself from murder; his resolve collapses under his wife's pressure ;; **Context:** Shows Macbeth is not purely evil – he has a conscience
Macbeth – Point: Lady Macbeth will reject even motherhood to prove her resolve. :: **Quote:** "And dashed the brains out" (Lady Macbeth, Act 1 Scene 7) ;; **Analysis:** The shocking, violent image of killing her own baby subverts maternal love completely, shaming Macbeth into agreeing ;; **Context:** Motherhood was central to Jacobean ideas of womanhood
Macbeth – Point: Lady Macbeth demands courage and commitment. :: **Quote:** "screw your courage to the sticking-place" (Lady Macbeth, Act 1 Scene 7) ;; **Analysis:** The metaphor (from tightening a crossbow or a musical instrument's peg) suggests courage must be forced to its limit ;; **Context:** She takes on the masculine role of persuader and planner
Macbeth – Point: Macbeth agrees to hide his guilt behind a false face. :: **Quote:** "False face must hide" (Macbeth, Act 1 Scene 7) ;; **Analysis:** The rhyming couplet ('…what the false heart doth know') ends the act with resolve; repetition of 'false' shows deception is now his plan ;; **Context:** Appearance vs reality – echoes 'look like th' innocent flower'
Macbeth – Point: Banquo resists the temptation that Macbeth gives in to. :: **Quote:** "Restrain in me the cursed thoughts" (Banquo, Act 2 Scene 1) ;; **Analysis:** He prays for protection from dark dreams, showing he also feels tempted but resists – a foil to Macbeth ;; **Context:** Banquo is idealised as James I's ancestor
Macbeth – Point: Killing Duncan destroys Macbeth's peace of mind. :: **Quote:** "Macbeth does murder sleep" (Macbeth, Act 2 Scene 2) ;; **Analysis:** Sleep symbolises innocence and peace; by killing a sleeping man he has destroyed his own rest. Speaking of himself in the third person suggests his identity is split ;; **Context:** Sleeplessness as a sign of a guilty conscience
Macbeth – Point: Lady Macbeth mocks her husband's guilt. :: **Quote:** "My hands are of your colour, but I shame" (Lady Macbeth, Act 2 Scene 2) ;; **Analysis:** She shares the bloody guilt but is ashamed to have a heart 'so white' (cowardly, pale) – yet white also suggests innocence, so the insult is ironic ;; **Context:** Blood as the symbol of guilt
Macbeth – Point: Lady Macbeth is more human than she seems. :: **Quote:** "Had he not resembled / My father as he slept" (Lady Macbeth, Act 2 Scene 2) ;; **Analysis:** She could not kill Duncan herself because he looked like her father – a crack in her ruthless image that foreshadows her collapse ;; **Context:** Kings were seen as father figures to the nation
Macbeth – Point: Macbeth's castle has become a version of hell. :: **Quote:** "If a man were porter of hell-gate" (Porter, Act 2 Scene 3) ;; **Analysis:** The drunken Porter offers comic relief but the joke is darkly ironic: the castle really is hell after the murder ;; **Context:** His 'equivocator' alludes to the Gunpowder Plot trial
Macbeth – Point: Duncan's sons realise they are in danger. :: **Quote:** "There's daggers in men's smiles" (Donalbain, Act 2 Scene 3) ;; **Analysis:** The metaphor captures the theme of appearance vs reality – friendly faces hide murderous intentions ;; **Context:** Courtly politics and treachery
Macbeth – Point: Nature reacts to the unnatural murder of a king. :: **Quote:** "'Tis unnatural, / Even like the deed that's done." (Old Man, Act 2 Scene 4) ;; **Analysis:** The simile links strange events (darkness by day, horses eating each other) to the murder; the universe is disordered ;; **Context:** The Great Chain of Being and Divine Right of Kings
Macbeth – Point: Banquo suspects Macbeth. :: **Quote:** "I fear / Thou play'dst most foully for't" (Banquo, Act 3 Scene 1) ;; **Analysis:** 'Foully' echoes 'Fair is foul'; Banquo's suspicion makes him a threat, but his silence also implicates him ;; **Context:** Shakespeare keeps Banquo innocent to flatter James I
Macbeth – Point: Being king is worthless to Macbeth without security. :: **Quote:** "To be thus is nothing" (Macbeth, Act 3 Scene 1) ;; **Analysis:** With its second half ('But to be safely thus') the balanced line shows his insecurity; he now kills to protect what he has, not to gain it ;; **Context:** Tyrants are always insecure – they rule by fear
Macbeth – Point: Macbeth realises his crown will not pass to his heirs. :: **Quote:** "Upon my head they placed a fruitless crown" (Macbeth, Act 3 Scene 1) ;; **Analysis:** 'Fruitless' suggests barrenness – no children to inherit; his crime has gained him nothing lasting ;; **Context:** Lineage and inheritance were central to kingship
Macbeth – Point: Achieving their ambition has not brought happiness. :: **Quote:** "Nought's had, all's spent" (Lady Macbeth, Act 3 Scene 2) ;; **Analysis:** The balanced antithesis shows emptiness – they have lost everything to gain nothing; her confidence is fading ;; **Context:** Ambition that breaks the natural order brings no satisfaction
Macbeth – Point: Macbeth now acts without his wife. :: **Quote:** "Be innocent of the knowledge, dearest chuck" (Macbeth, Act 3 Scene 2) ;; **Analysis:** The affectionate 'dearest chuck' contrasts with his secret plan to murder Banquo; the power balance has shifted to Macbeth ;; **Context:** Their partnership breaks down as Macbeth becomes a tyrant
Macbeth – Point: Banquo's ghost torments Macbeth publicly. :: **Quote:** "Never shake / Thy gory locks at me." (Macbeth, Act 3 Scene 4) ;; **Analysis:** Imperative and gruesome imagery show his terror; his outburst in front of the guests reveals his guilt ;; **Context:** Ghosts were believed to return to accuse their murderers
Macbeth – Point: Macbeth believes violence will inevitably lead to more violence. :: **Quote:** "blood will have blood" (Macbeth, Act 3 Scene 4) ;; **Analysis:** The chiasmus-like repetition of 'blood' shows a cycle of violence and revenge he cannot escape ;; **Context:** Biblical idea that murder is repaid with murder
Macbeth – Point: Even the witches see Macbeth as evil. :: **Quote:** "Something wicked this way comes." (Second Witch, Act 4 Scene 1) ;; **Analysis:** The rhyming chant ('thumbs/comes') announces Macbeth as 'wicked' – he has become as evil as the witches ;; **Context:** Jacobean fears of witchcraft
Macbeth – Point: The apparitions give Macbeth false confidence. :: **Quote:** "none of woman born / Shall harm Macbeth" (Second Apparition, Act 4 Scene 1) ;; **Analysis:** Equivocation – it seems to promise invincibility but hides the truth about Macduff's birth; Macbeth's overconfidence leads to his downfall ;; **Context:** Equivocation and the Gunpowder Plot trial
Macbeth – Point: Macbeth's murderers kill an innocent child. :: **Quote:** "What, you egg! / Young fry of treachery!" (Murderer, Act 4 Scene 2) ;; **Analysis:** The dehumanising metaphors 'egg' and 'fry' (young fish) stress the child's smallness and innocence, making the murder repulsive ;; **Context:** Killing children marks Macbeth as a complete tyrant
Macbeth – Point: Scotland suffers under the tyrant. :: **Quote:** "Bleed, bleed, poor country!" (Macduff, Act 4 Scene 3) ;; **Analysis:** Personification of Scotland as a wounded body; repetition shows Macduff's anguish ;; **Context:** A tyrant was thought to harm the whole nation, not just individuals
Macbeth – Point: Malcolm describes what makes a good king. :: **Quote:** "The king-becoming graces" (Malcolm, Act 4 Scene 3) ;; **Analysis:** His list of virtues ('justice, verity, temp'rance, stableness') defines ideal kingship – the opposite of Macbeth ;; **Context:** James I wrote about kingship in Basilikon Doron

(narrator, Chapter 4) ;; **Analysis:** After Curley's wife's threat he withdraws completely – 'nothing' shows how racism strips away his dignity and identity ;; **Context:** Black Americans had no protection against white accusations
Of Mice and Men – Point: Crooks defends the little space he has. :: **Quote:** "You got no right to come in my room." (Crooks, Chapter 4) ;; **Analysis:** He uses the language of 'rights' that society denies him; his room is the only place he has any power ;; **Context:** Segregation and exclusion
Of Mice and Men – Point: Crooks has seen countless men fail to achieve the dream. :: **Quote:** "that same damn thing in their heads" (Crooks, Chapter 4) ;; **Analysis:** The dream is something 'in their heads' – a fantasy shared by hundreds of men, none of whom got land ;; **Context:** The American Dream as illusion in the Depression
Of Mice and Men – Point: Curley's wife's dreams have been crushed. :: **Quote:** "Coulda been in the movies, an' had nice clothes" (Curley's wife, Chapter 5) ;; **Analysis:** The colloquial, wistful tone shows her longing for glamour and attention; she believes her mother stole her letter – her dream was probably never real ;; **Context:** Hollywood glamour as an escape for 1930s women
Of Mice and Men – Point: Curley's wife is trapped in an unhappy marriage. :: **Quote:** "I don't like Curley. He ain't a nice fella." # Exam technique – Edexcel IGCSE 4ET1
## The papers
How is Edexcel International GCSE English Literature (4ET1) assessed? :: **Paper 1** Poetry and Modern Prose – 2 hours, 90 marks, 60% ;; **Paper 2** Modern Drama and Literary Heritage Texts – 1 hour 30 minutes, 60 marks, 40% ;; (Some schools take coursework, Paper 3, instead of Paper 2 – check with your teacher)
What are the three sections of Paper 1? :: **Section A** Unseen Poetry – one question on a poem you have not seen, 20 marks ;; **Section B** Anthology Poetry – one question from a choice of two, comparing two anthology poems, 30 marks ;; **Section C** Modern Prose – one essay from a choice of two on Of Mice and Men, 40 marks
What are the two sections of Paper 2? :: **Section A** Modern Drama – one essay from a choice of two on An Inspector Calls, 30 marks ;; **Section B** Literary Heritage – one essay from a choice of two on Macbeth, 30 marks
Which paper and section are your three set texts in? :: **Of Mice and Men** – Paper 1, Section C (Modern Prose, 40 marks) ;; **An Inspector Calls** – Paper 2, Section A (Modern Drama, 30 marks) ;; **Macbeth** – Paper 2, Section B (Literary Heritage, 30 marks)
How does the anthology question in Paper 1 Section B work? :: The question names one poem from Part 3 of the Anthology and asks you to compare it with a second poem of your own choice from the same collection ;; so you must know all 16 poems well enough to pick a good partner and quote it from memory
How should you divide your time in Paper 1 (2 hours)? :: About 1⅓ minutes per mark: **Unseen poetry (20)** ~25–30 min including careful reading ;; **Anthology comparison (30)** ~40 min ;; **Of Mice and Men (40)** ~50 min ;; spend 3–5 minutes planning each answer
How should you divide your time in Paper 2 (1 hour 30)? :: Two 30-mark essays – about **45 minutes each**: ~5 min choosing and planning, ~35 min writing, ~5 min checking ;; don't overrun on the first essay – a rushed second essay costs more marks than you gain
## Assessment objectives
What is AO1? :: Demonstrate a close knowledge and understanding of texts, maintaining a critical style and presenting an informed personal engagement ;; = know the text, answer the question, use well-chosen references and quotations, write a clear argument
What is AO2? :: Analyse the language, form and structure used by a writer to create meanings and effects ;; = zoom in on methods (word choice, imagery, verse form, stage directions, structure) and explain their effect, using subject terminology
What is AO3? :: Explore links and connections between texts ;; = compare – similarities AND differences in ideas, attitudes and methods; assessed in the anthology poetry comparison
What is AO4? :: Show understanding of the relationships between texts and the contexts in which they were written ;; = link the text to its historical, social and literary context (e.g. the Depression, 1912 vs 1945) – woven into the argument, not bolted on
Which assessment objectives are tested in each section of 4ET1? :: **Unseen poetry** – AO1, AO2 ;; **Anthology poetry** – AO2, AO3 ;; **Modern prose (OMAM)** – AO1, AO4 ;; **Modern drama (AIC)** – AO1, AO4 ;; **Literary heritage (Macbeth)** – AO1, AO2 ;; (check the latest mark scheme for exact weightings)
What does the AO pattern mean for your Macbeth essay? :: Macbeth (Literary Heritage) rewards knowledge (AO1) and analysis of Shakespeare's language, form and structure (AO2) – so close analysis of methods is essential; context is useful only when it sharpens an interpretation
What does the AO pattern mean for your An Inspector Calls and Of Mice and Men essays? :: These reward AO1 and AO4 – a sustained argument with secure knowledge, and context (1912/1945, the Depression) linked to the writer's purpose throughout; comment on methods too, because it shows understanding of how meaning is made
## Top-band essays
What does a top-band (Level 5) answer look like? :: Perceptive, assured personal response; sustained critical style; well-chosen, embedded references; detailed analysis of how methods create meaning; (where assessed) context or comparison integrated throughout, not added at the end
What is a good essay structure for a set-text question? :: **Introduction** – answer the question directly with a thesis and the writer's purpose ;; **3–4 analytical paragraphs** – each a clear point about the question, traced across the text (beginning, middle, end) ;; **Conclusion** – return to the writer's message/purpose; don't just repeat
How should each analytical paragraph work? :: **Point** (answer the question) → **embedded short quotation** → **method** (name the technique) → **effect** (zoom in on a word; alternative interpretations) → **context or purpose** → link back to the question
Why should quotations be short and embedded? :: Short quotations (a few words) can be woven into your own sentences and analysed word by word; long quotations waste time and show less analysis ;; e.g. Macbeth's "vaulting ambition" suggests…
How do you show 'alternative interpretations'? :: Use tentative phrases: "perhaps", "could suggest", "alternatively", "a modern audience might…, whereas a Jacobean audience…" ;; offering two readings of one word or moment is a mark of a top-band answer
What does 'writer's purpose' mean, and how do you use it? :: Why the writer made a choice – what they want the audience/reader to think or feel ;; name the writer as a deliberate craftsman ("Priestley uses Sheila to…", "Steinbeck presents Crooks as…") rather than treating characters as real people
How should you plan a 30–40 mark essay in 5 minutes? :: Underline key words in the question ;; write a one-sentence thesis ;; choose 3–4 points covering the whole text ;; list 2–3 short quotations for each ;; note context/purpose links ;; decide an order that builds the argument
What should you do if a question is about a character or theme you didn't expect? :: Link it to what you know – every character connects to the big themes and the writer's purpose; track the character/theme at the start, middle and end of the text, and show how it changes
## Poetry questions
How should you structure the unseen poetry answer (20 marks)? :: **Intro** – what the poem is about and its overall tone/message ;; **Paragraphs** on language, imagery, form and structure, each linked to the question ;; **Ending** – how the poem develops or shifts (e.g. at a volta or the final lines) ;; AO2 analysis of methods is central
How should you structure the anthology comparison (30 marks)? :: **Intro** – both poems' attitudes to the theme in the question ;; **3–4 comparative paragraphs** – one aspect at a time (ideas, language, form, structure), discussing BOTH poems with connectives ;; **Conclusion** – key similarity and key difference
Which comparative connectives help with AO3? :: **Similarity** – similarly, likewise, both poets, in the same way ;; **Difference** – whereas, however, in contrast, on the other hand, unlike ;; aim to compare in every paragraph, not write about one poem then the other
How do you choose the second poem for the anthology comparison? :: Pick one that shares the theme in the question but offers a contrast in attitude or method, and that you know well enough to quote ;; prepare 2–3 'go-to' pairings for each poem before the exam

# Unseen poetry – method
What is a step-by-step method for the unseen poem? :: 1. Read the question and underline its focus ;; 2. Read the poem twice – once for sense, once with a pen ;; 3. Work out the speaker, situation and tone ;; 4. Annotate language, imagery, sound, form, structure ;; 5. Find the shift/turning point ;; 6. Plan 3–4 points answering the question ;; 7. Write, analysing methods and effects
What questions should you ask about the speaker and situation? :: Who is speaking (first person? persona?) and to whom? ;; What is happening, where and when? ;; What is the speaker's attitude or feeling? ;; Does it change by the end?
What is SMILE (a checklist for unseen poems)? :: **S**tructure – how the poem is organised and develops ;; **M**eaning – what it is about and its message ;; **I**magery – similes, metaphors, personification, senses ;; **L**anguage – word choices, connotations, tone ;; **E**ffect – how each choice makes the reader think/feel
What should you look for in the title and the ending of an unseen poem? :: The **title** often states the subject or sets up irony; the **ending** usually carries the message, a twist or a change of tone ;; comparing the opening with the ending shows how the poem develops
How do you analyse form in an unseen poem? :: Identify it (sonnet, ballad, free verse, dramatic monologue…) and ask why the poet chose it ;; e.g. a love sonnet used for grief, or free verse to suggest freedom or chaos ;; is the form regular or broken – and does that match the subject?
How do you analyse structure in an unseen poem? :: Look at stanza lengths, line lengths, enjambment, caesura, repetition, the order of ideas, shifts in time/tone (a volta), and the relationship between opening and ending ;; always say what effect the structure has
How do you write about rhyme and rhythm without 'feature spotting'? :: Name the feature only to explain its effect: e.g. "regular rhyming couplets create a calm, controlled tone that clashes with the violent subject" ;; never just say "the poem has an ABAB rhyme scheme"
What is the difference between 'tone' and 'mood'? :: **Tone** is the speaker's/poet's attitude (bitter, nostalgic, ironic, angry) ;; **mood** (atmosphere) is the feeling created for the reader (tense, peaceful, eerie) ;; describe both with precise adjectives
How do you zoom in on a single word for AO2? :: Pick a key word, give its connotations (associations), explain why the poet chose it over alternatives, and link it to the poem's meaning and the question ;; e.g. 'spools of suffering' – 'spools' suggests the endless, mechanical nature of the photographer's work
How long should you spend annotating the unseen poem? :: About 8–10 minutes of the ~25–30 minutes: careful reading and planning give better analysis than writing more ;; aim for 3–4 well-developed paragraphs
What common mistakes lose marks on the unseen poem? :: Retelling the poem line by line ;; naming techniques without effects ;; ignoring the question's focus ;; ignoring form and structure ;; claiming one fixed meaning when the poem is ambiguous ;; making up a biography of the poet

# Poetic terminology
## Language and imagery
Simile – definition and effect :: A comparison using 'like' or 'as' ;; makes an idea vivid or surprising by linking it to something familiar (e.g. "Blind eyes could blaze like meteors" in 'Do not go gentle')
Metaphor – definition and effect :: Saying one thing IS another ;; creates a stronger, more direct comparison than a simile and can carry many connotations
Extended metaphor – definition and effect :: A metaphor developed over several lines or the whole poem ;; builds a sustained idea (e.g. water as a divine blessing throughout 'Blessing')
Personification – definition and effect :: Giving human qualities to a non-human thing ;; makes nature or objects seem alive, threatening or sympathetic (e.g. 'The darkening garden watches')
Pathetic fallacy – definition and effect :: Weather or setting reflecting human emotions ;; creates atmosphere and mirrors a character's feelings (e.g. withered sedge in 'La Belle Dame sans Merci')
Imagery – definition and effect :: Language that appeals to the senses (visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, gustatory) ;; helps the reader picture and feel the experience
Semantic field (lexical field) – definition and effect :: A group of words linked to the same topic (e.g. religion in 'War Photographer') ;; builds a theme or atmosphere across the poem
Connotation – definition :: The ideas and feelings associated with a word beyond its literal meaning (e.g. 'red' connotes danger, passion, blood)
Juxtaposition – definition and effect :: Placing contrasting ideas or images side by side ;; highlights difference (e.g. war zones vs a peaceful English Sunday in 'War Photographer')
Oxymoron – definition and effect :: Two contradictory words together (e.g. 'blinding sight') ;; creates a striking paradox that makes the reader think
Paradox – definition and effect :: A statement that seems contradictory but reveals a truth (e.g. "Fair is foul") ;; challenges the reader
Hyperbole – definition and effect :: Deliberate exaggeration ;; emphasises strength of feeling
Symbolism – definition and effect :: An object, colour or image that represents a bigger idea (e.g. the piano = childhood and home) ;; adds layers of meaning
Irony – definition and effect :: A gap between what is said and what is meant, or between expectation and outcome ;; can be humorous, bitter or critical (e.g. the Duke's polite tone hiding murder)
Apostrophe (in poetry) – definition and effect :: Directly addressing someone absent or something non-human (e.g. "Tyger Tyger") ;; creates intensity and emotion
Rhetorical question – definition and effect :: A question asked for effect, not an answer ;; involves the reader, or shows doubt, anger or wonder
Imperative – definition and effect :: A command verb (e.g. "Remember me", "Rage, rage") ;; creates urgency, authority or pleading
Tricolon (rule of three) – definition and effect :: A list of three ;; sounds complete and memorable; builds emphasis
Listing / asyndeton / polysyndeton – definition and effect :: **Listing** piles up items ;; **asyndeton** – a list without conjunctions (fast, abrupt) ;; **polysyndeton** – repeated 'and' (slow, overwhelming, cumulative)
Colloquial language and dialect – definition and effect :: Informal, everyday or regional speech (e.g. Agard's Caribbean dialect) ;; creates an authentic voice and can assert identity
Persona / speaker – definition :: The voice in the poem, which may not be the poet (e.g. the Duke in 'My Last Duchess', the unborn child in 'Prayer Before Birth')
Anaphora – definition and effect :: Repetition of a word or phrase at the start of successive lines (e.g. "If you can…", "I am not yet born") ;; builds rhythm, emphasis and intensity
Repetition – definition and effect :: Repeating words, phrases or lines ;; emphasises key ideas, creates rhythm or a sense of obsession
## Sound and rhythm
Alliteration – definition and effect :: Repetition of the same consonant sound at the start of nearby words ;; draws attention to words; can sound harsh or soft depending on the sound
Sibilance – definition and effect :: Repeated 's', 'sh' or soft 'c' sounds ;; can sound soothing, secretive or sinister (hissing)
Plosives – definition and effect :: Harsh 'p', 'b', 't', 'd', 'k', 'g' sounds ;; create anger, violence or force
Assonance – definition and effect :: Repetition of vowel sounds in nearby words ;; creates musical or mournful effects; can slow the pace
Onomatopoeia – definition and effect :: Words that sound like what they describe (e.g. 'boom', 'tinkling') ;; makes the scene vivid and immediate
Rhyme scheme – definition :: The pattern of rhymes at line ends, labelled with letters (ABAB, AABB) ;; regular rhyme suggests order and control; broken rhyme suggests disruption
Rhyming couplet – definition and effect :: Two consecutive rhyming lines ;; sounds neat and final – often used for conclusions (e.g. end of a Shakespearean sonnet)
Half-rhyme (slant rhyme) – definition and effect :: Words that nearly rhyme ;; creates a sense of unease or something not quite resolved
Internal rhyme – definition and effect :: Rhyme within a line ;; speeds up rhythm and links ideas
Iambic pentameter – definition and effect :: Ten syllables per line in five iambs (da-DUM) ;; closest to natural speech; suggests control and seriousness (Shakespeare, Browning)
Trochaic metre – definition and effect :: Feet of stressed-unstressed (DUM-da) ;; sounds forceful, chant-like or unnatural (the witches in Macbeth, 'The Tyger')
Metre and rhythm – what is the difference? :: **Metre** is the regular pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables; **rhythm** is the overall flow and pace of the lines ;; a break in metre highlights a key moment
Refrain – definition and effect :: A line repeated at intervals (e.g. the two refrains of 'Do not go gentle') ;; reinforces the central idea and gives a song-like or obsessive quality
## Form and structure
Enjambment – definition and effect :: A sentence running over the end of a line without punctuation ;; creates flow, speed, excitement or a sense of something spilling out of control; can put emphasis on the first word of the next line
Caesura – definition and effect :: A pause mid-line (usually punctuation) ;; slows the pace, creates hesitation, drama or emphasis
End-stopped line – definition and effect :: A line ending with punctuation ;; creates a measured, controlled or final tone
Stanza names by length :: **Couplet** 2 lines ;; **tercet** 3 ;; **quatrain** 4 ;; **quintain** 5 ;; **sestet** 6 ;; **octave** (octet) 8
Volta – definition and effect :: A turn or shift in thought or tone, typically at line 9 of a Petrarchan sonnet or the final couplet of a Shakespearean sonnet ;; marks a change of argument or feeling
Petrarchan sonnet – definition :: 14 lines: an octave (ABBAABBA) presenting a problem and a sestet answering it, with a volta at line 9 (e.g. Rossetti's 'Remember')
Shakespearean sonnet – definition :: 14 lines of iambic pentameter: three quatrains (ABAB CDCD EFEF) and a final rhyming couplet (GG) that concludes or twists the argument (e.g. 'Sonnet 116')
Villanelle – definition and effect :: 19 lines: five tercets and a final quatrain, rhyming ABA, with two refrains repeated alternately and joined at the end ;; the repetition creates obsession or insistence (e.g. 'Do not go gentle into that good night')
Ballad – definition and effect :: A narrative poem in quatrains, often ABCB, with a simple, song-like rhythm, often telling a tragic story (e.g. 'La Belle Dame sans Merci')
Dramatic monologue – definition and effect :: A poem in which a single character (not the poet) speaks to a silent listener, revealing more about themselves than they intend (e.g. 'My Last Duchess')
Free verse – definition and effect :: Poetry with no regular rhyme or metre ;; feels natural and conversational, or suggests freedom, chaos or resistance to rules
Blank verse – definition :: Unrhymed iambic pentameter (used by Shakespeare for noble characters)
Lyric – definition :: A short poem expressing personal thoughts and feelings in the first person
Elegy – definition :: A poem of mourning or reflection on death (e.g. elements in 'Do not go gentle' and 'Remember')
Cyclical structure – definition and effect :: Ending where the poem began (repeated lines or images) ;; suggests nothing has changed, a trap, or completion
Chronological structure – definition :: Events presented in time order; a poem may instead use flashback or move between past and present (e.g. 'Piano')

# Poetry anthology – poem guide
## Overview
Which 16 poems are in Part 3 of the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English Anthology (4ET1)? :: 'If–' (Kipling) ;; 'Prayer Before Birth' (MacNeice) ;; 'Blessing' (Dharker) ;; 'Search For My Tongue' (Bhatt) ;; 'Half-past Two' (Fanthorpe) ;; 'Piano' (Lawrence) ;; 'Hide and Seek' (Scannell) ;; 'Sonnet 116' (Shakespeare) ;; 'La Belle Dame sans Merci' (Keats) ;; 'Poem at Thirty-Nine' (Walker) ;; 'War Photographer' (Duffy) ;; 'The Tyger' (Blake) ;; 'My Last Duchess' (Browning) ;; 'Half-caste' (Agard) ;; 'Do not go gentle into that good night' (Thomas) ;; 'Remember' (Rossetti)
Which anthology poems are about childhood? :: 'Half-past Two', 'Hide and Seek', 'Piano', 'Prayer Before Birth' (and memories of childhood in 'Poem at Thirty-Nine')
Which anthology poems are about parents and children? :: 'If–' (father to son), 'Do not go gentle' (son to dying father), 'Poem at Thirty-Nine' (daughter remembering father), 'Piano' (son remembering mother), 'Prayer Before Birth' (unborn child)
Which anthology poems are about love and relationships? :: 'Sonnet 116' (ideal, constant love), 'La Belle Dame sans Merci' (destructive love), 'My Last Duchess' (possessive, controlling marriage), 'Remember' (love beyond death)
Which anthology poems are about death and loss? :: 'Remember', 'Do not go gentle', 'Piano', 'Poem at Thirty-Nine', 'La Belle Dame sans Merci', 'My Last Duchess', 'War Photographer'
Which anthology poems are about identity and culture? :: 'Search For My Tongue' (language and identity), 'Half-caste' (mixed-race identity and prejudice), 'Poem at Thirty-Nine' (inheriting a parent's identity), 'Prayer Before Birth' (fear of losing individuality)
Which anthology poems are about conflict and violence? :: 'War Photographer' (war and media), 'Prayer Before Birth' (WW2 and cruelty), 'My Last Duchess' (hidden domestic violence), 'Half-caste' (prejudice as verbal aggression)
Which anthology poems are about power? :: 'My Last Duchess' (male power), 'The Tyger' (the creator's power), 'Prayer Before Birth' (oppressive society), 'La Belle Dame sans Merci' (a woman's power over a man), 'Half-caste' (the power of language/labels)
Which anthology poems are about time and memory? :: 'Half-past Two' (a child's sense of time), 'Piano' (memory overwhelming the present), 'Poem at Thirty-Nine' (looking back), 'Remember' (being remembered), 'Sonnet 116' (love outlasting time)
## If–
'If–': poet, date and context :: **Rudyard Kipling** (1865–1936), born in India; written c.1895, published 1910 ;; inspired by Leander Starr Jameson, leader of the failed Jameson Raid (1895–96), whom Kipling saw as calm in defeat ;; addressed to his son John, who was killed in WW1 (1915)
'If–': what is it about? :: A father advises his son on how to become a 'Man': keep calm under pressure, be patient and honest, dream and think without being ruled by them, accept success and failure equally, persevere, stay humble – if he does all this, the world is his
'If–': themes :: Masculinity and stoicism ('stiff upper lip'); self-control; integrity; perseverance; parent–child advice; Victorian/Edwardian ideals of duty and empire
'If–': form and structure :: Four eight-line stanzas in mostly iambic pentameter with ABAB rhyme (alternating feminine endings); the whole poem is one long sentence of conditional 'If' clauses, and the main clause only arrives in the last two lines – the reward is delayed just as the son must wait to become a man
'If–': key methods :: **Anaphora** of 'If you can' builds a list of demands ;; **balanced antitheses** (Triumph/Disaster, crowds/Kings) show a balanced character ;; **personification** of Triumph and Disaster as 'impostors' ;; **second-person address** and exclamation in the final line
'If–': comparison links :: **'Do not go gentle'** – both parent–child advice, but Kipling urges calm self-control while Thomas urges rage ;; **'Prayer Before Birth'** – both list what a person needs to face the world ;; **'Poem at Thirty-Nine'** – lessons passed from father to child
## Prayer Before Birth
'Prayer Before Birth': poet, date and context :: **Louis MacNeice** (1907–1963), born in Belfast; written in 1944 during the Second World War, when bombing, propaganda, totalitarian regimes and mass killing showed the worst of humanity
'Prayer Before Birth': what is it about? :: An unborn child prays to God (or the reader) for protection from the evils of the world: frightening creatures, human cruelty, guilt, pressure to conform and being turned into a machine; it ends demanding to be killed rather than lose its humanity
'Prayer Before Birth': themes :: Innocence vs corruption; loss of individuality; the cruelty of humanity; war; religion and sin; the pressure of society and authority
'Prayer Before Birth': form and structure :: A prayer/litany in eight stanzas, each beginning 'I am not yet born' followed by a request ;; stanzas grow longer and lines more crowded as the fears pile up, then the final stanza is just two lines, ending with the blunt 'Otherwise kill me.'
'Prayer Before Birth': key methods :: **Anaphora** ('I am not yet born') ;; **internal rhyme and alliteration** ('wise lies lure me', 'black racks rack me') making violence sound like a chant ;; **listing** of fears and of natural comforts ;; **imperatives** (hear, console, provide, fill) ;; **metaphor** of the 'cog in a machine'
'Prayer Before Birth': comparison links :: **'If–'** – both list qualities needed to survive the world, but MacNeice is fearful where Kipling is confident ;; **'War Photographer'** – the effects of violence and cruelty ;; **'Half-caste'** – resisting being labelled and reduced
## Search For My Tongue
'Search For My Tongue': poet, date and context :: **Sujata Bhatt** (born 1956, Ahmedabad, India); moved to the USA as a child and later lived in Germany; her mother tongue is Gujarati ;; published in 'Brunizem' (1988)
'Search For My Tongue': what is it about? :: The speaker explains to 'you' that having two languages feels like having two tongues; she fears her mother tongue (Gujarati) will rot and die because she uses English abroad – but it grows back in her dreams and 'blossoms' stronger than the foreign tongue
'Search For My Tongue': themes :: Language and cultural identity; living between two cultures; loss and survival of heritage; the strength of one's roots
'Search For My Tongue': form and structure :: Free verse in three sections: English (fear of loss) → Gujarati script with phonetic transliteration (the mother tongue literally returns on the page) → English translation and triumphant ending ;; structure enacts the regrowth of the tongue
'Search For My Tongue': key methods :: **Extended metaphor** of the tongue as a plant that rots, then regrows ('stump', 'shoot', 'bud', 'blossoms') ;; **double meaning** of 'tongue' (organ and language) ;; **direct address** to 'you' ;; **repetition** ('grows', 'the bud opens') builds momentum
'Search For My Tongue': comparison links :: **'Half-caste'** – both explore identity through language, but Agard is confrontational and witty while Bhatt is reflective ;; **'Poem at Thirty-Nine'** – heritage that stays with you ;; **'Piano'** – the past returning powerfully against the speaker's will
## Half-past Two
'Half-past Two': poet, date and context :: **U A Fanthorpe** (1929–2009), English poet who taught English at Cheltenham Ladies' College before working as a hospital clerk ;; the poem recalls a school memory from a child's point of view
'Half-past Two': what is it about? :: A young boy does 'Something Very Wrong' and his teacher tells him to stay in until half-past two – but he can't yet tell the time; she forgets him, and while he waits he 'escapes' into a timeless world, until she returns and releases him
'Half-past Two': themes :: Childhood and innocence; time (a child's vs an adult's understanding); memory; authority and power; escape and imagination
'Half-past Two': form and structure :: Free verse in three-line stanzas; opens like a fairy tale ('Once upon a schooltime') ;; moves from the classroom to the boy's 'clockless land' and back to reality; the ending looks back from adulthood
'Half-past Two': key methods :: **Child's perspective and vocabulary** ;; **capitalisation** ('Something Very Wrong', 'She') shows the teacher's huge authority ;; **compound words** ('Gettinguptime', 'TVtime') show time measured by events ;; **personification** of the clock face ;; parenthesis '(I forget what it was)'
'Half-past Two': comparison links :: **'Hide and Seek'** – a child alone and forgotten, a moment of change ;; **'Piano'** – adult memory of childhood ;; **'Remember'** and **'Sonnet 116'** – contrasting ideas about time
## Piano
'Piano': poet, date and context :: **D H Lawrence** (1885–1930), son of a Nottinghamshire coal miner and a mother he was very close to; she died in 1910 ;; published 1918
'Piano': what is it about? :: An adult man listens to a woman singing; the music carries him back to childhood, sitting under the piano pressing his mother's feet as she played hymns on Sunday evenings; despite trying to resist, he is overwhelmed and weeps 'like a child for the past'
'Piano': themes :: Memory and nostalgia; childhood; mother–son love; loss and grief; the power of music; masculinity and vulnerability
'Piano': form and structure :: Three quatrains in rhyming couplets (AABB) with long, flowing lines ;; moves between present and past: stanza 1 present → memory, stanza 2 resisting the memory, stanza 3 the present dissolves and he is 'cast / Down in the flood of remembrance'
'Piano': key methods :: **Sensory imagery and onomatopoeia** ('boom', 'tingling', 'tinkling') ;; **sibilance** ('Softly… singing') ;; **personification** of song as 'insidious' and betraying him ;; **contrast** between the 'great black piano appassionato' and the 'tinkling piano' of the past ;; final **simile** 'I weep like a child'
'Piano': comparison links :: **'Poem at Thirty-Nine'** – an adult missing a parent through a shared activity ;; **'Half-past Two'** – childhood memory ;; **'Search For My Tongue'** – something from the past returning that cannot be suppressed
## Hide and Seek
'Hide and Seek': poet, date and context :: **Vernon Scannell** (1922–2007), English poet who served in the Second World War and was also a boxer; many of his poems explore childhood and fear
'Hide and Seek': what is it about? :: A child hides in a toolshed during a game of hide and seek; he waits, hears the seekers nearby and stays hidden; as it grows dark he emerges to claim victory – but the others have gone home and he is alone
'Hide and Seek': themes :: Childhood; isolation and abandonment; growing up and disillusionment; triumph turning to loss
'Hide and Seek': form and structure :: One unbroken stanza in the present tense and second person ('you'), like the child talking to himself ;; short sentences and caesurae build tension; the ending turns from excitement to emptiness with a final unanswered question
'Hide and Seek': key methods :: **Imperatives** ('Call out', 'Don't breathe') ;; **sensory imagery** (smell of the sacks, the cold, the dark) ;; **personification** of the garden and bushes, which become threatening ;; **dramatic irony** – the reader suspects the others have left ;; **rhetorical question** at the end
'Hide and Seek': comparison links :: **'Half-past Two'** – a child forgotten by others, stepping outside normal time ;; **'Prayer Before Birth'** – a child's vulnerability ;; **'La Belle Dame sans Merci'** – both end with a lonely figure in a bleak landscape
## Sonnet 116
'Sonnet 116': poet, date and context :: **William Shakespeare** (1564–1616); published 1609 in his collection of 154 sonnets; part of the 'Fair Youth' sequence ;; Elizabethan sonnets were usually love poems
'Sonnet 116': what is it about? :: The speaker defines true love: a 'marriage of true minds' that does not change when circumstances change, is a fixed guide like a star or lighthouse, and lasts until doomsday despite the ageing of beauty ;; he stakes his reputation on it in the final couplet
'Sonnet 116': themes :: Ideal, constant love; time and ageing; marriage and commitment; faith and certainty
'Sonnet 116': form and structure :: Shakespearean sonnet – three quatrains (ABAB CDCD EFEF) and a rhyming couplet in iambic pentameter ;; Q1 what love is not, Q2 what love is (metaphors), Q3 love vs Time, couplet a confident challenge to the reader
'Sonnet 116': key methods :: **Negative definition** ('Love is not love / Which alters') ;; **nautical metaphors** ('ever-fixed mark', 'star to every wandering bark') ;; **personification** of Time as the Grim Reaper with a 'bending sickle' ;; **legal/religious language** ('impediments', 'error… proved') ;; **polyptoton** ('alters… alteration', 'remover to remove')
'Sonnet 116': comparison links :: **'Remember'** – love and death in sonnet form; Rossetti puts the beloved's happiness above being remembered ;; **'La Belle Dame sans Merci'** and **'My Last Duchess'** – destructive or controlling love as a contrast to ideal love
## La Belle Dame sans Merci
'La Belle Dame sans Merci': poet, date and context :: **John Keats** (1795–1821), Romantic poet; written 1819 ;; title ('the beautiful lady without pity') from a medieval French poem ;; Keats had nursed his brother Tom through tuberculosis and was in love with Fanny Brawne; he died of TB aged 25
'La Belle Dame sans Merci': what is it about? :: A passer-by asks a pale, lonely knight what is wrong; the knight tells how he met a beautiful faery lady, fell in love, and was lulled to sleep in her 'elfin grot', where he dreamed of pale kings and warriors warning that she had enslaved them; he woke alone on a cold hillside
'La Belle Dame sans Merci': themes :: Destructive love and desire; the femme fatale; the supernatural; illness and death; isolation; dreams vs reality
'La Belle Dame sans Merci': form and structure :: A ballad of twelve quatrains (ABCB), with a shortened final line in each stanza that sounds like a dying fall ;; frame narrative – stanzas 1–3 the questioner, 4–12 the knight ;; cyclical ending repeats the opening imagery
'La Belle Dame sans Merci': key methods :: **Pathetic fallacy** (withered sedge, no birds) ;; **imagery of illness** ('lily on thy brow', 'fever-dew', 'death-pale') ;; **archaic, medieval diction** ('thee', 'thrall', 'meads') ;; **ambiguity** – is the lady evil, or is the knight's obsession to blame? ;; repetition of 'Alone and palely loitering'
'La Belle Dame sans Merci': comparison links :: **'My Last Duchess'** – power between men and women (but reversed) ;; **'Sonnet 116'** – destructive vs constant love ;; **'Hide and Seek'** – ending alone in a cold, empty place
## Poem at Thirty-Nine
'Poem at Thirty-Nine': poet, date and context :: **Alice Walker** (born 1944, Georgia, USA), African American writer of 'The Color Purple'; her father was a poor sharecropper in the segregated South ;; published 1984
'Poem at Thirty-Nine': what is it about? :: At 39 the speaker misses her father; she remembers him teaching her to manage money, to tell the truth, and his joyful cooking; she realises she has become like him and believes he would have admired the woman she is
'Poem at Thirty-Nine': themes :: Parent–child relationships; memory and grief; inheritance and identity; growing up; independence
'Poem at Thirty-Nine': form and structure :: Free verse in four stanzas of very short lines, giving a conversational, reflective, hesitant feel ;; moves from regret (past) to memories to the present ('Now I look and cook just like him') to an imagined future approval
'Poem at Thirty-Nine': key methods :: **Exclamation and repetition** ('How I miss my father') ;; **simile** of cooking 'like a person / dancing / in a yoga meditation' ;; **enjambment** across short lines slows the reading ;; **list** of activities at the end ('cooking, writing, chopping wood') shows an independent woman
'Poem at Thirty-Nine': comparison links :: **'Piano'** – grief and memory of a parent, but Walker is grateful rather than overwhelmed ;; **'Do not go gentle'** – a child's love for a father facing death ;; **'If–'** – lessons passed from father to child
## War Photographer
'War Photographer': poet, date and context :: **Carol Ann Duffy** (born 1955), Poet Laureate 2009–2019; published 1985 in 'Standing Female Nude' ;; inspired by her friendship with war photographers such as Don McCullin
'War Photographer': what is it about? :: A war photographer develops his photos in his darkroom back in England; he remembers the horrors he witnessed; he knows his editor will print only a few and readers will feel brief sympathy before forgetting; he flies off to the next war
'War Photographer': themes :: Conflict and suffering; the role of the media; public indifference; memory and trauma; duty; contrast between safe England and war zones
'War Photographer': form and structure :: Four regular six-line stanzas (rhyming ABBCDD) – the ordered form mirrors his 'ordered rows' of film and his attempt to control chaotic memories ;; moves from darkroom → memories → developing a photo → the public's reaction → leaving again
'War Photographer': key methods :: **Religious semantic field** (church, priest, Mass, 'All flesh is grass') makes his work a sacred duty ;; **alliteration** ('spools of suffering') ;; **juxtaposition** of 'Rural England' and war zones ;; **caesura** in 'Belfast. Beirut. Phnom Penh.' ;; **ghost** imagery as the photo develops
'War Photographer': comparison links :: **'Blessing'** – suffering in a poorer country seen from outside, and how we respond to it ;; **'Prayer Before Birth'** – human cruelty ;; **'Remember'** – memory and forgetting
## The Tyger
'The Tyger': poet, date and context :: **William Blake** (1757–1827), Romantic poet, artist and radical Christian; published 1794 in 'Songs of Experience', paired with 'The Lamb' from 'Songs of Innocence' (1789) ;; written during the Industrial Revolution and the French Revolution
'The Tyger': what is it about? :: The speaker asks a series of questions about who could have created the terrifying, beautiful tiger – what kind of power, hand and tools could make it – and whether the same God who made the gentle Lamb also made the Tyger
'The Tyger': themes :: Creation and God; good and evil; the sublime (beauty and terror); power; innocence vs experience
'The Tyger': form and structure :: Six quatrains in rhyming couplets (AABB) with a strong, mostly trochaic beat like a hammer or heartbeat ;; first and last stanzas almost identical (cyclical), but 'Could frame' becomes 'Dare frame' – the question shifts from ability to daring
'The Tyger': key methods :: **Rhetorical questions** – the whole poem asks and never answers ;; **fire imagery** ('burning bright') ;; **metaphor of the blacksmith/industry** (hammer, chain, furnace, anvil) ;; **apostrophe** ('Tyger Tyger') ;; **allusions** to the Lamb (Christ) and to rebellion ('stars threw down their spears')
'The Tyger': comparison links :: **'Prayer Before Birth'** – God, creation and evil in the world ;; **'My Last Duchess'** – fearsome power ;; **'La Belle Dame sans Merci'** – Romantic fascination with dangerous beauty
## My Last Duchess
'My Last Duchess': poet, date and context :: **Robert Browning** (1812–1889), Victorian poet; published 1842 ;; based on Alfonso II, Duke of Ferrara (16th-century Italy), whose young wife died in 1561 in suspicious circumstances; he then negotiated to marry a count's daughter
'My Last Duchess': what is it about? :: The Duke shows a visitor (an envoy arranging his next marriage) a portrait of his dead first wife; he complains she smiled at everyone and valued his 'nine-hundred-years-old name' no more than small kindnesses; he 'gave commands' and her smiles 'stopped' – implying he had her killed
'My Last Duchess': themes :: Power and control; jealousy and pride; status and wealth; women as possessions; art and appearance vs reality
'My Last Duchess': form and structure :: A dramatic monologue in rhyming couplets of iambic pentameter, but with heavy enjambment, so the rhyme is barely noticed – the Duke's controlled surface hides something sinister ;; ends with him moving on to another possession (the bronze Neptune)
'My Last Duchess': key methods :: **Dramatic irony** – he reveals more than he intends ;; **caesura and parenthesis** ('how shall I say?') show false hesitation ;; **euphemism** ('I gave commands') ;; **possessive pronouns** ('my') ;; the **curtain** he controls symbolises his power over her ;; the Neptune statue 'taming a sea-horse' symbolises domination
'My Last Duchess': comparison links :: **'La Belle Dame sans Merci'** – power in relationships (male vs female control) ;; **'Sonnet 116'** – possessive vs ideal love ;; **'The Tyger'** – terrifying power
## Half-caste
'Half-caste': poet, date and context :: **John Agard** (born 1949, British Guiana, now Guyana); moved to the UK in 1977 ;; writes and performs in Caribbean Creole and Standard English; 'half-caste' was a common, offensive term for mixed-race people
'Half-caste': what is it about? :: The speaker challenges a listener who calls him 'half-caste': using examples of great mixtures (Picasso's colours, English weather, Tchaikovsky's black and white keys) he mocks the term, then sarcastically offers 'half' of himself, and finally demands the listener come back with a 'whole' mind to hear his full story
'Half-caste': themes :: Identity and race; prejudice and the power of language; ignorance; pride; challenging labels
'Half-caste': form and structure :: Free verse with almost no punctuation apart from slashes; three movements – mocking opening, examples of mixing, sarcastic 'half' of himself – ending with a shift to a serious invitation
'Half-caste': key methods :: **Phonetic Creole** ('yu', 'dah', 'wid') asserts his identity ;; **sarcasm and humour** ('standing on one leg') ;; **rhetorical questions and imperatives** ('Explain yuself') ;; **analogies** with art, weather and music ;; **repetition** of 'half' and finally 'whole'
'Half-caste': comparison links :: **'Search For My Tongue'** – identity and language, defiance vs reflection ;; **'Prayer Before Birth'** – refusing to be reduced to 'a thing' ;; **'Poem at Thirty-Nine'** – pride in who you have become
## Do not go gentle into that good night
'Do not go gentle': poet, date and context :: **Dylan Thomas** (1914–1953), Welsh poet; written for his father, a former English teacher who was ill and going blind; published 1951, and his father died in 1952
'Do not go gentle': what is it about? :: The speaker urges his dying father not to accept death quietly but to fight it; he describes how wise, good, wild and grave men all rage against death because of things left unachieved; finally he begs his father to 'Curse, bless' him and to rage
'Do not go gentle': themes :: Death and mortality; father–son relationships; regret and unfulfilled lives; defiance; love and grief
'Do not go gentle': form and structure :: A villanelle – five tercets and a quatrain, ABA rhyme, with two refrains ('Do not go gentle into that good night' / 'Rage, rage against the dying of the light') alternating and joined in the final quatrain ;; the strict, repetitive form mirrors the speaker's desperate insistence – and death's inevitability
'Do not go gentle': key methods :: **Imperatives** ('Do not', 'Rage') ;; **metaphors of light and dark** for life and death ('good night', 'dying of the light') ;; **oxymorons** ('blinding sight', 'Curse, bless') ;; **pun** on 'Grave men' ;; **listing** of types of men ;; **direct address** 'And you, my father'
'Do not go gentle': comparison links :: **'Remember'** – opposite attitudes to death: rage vs acceptance ;; **'Poem at Thirty-Nine'** – a child's love for a father ;; **'If–'** – a father advising a son vs a son advising a father
## Remember
'Remember': poet, date and context :: **Christina Rossetti** (1830–1894), Victorian poet, devout Anglican, linked to the Pre-Raphaelites; written 1849 (aged 19), published 1862 ;; Victorians were preoccupied with death and mourning
'Remember': what is it about? :: The speaker asks her beloved to remember her after she dies; then, after the volta, she changes her mind – if remembering her makes him sad, she would rather he forgot her and smiled
'Remember': themes :: Death; love; memory and forgetting; selflessness; faith
'Remember': form and structure :: A Petrarchan sonnet – octave (ABBAABBA) asking to be remembered, volta at line 9 ('Yet'), sestet (CDDECE) letting him forget ;; the turn shows love maturing from need into selflessness
'Remember': key methods :: **Imperatives** ('Remember me') softened by 'only' ;; **euphemisms for death** ('gone away', 'the silent land') ;; **repetition** of 'remember' ;; **volta** marked by 'Yet' ;; **antithesis** in the final couplet ('forget and smile' vs 'remember and be sad')
'Remember': comparison links :: **'Do not go gentle'** – acceptance vs defiance of death ;; **'Sonnet 116'** – love in sonnet form that outlasts time ;; **'War Photographer'** – remembering and forgetting
## Blessing
'Blessing': poet, date and context :: **Imtiaz Dharker** (born 1954 in Pakistan, raised in Glasgow, lived in Mumbai) ;; published 1989 ;; set in a poor district of an Indian city where water is scarce; a burst municipal water pipe brings a moment of joy to people who rarely have enough
'Blessing': what is it about? :: Water is so scarce that even a drip seems holy; when a municipal pipe bursts, the whole community rushes out with pots and buckets, and children play in the water as if it were a blessing from a god
'Blessing': themes :: Poverty and scarcity; the value of water; religion and blessings; community and joy; inequality (a 'blessing' that is really an accident)
'Blessing': form and structure :: Four free-verse stanzas of uneven length that grow longer as the water flows ;; opens with a short, stark two-line stanza; the long final stanza is full of movement and celebration – the form mirrors scarcity turning to abundance
'Blessing': key methods :: **Religious semantic field** ('blessing', 'congregation', 'kindly god') ;; **simile** ('The skin cracks like a pod') ;; **onomatopoeia** ('drip', 'splash') ;; **metaphor** of water as 'silver' – precious wealth ;; **listing** of containers ('pots, brass, copper, aluminium, plastic buckets') ;; **irony** in the title
'Blessing': comparison links :: **'War Photographer'** – suffering in poorer countries seen from outside ;; **'Search For My Tongue'** – South Asian setting and identity ;; **'Half-past Two'** – a child's-eye moment where ordinary time stops

# Poetry anthology – points, quotes and analysis
'If–' – Point: Kipling's ideal man keeps calm when others panic. :: **Quote:** "If you can keep your head" (Kipling, If–, stanza 1) ;; **Analysis:** The idiom 'keep your head' means staying calm and rational; it opens the anaphora of 'If you can', the first of many conditions. The steady iambic rhythm itself sounds controlled ;; **Context:** Victorian/Edwardian ideal of the 'stiff upper lip'; inspired by Jameson's calm after the failed Jameson Raid
'If–' – Point: Kipling values imagination, but only under control. :: **Quote:** "not make dreams your master" (Kipling, If–, stanza 2) ;; **Analysis:** The line qualifies the dream (it follows a dash: 'If you can dream –'); 'master' personifies dreams as a ruler who could enslave you. Balance and self-discipline are the keys to manhood ;; **Context:** Empire-era values of duty and practical action over idle fantasy
'If–' – Point: Success and failure should be treated with equal detachment. :: **Quote:** "treat those two impostors just the same" (Kipling, If–, stanza 2) ;; **Analysis:** Triumph and Disaster are personified (capital letters) and called 'impostors' – neither is real or lasting. The balanced pairing reflects the balanced mind Kipling wants ;; **Context:** The line is written above the players' entrance to Centre Court at Wimbledon – a famous ideal of sportsmanship
'If–' – Point: A true man uses every moment purposefully. :: **Quote:** "the unforgiving minute" (Kipling, If–, stanza 4) ;; **Analysis:** Personification makes time harsh and relentless; the following image of 'sixty seconds' worth of distance run' turns life into a race demanding constant effort ;; **Context:** Victorian work ethic and the drive of the British Empire
'If–' – Point: Kipling values humility whatever one's status. :: **Quote:** "walk with Kings – nor lose the common touch" (Kipling, If–, stanza 4) ;; **Analysis:** Antithesis between 'Kings' and the 'common touch' shows a man equally at ease with all classes. The dash creates a pause that stresses the moral warning ;; **Context:** Kipling's class-conscious, imperial society; the ideal English gentleman
'If–' – Point: The reward of manhood is delayed until the very end. :: **Quote:** "you'll be a Man, my son!" (Kipling, If–, stanza 4) ;; **Analysis:** The main clause finally resolves the long conditional sentence; the capital 'M' makes 'Man' an ideal to achieve, and the exclamation and intimate 'my son' add warmth and pride ;; **Context:** Addressed to Kipling's son John, killed in the First World War in 1915 – a poignant irony for modern readers
Prayer Before Birth – Point: The unborn child desperately pleads for protection. :: **Quote:** "I am not yet born; O hear me." (MacNeice, Prayer Before Birth, stanza 1) ;; **Analysis:** The anaphoric opening is repeated in every stanza like a litany; the apostrophe 'O hear me' and the imperative make it a prayer and a cry for help ;; **Context:** Written in 1944 during the Second World War
Prayer Before Birth – Point: The child fears manipulation and propaganda. :: **Quote:** "with wise lies lure me" (MacNeice, Prayer Before Birth, stanza 2) ;; **Analysis:** The oxymoron 'wise lies' suggests clever deception; alliteration and internal rhyme make the threat sound sing-song and seductive ;; **Context:** WW2 propaganda and totalitarian regimes (Nazi Germany, Stalin's USSR)
Prayer Before Birth – Point: Human cruelty is presented as torture and slaughter. :: **Quote:** "on black racks rack me, in blood-baths roll me" (MacNeice, Prayer Before Birth, stanza 2) ;; **Analysis:** Harsh plosives and the repetition of 'rack' sound violent; 'black' suggests evil. The verbs at the end of each phrase make the child the helpless object of violence ;; **Context:** Torture, war crimes and mass killing in WW2
Prayer Before Birth – Point: The child longs for the comfort of nature. :: **Quote:** "water to dandle me, grass to grow for me" (MacNeice, Prayer Before Birth, stanza 3) ;; **Analysis:** Personification makes nature a loving parent; gentle alliteration and the softer rhythm contrast with the violent stanza before ;; **Context:** Romantic idea of nature as pure, against a corrupt, mechanised modern world
Prayer Before Birth – Point: The child expects to be blamed for the world's sins. :: **Quote:** "the sins that in me the world shall commit" (MacNeice, Prayer Before Birth, stanza 4) ;; **Analysis:** The inverted syntax shows the world acting through the child – he will be corrupted by society, not born evil. The religious word 'sins' continues the prayer form ;; **Context:** Christian ideas of original sin; the guilt of ordinary people in wartime
Prayer Before Birth – Point: The greatest fear is losing individuality. :: **Quote:** "would make me a cog in a machine" (MacNeice, Prayer Before Birth, stanza 7) ;; **Analysis:** The industrial metaphor shows people reduced to replaceable parts in a system; it builds to 'a thing with / one face', a total loss of humanity ;; **Context:** Mass armies and totalitarian states demanded obedience and conformity
Prayer Before Birth – Point: The poem ends with a shocking demand. :: **Quote:** "Otherwise kill me." (MacNeice, Prayer Before Birth, final line) ;; **Analysis:** The short, blunt final line after long, crowded stanzas is a powerful shock: death is better than a life without humanity. The imperative shows the child's resolve ;; **Context:** Despair at the horrors of 1944
Search For My Tongue – Point: The speaker feels she has lost part of her identity. :: **Quote:** "by saying I have lost my tongue" (Bhatt, Search For My Tongue, opening) ;; **Analysis:** The pun on 'tongue' (body part and language) makes losing a language feel like physical mutilation; the conversational address to 'you' draws the reader in ;; **Context:** Bhatt grew up speaking Gujarati and moved to the USA as a child
Search For My Tongue – Point: Using a foreign language threatens to destroy the mother tongue. :: **Quote:** "rot and die in your mouth" (Bhatt, Search For My Tongue, opening section) ;; **Analysis:** Repetition of 'rot' and the image of decay are disgusting and frightening; the second person ('your') makes the reader imagine it happening to them ;; **Context:** Immigrants' fear of losing their heritage language and culture
Search For My Tongue – Point: The mother tongue grows back while she sleeps. :: **Quote:** "it grows back, a stump of a shoot" (Bhatt, Search For My Tongue, final section) ;; **Analysis:** The extended plant metaphor begins: 'stump' suggests damage, 'shoot' new growth. Her unconscious mind keeps her culture alive ;; **Context:** Identity rooted deeply in childhood language
Search For My Tongue – Point: The mother tongue overpowers the foreign tongue. :: **Quote:** "it ties the other tongue in knots" (Bhatt, Search For My Tongue, final section) ;; **Analysis:** The idiom 'tongue-tied' is made literal – Gujarati confuses and dominates English, showing the strength of her native identity ;; **Context:** Living between two cultures
Search For My Tongue – Point: Her native language returns with growing energy. :: **Quote:** "the bud opens, the bud opens in my mouth" (Bhatt, Search For My Tongue, final section) ;; **Analysis:** Repetition slows the moment and celebrates it; the bud suggests new life and beauty about to flower ;; **Context:** The Gujarati lines printed in the poem physically show the mother tongue returning
Search For My Tongue – Point: The ending celebrates the survival of her identity. :: **Quote:** "it blossoms out of my mouth." (Bhatt, Search For My Tongue, last line) ;; **Analysis:** 'Blossoms' completes the plant metaphor with beauty and fulfilment; the confident final line reverses the fear of the opening ;; **Context:** Pride in heritage despite migration
Half-past Two – Point: The adult world seems huge and frightening to the child. :: **Quote:** "He did Something Very Wrong" (Fanthorpe, Half-past Two, opening) ;; **Analysis:** Capitals make the phrase sound official and enormous from the child's view; the vagueness shows he doesn't understand what he did ;; **Context:** Strict mid-20th-century schooling and adult authority
Half-past Two – Point: The adult narrator has forgotten the crime but not the experience. :: **Quote:** "I forget what it was" (Fanthorpe, Half-past Two, stanza 1) ;; **Analysis:** The aside in brackets is humorous – the 'Something Very Wrong' was trivial – and shows that what lasted was the strange experience of time, not the punishment ;; **Context:** The poem is a memory looking back on childhood
Half-past Two – Point: The child understands time through events, not clocks. :: **Quote:** "Gettinguptime, timeyouwereofftime, / Timetogohomenowtime, TVtime" (Fanthorpe, Half-past Two, middle) ;; **Analysis:** Invented compound words copy a child's way of speaking and show time as a series of routines rather than numbers ;; **Context:** Fanthorpe was a teacher and observed children closely
Half-past Two – Point: The child cannot yet read the clock. :: **Quote:** "But he couldn't click its language" (Fanthorpe, Half-past Two, middle) ;; **Analysis:** The clock is personified with a face and a 'language' he can't 'click' (understand); onomatopoeic 'click' echoes its ticking ;; **Context:** The gap between adult and child understanding
Half-past Two – Point: Being forgotten frees the child into a timeless world. :: **Quote:** "He escaped into the clockless land for ever" (Fanthorpe, Half-past Two, ending) ;; **Analysis:** The neologism 'clockless' suggests a magical, fairy-tale land; 'escaped' and 'for ever' show the memory has stayed with him as adult ;; **Context:** The fairy-tale opening 'Once upon a schooltime' prepares for this magical escape
Half-past Two – Point: Time is presented as something alive and waiting. :: **Quote:** "Where time hides tick-less waiting to be born." (Fanthorpe, Half-past Two, last line) ;; **Analysis:** Personification of time as unborn suggests the innocence of childhood before adult routines; 'tick-less' echoes 'clockless' ;; **Context:** Childhood innocence versus the adult world of schedules
Piano – Point: The music transports the speaker back to childhood. :: **Quote:** "in the boom of the tingling strings" (Lawrence, Piano, stanza 1) ;; **Analysis:** Onomatopoeia ('boom', 'tingling') recreates how the piano sounded and felt to a child underneath it – a vivid, sensory memory ;; **Context:** Lawrence was very close to his mother, who died in 1910
Piano – Point: The speaker resists nostalgia, but the music overpowers him. :: **Quote:** "the insidious mastery of song" (Lawrence, Piano, stanza 2) ;; **Analysis:** 'Insidious' suggests something sly and harmful; 'mastery' shows the song controlling him. Nostalgia is presented as a seductive trap ;; **Context:** Early 20th-century expectations that men should hide emotion
Piano – Point: The speaker longs to belong to the past. :: **Quote:** "the heart of me weeps to belong" (Lawrence, Piano, stanza 2) ;; **Analysis:** The unusual phrase 'the heart of me' suggests his deepest self; 'weeps' foreshadows his tears at the end ;; **Context:** Grief for his mother and lost family life
Piano – Point: The childhood home was warm and secure. :: **Quote:** "the tinkling piano our guide" (Lawrence, Piano, stanza 2) ;; **Analysis:** The light onomatopoeia 'tinkling' contrasts with the loud present-day piano; 'guide' suggests the music and his mother directed family life ;; **Context:** Sunday hymns in a religious working-class home
Piano – Point: The present-day performance cannot compete with memory. :: **Quote:** "the great black piano appassionato" (Lawrence, Piano, stanza 3) ;; **Analysis:** The musical term 'appassionato' (passionately) and 'great black' suggest a loud, showy concert – yet it seems hollow next to the past ;; **Context:** Contrast between a grand public performance and private family music
Piano – Point: The speaker is finally overwhelmed by grief and nostalgia. :: **Quote:** "I weep like a child for the past." (Lawrence, Piano, stanza 3) ;; **Analysis:** The simile shows the adult reduced to a child; the final line admits defeat, and the 'flood of remembrance' that precedes it shows memory sweeping him away ;; **Context:** Lawrence's grief for his mother
Hide and Seek – Point: The poem opens with the child's excitement. :: **Quote:** "Call loud: 'I'm ready! Come and find me!'" (Scannell, Hide and Seek, opening) ;; **Analysis:** Short imperatives and exclamations create energy and confidence; the second person puts the reader in the child's place ;; **Context:** Scannell often wrote about childhood experience
Hide and Seek – Point: Sensory details make the hiding place vivid. :: **Quote:** "The sacks in the toolshed smell like the seaside." (Scannell, Hide and Seek, opening) ;; **Analysis:** The simile links the shed to a happy childhood memory; sibilance creates a hushed, secretive tone ;; **Context:** Childhood play and imagination
Hide and Seek – Point: Tension builds as the child stays hidden. :: **Quote:** "Don't move. Stay dumb. Hide in your blindness." (Scannell, Hide and Seek, middle) ;; **Analysis:** Short imperative sentences and caesurae create suspense; 'blindness' suggests he cannot see what is really happening – foreshadowing ;; **Context:** The game becomes a metaphor for isolation
Hide and Seek – Point: The setting turns threatening as time passes. :: **Quote:** "The darkening garden watches. Nothing stirs." (Scannell, Hide and Seek, ending) ;; **Analysis:** Personification makes the garden sinister; 'darkening' shows time passing and the short sentence 'Nothing stirs' creates eerie silence ;; **Context:** Pathetic fallacy reflects growing loneliness
Hide and Seek – Point: Nature seems to wait for the child's discovery. :: **Quote:** "The bushes hold their breath; the sun is gone." (Scannell, Hide and Seek, ending) ;; **Analysis:** Personification echoes the child holding his breath; the loss of the sun symbolises the end of happiness and innocence ;; **Context:** The move from day to night mirrors a move from innocence to experience
Hide and Seek – Point: Victory turns into abandonment. :: **Quote:** "But where are they who sought you?" (Scannell, Hide and Seek, last line) ;; **Analysis:** The final rhetorical question is left unanswered; the child's triumph is empty because he is alone. It may symbolise the loneliness of growing up ;; **Context:** Childhood games as a rehearsal for adult disappointment

# Macbeth – plot by act
Macbeth Act 1 Scenes 1–3 – what happens? :: **1.1** Three witches plan to meet Macbeth ('Fair is foul') ;; **1.2** A wounded Captain reports Macbeth's heroism against the rebels; Duncan orders the traitor Thane of Cawdor executed and gives his title to Macbeth ;; **1.3** The witches hail Macbeth as Thane of Glamis, Thane of Cawdor and future king, and tell Banquo his descendants will be kings; Ross then announces Macbeth is Thane of Cawdor
Macbeth Act 1 Scenes 4–5 – what happens? :: **1.4** Duncan names his son Malcolm Prince of Cumberland (heir); Macbeth's aside reveals 'black and deep desires'; Duncan will stay at Macbeth's castle, Inverness ;; **1.5** Lady Macbeth reads Macbeth's letter, fears he is too kind, calls on spirits to 'unsex' her and plans Duncan's murder when he arrives
Macbeth Act 1 Scenes 6–7 – what happens? :: **1.6** Duncan arrives and praises the 'pleasant seat' of the castle – dramatic irony ;; **1.7** In a soliloquy Macbeth lists reasons not to kill Duncan and decides 'We will proceed no further'; Lady Macbeth attacks his manhood and outlines a plan to drug the guards and blame them; he agrees
Macbeth Act 2 – what happens? :: **2.1** Banquo is troubled by dreams; Macbeth sees a dagger ;; **2.2** Macbeth kills Duncan offstage, hears voices crying 'Sleep no more', and brings back the daggers; Lady Macbeth returns them and smears the guards with blood; knocking ;; **2.3** The drunken Porter pretends to be hell's gatekeeper; Macduff discovers the body; Macbeth kills the guards; Lady Macbeth faints; Malcolm flees to England and Donalbain to Ireland ;; **2.4** Unnatural events reported; Macbeth to be crowned; Macduff refuses to attend
Macbeth Act 3 Scenes 1–3 – what happens? :: **3.1** Banquo suspects Macbeth; Macbeth, now king, fears Banquo's 'royalty of nature' and the prophecy about his heirs, so hires murderers to kill Banquo and Fleance ;; **3.2** Macbeth hides the plan from Lady Macbeth ('Be innocent of the knowledge') ;; **3.3** Banquo is murdered but Fleance escapes
Macbeth Act 3 Scenes 4–6 – what happens? :: **3.4** At the banquet Banquo's ghost sits in Macbeth's place; only Macbeth sees it and his outbursts alarm the guests; Lady Macbeth sends them away; Macbeth notes Macduff's absence and resolves to visit the witches ;; **3.5** Hecate scolds the witches (often cut) ;; **3.6** Lennox sarcastically voices suspicion of Macbeth; Macduff has gone to England to raise an army with Malcolm
Macbeth Act 4 Scene 1 – what happens? :: Macbeth visits the witches; apparitions warn him to 'beware Macduff', say 'none of woman born' can harm him, and that he is safe until Birnam Wood comes to Dunsinane; a show of eight kings descended from Banquo torments him ;; learning Macduff has fled to England, he orders the murder of Macduff's wife and children
Macbeth Act 4 Scenes 2–3 – what happens? :: **4.2** Lady Macduff, feeling abandoned, talks with her son; murderers kill them ;; **4.3** In England Malcolm tests Macduff's loyalty by pretending to be more evil than Macbeth; satisfied, he reveals that the English king has given them an army under Siward; Ross brings news of the slaughter; Macduff grieves ('he must also feel it as a man') and vows revenge
Macbeth Act 5 Scenes 1–4 – what happens? :: **5.1** A Doctor and Gentlewoman watch Lady Macbeth sleepwalk, washing imaginary blood and revealing the murders ;; **5.2** Scottish lords join the English army near Birnam Wood ;; **5.3** Macbeth, deserted by his thanes, is defiant but weary ('the sere, the yellow leaf') and trusts the prophecies ;; **5.4** Malcolm orders soldiers to cut branches from Birnam Wood as camouflage
Macbeth Act 5 Scenes 5–end – what happens? :: Lady Macbeth's death is announced; Macbeth's 'Tomorrow' speech; a messenger reports the wood moving ;; Macbeth fights, killing Young Siward ;; Macduff reveals he was 'from his mother's womb / Untimely ripped' (born by caesarean); Macbeth fights on and is killed offstage ;; Macduff brings Macbeth's head; Malcolm is hailed king, makes his thanes earls and calls Macbeth a 'butcher'
What are the key turning points in the structure of Macbeth? :: **Inciting incident** – witches' prophecies (1.3) ;; **Point of no return** – Duncan's murder (2.2) ;; **Climax / turning point** – Fleance escapes and Banquo's ghost appears (3.3–3.4): Macbeth's control begins to slip ;; **Falling action** – murder of Macduff's family; armies gather ;; **Catastrophe** – Lady Macbeth's death, Macbeth killed, order restored (Act 5)

# Macbeth – characters
## Macbeth
Macbeth – role and summary :: Thane of Glamis, later Thane of Cawdor and King of Scotland; the tragic hero whose 'vaulting ambition', encouraged by the witches and his wife, leads him to murder Duncan and become a paranoid tyrant who is finally killed by Macduff
Macbeth – how does he develop through the play? :: **Act 1** brave, loyal warrior ('brave Macbeth') but tempted, wrestling with conscience ;; **Act 2** guilt-stricken murderer ('this blood / Clean from my hand?') ;; **Act 3** paranoid king who plans murders alone ('full of scorpions') ;; **Act 4** ruthless tyrant who kills innocent children ;; **Act 5** weary, numb nihilist ('a tale / Told by an idiot') who still fights bravely to the end
Macbeth – key short quotations to learn :: 'So foul and fair a day' (1.3) ;; 'vaulting ambition' (1.7) ;; 'Is this a dagger' (2.1) ;; 'Macbeth does murder sleep' (2.2) ;; 'full of scorpions is my mind' (3.2) ;; 'I am in blood / Stepped in so far' (3.4) ;; 'Life's but a walking shadow' (5.5) ;; 'Lay on, Macduff' (5.8)
Macbeth – Shakespeare's purpose :: To show the destructive power of unchecked ambition and the consequences of breaking the natural order (regicide); as a tragic hero he arouses pity and fear – a warning to the audience (and flattering to James I) that usurpers are punished
Macbeth – is he a villain or a victim? (interpretations) :: **Victim** – manipulated by the witches' prophecies and his wife; troubled conscience shows goodness ;; **Villain** – he first thinks of murder himself (1.3 'horrible imaginings'), later acts alone and kills children ;; a strong answer argues he is responsible: the witches only predict, he chooses
## Lady Macbeth
Lady Macbeth – role and summary :: Macbeth's wife and the driving force behind Duncan's murder; she calls on evil spirits, manipulates her husband by questioning his manhood, and plans the murder – but is later excluded, haunted by guilt, sleepwalks and dies (suggested suicide)
Lady Macbeth – how does she develop through the play? :: **Act 1** ambitious, ruthless, dominant ('unsex me here') ;; **Act 2** calm and practical ('A little water clears us') but faints (real or fake?) ;; **Act 3** losing influence; dissatisfied ('Nought's had, all's spent') ;; **Act 5** mad with guilt, speaking in prose ('Out, damned spot!') ;; dies offstage – 'by self and violent hands'
Lady Macbeth – key short quotations to learn :: 'too full o' th' milk of human kindness' (1.5) ;; 'unsex me here' (1.5) ;; 'look like th' innocent flower' (1.5) ;; 'When you durst do it, then you were a man' (1.7) ;; 'A little water clears us of this deed' (2.2) ;; 'Out, damned spot!' (5.1) ;; 'What's done cannot be undone' (5.1)
Lady Macbeth – Shakespeare's purpose :: To explore gender roles: she subverts the Jacobean ideal of the obedient wife, links her to witchcraft (summoning spirits), and her collapse suggests that going against nature and God brings punishment; her arc mirrors Macbeth's in reverse
## Banquo and Fleance
Banquo – role and summary :: Macbeth's fellow general who also hears the prophecies; he is cautious ('instruments of darkness') and resists temptation, though he suspects Macbeth and keeps quiet; murdered on Macbeth's orders but returns as a ghost; his son **Fleance** escapes, so the prophecy that Banquo's heirs will be kings survives
Banquo – key quotations and purpose :: 'instruments of darkness tell us truths' (1.3) ;; 'Merciful powers, / Restrain in me the cursed thoughts' (2.1) ;; 'I fear / Thou play'dst most foully for't' (3.1) ;; **Purpose:** a foil to Macbeth showing temptation can be resisted; flattering to James I, who claimed descent from Banquo
## Duncan
Duncan – role and summary :: The good, generous King of Scotland; trusting to a fault – he trusted the first Thane of Cawdor and now trusts Macbeth ('There's no art / To find the mind's construction in the face'); murdered in his sleep in Act 2
Duncan – Shakespeare's purpose :: Represents the ideal, divinely appointed king; his virtues ('plead like angels') make the murder more horrifying; the unnatural events after his death show regicide disrupts the Great Chain of Being
## Macduff, Lady Macduff and Malcolm
Macduff – role and summary :: Thane of Fife who discovers Duncan's body, suspects Macbeth, refuses to attend his coronation and flees to England; his family is slaughtered; he grieves deeply ('feel it as a man') and kills Macbeth, being 'from his mother's womb / Untimely ripped'
Macduff – key quotations and purpose :: 'O horror, horror, horror!' (2.3) ;; 'Bleed, bleed, poor country!' (4.3) ;; 'He has no children' (4.3) ;; 'I must also feel it as a man' (4.3) ;; **Purpose:** a loyal patriot and avenger who represents good; redefines masculinity to include feeling; fulfils the witches' prophecy
Lady Macduff – role and purpose :: Appears only in 4.2 with her son; she criticises Macduff for leaving them ('He loves us not') before they are murdered ;; **Purpose:** her innocence and the child's death show the depths of Macbeth's tyranny; a contrast to Lady Macbeth as a loving mother
Malcolm – role and summary :: Duncan's elder son and heir; flees to England after the murder, raises an army, tests Macduff's loyalty in 4.3 by pretending to be wicked, and becomes king at the end, calling the Macbeths 'this dead butcher and his fiend-like queen'
Malcolm – Shakespeare's purpose :: Represents rightful kingship and the restoration of order; his list of 'king-becoming graces' (justice, verity, temperance…) in 4.3 defines a good king – the opposite of Macbeth
## Other characters
The Witches (Weird Sisters) – role and purpose :: Supernatural figures who speak in riddling trochaic chants; they prophesy but never tell Macbeth to murder; in Act 4 their apparitions give him false confidence ;; **Purpose:** embody evil and chaos, play on Jacobean fears of witchcraft, and raise the question of fate vs free will
Ross – role :: A Scottish nobleman who acts as a messenger: brings news of Macbeth's new title (1.3), describes the unnatural events after Duncan's death (2.4), and tells Macduff his family has been murdered (4.3)
The Porter – role and purpose :: A drunken gatekeeper in 2.3 who imagines he is the porter of hell-gate, welcoming sinners including an 'equivocator' ;; **Purpose:** comic relief after the murder; ironically, Macbeth's castle has become hell; links to the Gunpowder Plot trial (Father Garnet's equivocation)
Donalbain – role :: Duncan's younger son, who flees to Ireland after the murder, warning 'There's daggers in men's smiles' – the flight of both sons briefly casts suspicion on them
Hecate – role :: Goddess of witchcraft who scolds the witches in 3.5 for dealing with Macbeth without her; the scene may have been added by Thomas Middleton and is often cut
The Doctor and the Gentlewoman – role :: Witnesses of Lady Macbeth's sleepwalking in 5.1; the Doctor says she needs 'the divine' more than a physician – her illness is spiritual guilt
Seyton and Siward – roles :: **Seyton** – Macbeth's servant who reports Lady Macbeth's death (5.5) ;; **Siward** – English general leading the army for Malcolm; his son Young Siward is killed by Macbeth

# An Inspector Calls – plot by act
An Inspector Calls – where and when is the play set? :: One evening in spring 1912, in the dining room of Arthur Birling's large suburban house in Brumley, 'an industrial city in the North Midlands' ;; the whole play takes place in this one room in real time (unity of time and place)
An Inspector Calls Act 1 (before the Inspector) – what happens? :: The Birlings and Gerald Croft celebrate Sheila's engagement to Gerald, son of Birling's business rival Sir George Croft ;; Birling makes speeches dismissing war and praising the Titanic, hopes for a knighthood, and lectures Gerald and Eric that 'a man has to mind his own business' – then the doorbell rings
An Inspector Calls Act 1 (Mr Birling and Sheila) – what happens? :: Inspector Goole reports that a young woman, Eva Smith, has died in the Infirmary after swallowing disinfectant ;; Birling admits he sacked her from his works in 1910 for leading a strike for higher wages ;; Sheila admits she had Eva sacked from Milwards, a dress shop, out of jealousy and bad temper ;; Gerald reacts to the name Daisy Renton – Act 1 ends on the Inspector's 'Well?'
An Inspector Calls Act 2 (Gerald) – what happens? :: Gerald admits he met Daisy Renton (Eva's new name) at the Palace bar in spring 1911, rescued her from Alderman Meggarty, installed her in a friend's flat as his mistress, and ended the affair that autumn ;; Sheila returns the engagement ring; Gerald goes out for a walk
An Inspector Calls Act 2 (Mrs Birling) – what happens? :: Mrs Birling admits that, two weeks earlier, a pregnant girl calling herself 'Mrs Birling' asked her charity for help and she persuaded the committee to refuse ;; she insists the father is to blame and should be made an example of – Sheila realises the father is Eric; Act 2 ends as Eric returns
An Inspector Calls Act 3 (Eric and the Inspector's exit) – what happens? :: Eric admits he met Eva at the Palace bar, forced his way into her lodgings while drunk, got her pregnant and gave her money he stole from his father's office; she refused to take stolen money or to marry him ;; the Inspector says each of them helped to kill her, gives his 'fire and blood and anguish' speech and leaves
An Inspector Calls Act 3 (after the Inspector) – what happens? :: The family argue; Gerald returns – there is no Inspector Goole on the police force (confirmed by phoning the Chief Constable) and the Infirmary has had no suicide ;; Birling, Mrs Birling and Gerald relax and celebrate; Sheila and Eric still feel responsible ;; the phone rings: a girl has just died on the way to the Infirmary and a police inspector is coming
How is An Inspector Calls structured? :: Three acts in one continuous evening; the Inspector questions one character at a time ('one line of enquiry at a time') in the order of Eva's life, creating a chain of events ;; each act ends on a cliffhanger ;; a final twist creates a cyclical structure
What genres does An Inspector Calls combine? :: **Well-made play** – tight plot, secrets revealed, climax ;; **detective thriller / whodunnit** – but everyone is guilty ;; **morality play** – the characters reflect sins (Birling greed, Sheila envy, Gerald lust, Eric drink/lust, Mrs Birling pride) and the Inspector judges them ;; **ghost story** – the mysterious 'Goole'

# An Inspector Calls – characters
## Mr Birling
Mr Birling – role and summary :: Arthur Birling, wealthy factory owner, former Lord Mayor and magistrate, hoping for a knighthood; a 'heavy-looking, rather portentous man'; he sacked Eva for striking and refuses responsibility throughout
Mr Birling – how does he develop? :: He doesn't: confident and pompous in Act 1, increasingly defensive, then worried about scandal and his knighthood; once the Inspector is 'exposed' he laughs it off and mocks 'the famous younger generation' – he has learned nothing
Mr Birling – key short quotations :: 'a hard-headed, practical man of business' ;; 'unsinkable, absolutely unsinkable' ;; 'lower costs and higher prices' ;; 'I can't accept any responsibility' ;; 'I'd give thousands' ;; 'Probably a Socialist or some sort of crank'
Mr Birling – Priestley's purpose :: To represent and ridicule selfish capitalism and the complacent older generation; dramatic irony makes him look foolish so the audience rejects his views and accepts the Inspector's socialist message
## Mrs Birling
Mrs Birling – role and summary :: Sybil Birling, 'a rather cold woman and her husband's social superior'; prominent in the Brumley Women's Charity Organisation; she refused Eva help when she was pregnant and desperate
Mrs Birling – how does she develop? :: Snobbish and controlling throughout; she denies knowing the photo, is shocked when Sheila and Eric challenge her, unknowingly blames her own son, briefly collapses when the truth comes out, then recovers her complacency – 'I was the only one of you who didn't give in'
Mrs Birling – key short quotations :: 'Girls of that class –' ;; 'she had only herself to blame' ;; 'Go and look for the father of the child' ;; 'I accept no blame for it at all' ;; 'a girl of that sort would ever refuse money'
Mrs Birling – Priestley's purpose :: To attack upper-class snobbery, hypocrisy and charity that judges the poor; she represents the old generation's refusal to change, and her condemnation of Eric is the play's most powerful dramatic irony
## Sheila
Sheila – role and summary :: Sheila Birling, 'a pretty girl in her early twenties, very pleased with life'; she had Eva sacked from Milwards out of jealousy; she is the character most changed by the Inspector
Sheila – how does she develop? :: From a spoilt, childish girl ('Mummy') to a mature, perceptive young woman: she feels immediate remorse, sees through the Inspector's method, returns the engagement ring, challenges her parents, and refuses to pretend nothing happened at the end
Sheila – key short quotations :: 'these girls aren't cheap labour – they're people' ;; 'I'll never, never do it again' ;; 'he knows' ;; 'build up a kind of wall' ;; 'You're just beginning to pretend all over again' ;; 'It frightens me the way you talk'
Sheila – Priestley's purpose :: To show that the young can learn and change – she becomes a voice for the Inspector's ideas and represents hope for a fairer post-war society; also shows women gaining independence of thought
## Eric
Eric – role and summary :: Eric Birling, 'in his early twenties, not quite at ease, half shy, half assertive'; a heavy drinker who forced himself on Eva, got her pregnant and stole money for her
Eric – how does he develop? :: From awkward, drunken and resentful to accepting responsibility; he condemns his mother ('you killed her') and father, and with Sheila refuses to forget what they did – though his actions remain shocking
Eric – key short quotations :: 'Why shouldn't they try for higher wages?' ;; 'You're not the kind of father a chap could go to' ;; 'my child – your own grandchild' ;; 'what we all did to her that matters'
Eric – Priestley's purpose :: To show the damage done by a cold, status-obsessed family and the abuse of working-class women by privileged men; his change shows the younger generation's capacity for responsibility
## Gerald
Gerald – role and summary :: Gerald Croft, 'an attractive chap about thirty', son of Sir George Croft; engaged to Sheila; he kept Eva (Daisy Renton) as his mistress for some months then ended it
Gerald – how does he develop? :: Seems decent and shows some genuine feeling for Daisy, but he sides with Birling, investigates the Inspector, and at the end offers Sheila the ring back – he returns to his old self, like the older generation
Gerald – key short quotations :: 'We're respectable citizens and not criminals' ;; 'young and pretty and warm-hearted' ;; 'I didn't feel about her as she felt about me' ;; 'Everything's all right now, Sheila'
Gerald – Priestley's purpose :: Represents the upper class and aristocracy; between the generations in age but sides with the old; shows how privileged men exploit working-class women while appearing charming and respectable
## The Inspector and Eva
Inspector Goole – role and summary :: A mysterious police inspector who arrives as Birling dismisses community; he controls the evening, reveals each character's part in Eva's death, delivers a final speech about collective responsibility, and is later found not to exist
Inspector Goole – key short quotations :: 'Public men… have responsibilities as well as privileges' ;; 'one line of enquiry at a time' ;; 'Each of you helped to kill her' ;; 'We are members of one body' ;; 'fire and blood and anguish'
Inspector Goole – Priestley's purpose and interpretations :: Priestley's mouthpiece for socialism; also interpreted as a ghost ('ghoul'), a conscience, an avenging angel or God-like judge who seems to know the future ;; his identity doesn't matter – what matters is whether the characters (and audience) learn
Eva Smith / Daisy Renton – role and purpose :: Never seen on stage; a working-class young woman exploited by each character ;; her common name makes her represent 'millions and millions' of powerless working people ;; we cannot be sure she was one person – but the Birlings' guilt is real either way
Edna – role :: The Birlings' parlourmaid; she announces the Inspector ;; a silent reminder of the working-class people who serve the family

## Context and techniques
What was Britain like in 1912 (the setting)? :: Edwardian era; rigid class system; huge gap between rich and poor; no welfare state; women could not vote (suffragettes campaigning); growing trade unions and strikes ;; the upper classes felt secure – before the Titanic, WW1 and the collapse of the old order
What was Britain like in 1945 (when the play was written)? :: After two world wars that broke down class barriers (rich and poor fought and worked together) ;; Labour won a landslide in 1945 and created the welfare state (based on the 1942 Beveridge Report) and the NHS (1948) ;; Priestley wanted audiences to vote for and support this new society
Who was J B Priestley? :: Born 1894 in Bradford; served in WW1; novelist, playwright and broadcaster ;; his WW2 radio 'Postscripts' were hugely popular but cancelled for being too left-wing ;; he co-founded the Common Wealth Party (1942) and campaigned for social justice
How does Priestley present gender in An Inspector Calls? :: Eva is exploited by men (Birling, Gerald, Eric) and judged by women of higher class (Sheila, Mrs Birling) ;; Mrs Birling expects Sheila to accept men's priorities; Sheila grows into an independent voice ;; Edwardian women had few rights – by 1945 their war work had changed attitudes
How does Priestley present age and the generations? :: The older generation (Mr and Mrs Birling, and Gerald) refuse to change; the younger (Sheila and Eric) learn and accept responsibility ;; Priestley places his hope for the future in the young
How does Priestley use the Inspector's entrances and exits? :: The Inspector arrives just as Birling says 'a man has to mind his own business' ;; characters enter at dramatic moments (Eric returns just as his mother condemns the father) ;; precise timing creates tension and irony
How does Priestley use stage directions in An Inspector Calls? :: Lighting changes from 'pink and intimate' to 'brighter and harder'; character descriptions establish personalities; adverbs guide delivery ('massively', 'bitterly', 'cutting in') ;; the Inspector's photograph shown to one person at a time creates mystery
Why is the time gap between 1912 and 1945 so important? :: It creates dramatic irony: the audience knows Birling is wrong about the Titanic and war, and that the 'fire and blood and anguish' came true ;; it asks a 1945 audience whether they will make the same mistakes again

# Of Mice and Men – plot by chapter
Of Mice and Men Chapter 1 – what happens? :: Thursday evening, a peaceful clearing by the Salinas River near Soledad ;; George and Lennie, migrant workers, camp before starting at a new ranch, having fled Weed after Lennie grabbed a girl's dress ;; George takes a dead mouse from Lennie, complains about him, then tells the dream of their own farm with rabbits; he tells Lennie to hide in the brush by the river if there's trouble
Of Mice and Men Chapter 2 – what happens? :: Friday morning, the bunkhouse ;; Candy shows them in; the boss is suspicious that George speaks for Lennie ;; Curley, the boss's aggressive son, picks on Lennie; Curley's wife appears 'looking for Curley' and George warns Lennie to keep away ;; Slim and Carlson are introduced; Slim's dog has puppies
Of Mice and Men Chapter 3 – what happens? :: Friday evening, the bunkhouse ;; George tells Slim about Weed and their friendship ;; Carlson shoots Candy's old dog ;; Candy overhears the dream and offers his savings – the dream suddenly seems possible ;; Curley attacks Lennie, who crushes Curley's hand; Slim makes Curley say it was caught in a machine
Of Mice and Men Chapter 4 – what happens? :: Saturday night, Crooks's room in the harness room ;; Lennie visits; Crooks cruelly suggests George might not come back, then reveals his loneliness ;; Candy joins and Crooks briefly asks to be part of the dream ;; Curley's wife comes in, mocks them and threatens Crooks with lynching; Crooks withdraws his request
Of Mice and Men Chapter 5 – what happens? :: Sunday afternoon, the barn ;; Lennie has accidentally killed his puppy ;; Curley's wife confides her lost dreams and lets Lennie stroke her hair; when she panics he holds on, breaks her neck and flees ;; Candy and George find the body; George knows the dream is over ;; Curley leads a lynch mob; Carlson's Luger is missing
Of Mice and Men Chapter 6 – what happens? :: Sunday evening, back at the clearing ;; a heron eats a water snake ;; Lennie hallucinates Aunt Clara and a giant rabbit scolding him ;; George arrives with Carlson's gun, tells the dream one last time and shoots Lennie in the back of the head ;; the men arrive; Slim comforts George and leads him away; Carlson doesn't understand
How does the time scheme of Of Mice and Men add to its effect? :: The story lasts only from Thursday evening to Sunday evening – the speed shows how quickly hope is destroyed; each chapter is a single scene in one setting, like a play
What is the significance of the novella's opening and closing settings? :: Both are at the pool in the clearing, first described as a peaceful, natural paradise ;; in Chapter 6 the same place contains predators (the heron eating the snake) and shadows – innocence has been destroyed; the cyclical structure shows nothing has changed for men like George
What examples of foreshadowing are there in Of Mice and Men? :: The dead mouse and the puppy (Lennie kills what he pets) ;; what happened in Weed (the red dress) ;; Candy's dog shot in the back of the head ;; George telling Lennie to hide in the brush ;; Lennie crushing Curley's hand ;; Crooks's 'nobody gets no land'

# Of Mice and Men – characters
## George
George – role and summary :: George Milton, 'small and quick, dark of face, with restless eyes'; Lennie's friend and protector, who travels with him and dreams of their own farm; he finally shoots Lennie to save him from Curley's lynch mob
George – how does he develop? :: Frustrated but loyal at the start; his hope grows when Candy joins the dream; after Curley's wife's death he admits 'I think I knowed we'd never do her'; at the end he loses both his friend and his dream and is left to the lonely life of an ordinary ranch worker
George – key short quotations :: 'Guys like us… are the loneliest guys in the world' ;; 'I could get along so easy and so nice' ;; 'nicer to go around with a guy you know' ;; 'I think I knowed we'd never do her' ;; 'I ain't mad. I never been mad'
George – Steinbeck's purpose :: To show the value of friendship and the painful choices forced on the powerless; his loyalty contrasts with the loneliness of other men, and his sacrifice is the tragic climax – the death of the dream
## Lennie
Lennie – role and summary :: Lennie Small (an ironic name), 'a huge man, shapeless of face'; enormously strong, with a learning disability; loves soft things and the dream of tending rabbits; accidentally kills mice, his puppy and Curley's wife
Lennie – how does he develop? :: He doesn't really change – childlike and dependent throughout ;; his strength becomes more dangerous (mouse → puppy → Curley's hand → Curley's wife) ;; his innocence makes him a tragic victim of a world with no place for him
Lennie – key short quotations :: 'I remember about the rabbits, George' ;; 'live off the fatta the lan'' ;; 'I got you to look after me' ;; 'I like to pet nice things' ;; 'I done another bad thing'
Lennie – Steinbeck's purpose :: Represents the vulnerable and powerless in society – innocent but destructive; animal imagery (bear, horse, 'paw') shows his instinctive nature; his death shows a harsh society destroys those who are different
## Candy
Candy – role and summary :: An old swamper (cleaner) who lost a hand in a ranch accident; owns an old dog that Carlson shoots; offers his savings ($350) to join George and Lennie's dream
Candy – key quotations and purpose :: 'I ought to of shot that dog myself' ;; 'When they can me here' ;; 'I ain't much good with on'y one hand' ;; **Purpose:** represents the old and disabled, discarded when no longer useful; his dog foreshadows Lennie's death, and his hope shows how much the dream means to the powerless
## Crooks
Crooks – role and summary :: The black stable buck, named for his crooked back (kicked by a horse); forced to live alone in the harness room because of segregation; proud, bitter, well-read but deeply lonely
Crooks – key quotations and purpose :: 'I can't play because I'm black' ;; 'A guy needs somebody' ;; 'You got no right to come in my room' ;; 'Nobody never gets to heaven' ;; **Purpose:** exposes racism in 1930s America; his brief hope and humiliation by Curley's wife show how prejudice crushes the powerless
## Curley and Curley's wife
Curley – role and summary :: The boss's son, a small, aggressive former boxer, jealous and newly married; he picks fights with bigger men, has his hand crushed by Lennie, and leads the lynch mob ;; **Purpose:** represents abusive power that comes from status, not respect – a contrast to Slim
Curley's wife – role and summary :: Never named – seen as Curley's possession; flirts with the men out of loneliness and is called a 'tramp' and 'tart'; she dreamed of being in the movies; Lennie accidentally kills her
Curley's wife – how is she presented as both villain and victim? :: **Temptress/threat:** red clothes, flirting, threatens Crooks with lynching ;; **Victim:** lonely ('I get lonely'), trapped in an unhappy marriage, mistreated by the men, her dreams crushed ;; in death she looks 'very pretty and simple' – Steinbeck builds sympathy
Curley's wife – key quotations and purpose :: 'I'm lookin' for Curley' ;; 'I could get you strung up on a tree' ;; 'Coulda been in the movies' ;; 'I don't like Curley' ;; **Purpose:** shows the limited lives of women in the 1930s and the loneliness that affects everyone on the ranch
## Slim, Carlson and others
Slim – role and summary :: The jerkline skinner, 'prince of the ranch', respected for his skill, calm and wisdom; he understands George's friendship, approves shooting Candy's dog and comforts George at the end ;; **Purpose:** represents natural authority and compassion
Carlson – role and summary :: A powerful, insensitive ranch worker who shoots Candy's dog with his Luger – the gun George later uses on Lennie ;; his final line shows he cannot understand grief or friendship ;; **Purpose:** represents the harsh, unfeeling attitudes of the ranch
The boss and Whit – roles :: **The boss** – Curley's father, suspicious of George speaking for Lennie; shows workers are distrusted and controlled ;; **Whit** – a young ranch hand who shows George a letter in a pulp magazine, showing the men's longing to be noticed
Aunt Clara – role :: Lennie's late aunt, who looked after him before George; she gave him mice to pet; in Chapter 6 Lennie hallucinates her scolding him – his guilty conscience

# Poetry anthology – comparison
Compare 'Piano' and 'Poem at Thirty-Nine' on parents. :: **Similar:** both adults remember a parent through a shared activity (music, cooking) and feel loss ;; **Different:** Lawrence is overwhelmed and regresses ('weep like a child'), using regular couplets and long lines; Walker is grateful and proud, using free verse with short lines, ending in confident independence
Compare 'Do not go gentle' and 'Remember' on death. :: **Similar:** both address a loved one about death using imperatives and strict forms (villanelle, sonnet) ;; **Different:** Thomas demands defiance ('Rage, rage') from a dying father; Rossetti, the one dying, accepts death calmly and finally lets go ('forget and smile') – rage vs acceptance
Compare 'If–' and 'Do not go gentle' on advice between generations. :: **Similar:** both are built on repeated instructions/conditions in regular forms ;; **Different:** a father advises a son to be calm and stoical (Kipling), while a son urges a father to rage (Thomas); Kipling ends with a reward ('you'll be a Man'), Thomas with a desperate plea
Compare 'War Photographer' and 'Blessing' on suffering. :: **Similar:** both present hardship in poorer countries and challenge comfortable readers; both use religious language ('Mass', 'priest' / 'congregation', 'blessing') ;; **Different:** Duffy's mood is bitter – the West ignores suffering; Dharker's ending is joyful but ironic – the water is a brief, accidental gift, and the children's poverty remains
Compare 'Search For My Tongue' and 'Half-caste' on identity. :: **Similar:** both explore identity through language, address 'you' directly and include non-standard language (Gujarati, Creole) ;; **Different:** Bhatt is reflective and fears loss, ending in celebration; Agard is confrontational, humorous and sarcastic, ending with a challenge to the listener
Compare 'My Last Duchess' and 'La Belle Dame sans Merci' on power in relationships. :: **Similar:** both show destructive relationships and use a framing listener ;; **Different:** the Duke has total power over his wife and silences her (dramatic monologue, couplets); in Keats's ballad the woman has power over the knight, who is left pale and enslaved
Compare 'Sonnet 116' and 'My Last Duchess' on love. :: **Similar:** both use iambic pentameter and a confident male speaker ;; **Different:** Shakespeare presents ideal love as constant and selfless ('ever-fixed mark'); the Duke's 'love' is possessive and jealous, ending in murder – a sinister contrast
Compare 'Half-past Two' and 'Hide and Seek' on childhood. :: **Similar:** both show a child alone, forgotten by others, and a moment outside normal time ;; **Different:** Fanthorpe's boy escapes into a magical 'clockless land' and the memory is positive; Scannell's child ends abandoned and disillusioned in the dark – loss of innocence
Compare 'Prayer Before Birth' and 'The Tyger' on creation and evil. :: **Similar:** both address a higher power and use repetition and rhetorical structures ;; **Different:** MacNeice's unborn child fears human evil and pleads for protection; Blake's speaker marvels at the terrifying beauty of God's creation and questions how evil can exist
Compare 'Piano' and 'Half-past Two' on memory. :: **Similar:** both are adults looking back on childhood moments in which time seems to stop ;; **Different:** Lawrence's memory overwhelms him painfully in the present; Fanthorpe's memory is gently humorous and treasured
Compare 'Sonnet 116' and 'Remember' on love and time. :: **Similar:** both sonnets present love that goes beyond time and death ;; **Different:** Shakespeare's love is unchanging and certain; Rossetti's love changes at the volta – true love wants the other's happiness even if it means being forgotten
Compare 'Prayer Before Birth' and 'Half-caste' on being dehumanised. :: **Similar:** both resist being reduced by society – to 'a cog in a machine' or to 'half' a person ;; **Different:** MacNeice's tone is fearful and desperate, ending with 'Otherwise kill me'; Agard's is witty and defiant, ending with an invitation

# Exam technique – set text essay plans
What might an Of Mice and Men question look like, and how would you plan it? :: e.g. 'How does Steinbeck present loneliness?' ;; Plan: (1) itinerant workers' isolation – George Ch1 ;; (2) Crooks and racism – Ch4 ;; (3) Curley's wife and gender – Ch2/5 ;; (4) George alone at the end – Ch6 ;; weave in context (Depression, segregation, women's roles) for AO4 throughout
What might an An Inspector Calls question look like, and how would you plan it? :: e.g. 'How does Priestley present Sheila as a character who changes?' ;; Plan: (1) spoilt and naïve in Act 1 ;; (2) immediate remorse and insight ;; (3) challenges parents and Gerald ;; (4) refuses to forget at the end ;; link to Priestley's purpose, the younger generation and 1912/1945 context (AO4)
What might a Macbeth question look like, and how would you plan it? :: e.g. 'Explore how Shakespeare presents the relationship between Macbeth and Lady Macbeth' ;; Plan: (1) close partnership, she dominates (1.5–1.7) ;; (2) shared guilt after the murder (2.2) ;; (3) drifting apart – he excludes her (3.2–3.4) ;; (4) isolation and death (5.1, 5.5) ;; focus on language, form and structure (AO2) with short quotes analysed closely
How should you begin a set-text essay? :: With a direct answer to the question (a thesis) that names the writer's purpose, e.g. 'Steinbeck presents loneliness as an inescapable part of the migrant workers' lives, using characters on the margins of society to criticise 1930s America' ;; no long background paragraphs
How should you end a set-text essay? :: Return to the question and the writer's overall message; show how your points connect (e.g. how the character or theme changes from beginning to end) and what the audience/reader should learn ;; avoid repeating paragraphs or adding new quotations

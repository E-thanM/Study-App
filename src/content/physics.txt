# 1 Forces and motion
What are the units of mass, length, time, force, velocity, acceleration, momentum and energy? :: kg, m, s, N, m/s, m/s², kg m/s, J
Write the equation for average speed. :: average speed = distance moved ÷ time taken
Write the equation for acceleration. :: acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time taken ;; a = (v − u) ÷ t
Write the equation linking v, u, a and s. :: v² = u² + 2as
What does the gradient of a distance–time graph show? :: Speed
What does the gradient of a velocity–time graph show? :: Acceleration
What does the area under a velocity–time graph show? :: Distance travelled
What is the difference between speed and velocity? :: Velocity is speed in a given direction (vector); speed is scalar
What is the difference between a scalar and a vector? :: Scalar – magnitude only (mass, speed, energy, distance) ;; Vector – magnitude and direction (force, velocity, acceleration, momentum, displacement)
What is a resultant force? :: The single force that has the same effect as all the forces acting on an object
What is friction? :: A force that opposes motion between surfaces in contact
State Newton's first law. :: An object stays at rest or moves at constant velocity unless a resultant force acts on it
Write the equation for Newton's second law. :: force = mass × acceleration ;; F = m × a
Write the equation for weight. :: weight = mass × gravitational field strength ;; W = m × g (g = 10 N/kg on Earth)
What is the stopping distance of a vehicle? :: Thinking distance + braking distance
Give factors affecting thinking distance. :: Speed; reaction time (tiredness, alcohol, drugs, distractions)
Give factors affecting braking distance. :: Speed, mass, condition of brakes and tyres, road surface (wet/icy)
Describe the motion of a falling object reaching terminal velocity. :: Initially accelerates at g; air resistance increases with speed; eventually air resistance = weight, resultant force zero, constant (terminal) velocity
Describe an experiment to investigate the motion of falling objects. :: Drop e.g. cupcake cases from a height and use light gates/video to measure speed over time, or time falls with different numbers of cases
What is Hooke's law? :: The extension of a spring is directly proportional to the force applied, provided the limit of proportionality is not exceeded
Describe how to investigate extension of a spring. :: Hang masses on a spring, measure extension with a ruler (at eye level) each time, plot force vs extension – straight line through origin up to the limit of proportionality
What is elastic behaviour? :: An object returns to its original shape when the force is removed
Write the equation for momentum. :: momentum = mass × velocity ;; p = m × v
State the principle of conservation of momentum. :: In a closed system, total momentum before = total momentum after a collision/explosion
Write the equation linking force and momentum. :: force = change in momentum ÷ time taken
Explain how crumple zones, seat belts and airbags reduce injury. :: They increase the time taken for the change in momentum, so the force on passengers is smaller
State Newton's third law. :: When two bodies interact, the forces they exert on each other are equal in size and opposite in direction
Write the equation for the moment of a force. :: moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot
State the principle of moments. :: For an object in equilibrium, the sum of clockwise moments equals the sum of anticlockwise moments
What is the centre of gravity? :: The point through which the weight of an object can be considered to act
How do upward forces on a beam change as a weight moves along it? :: The support nearer the weight carries a larger upward force

# 2 Electricity
What is the difference between AC and DC? :: AC – direction of current continually reverses (mains); DC – flows in one direction only (cells/batteries)
What is the UK mains supply? :: 230 V AC, 50 Hz
What does the earth wire do? :: Connects metal case to earth – if the live wire touches the case, a large current flows to earth and blows the fuse
How does a fuse work? :: Thin wire that melts if current is too high, breaking the circuit
How does a circuit breaker work? :: Switch that trips (opens) when the current is too large; can be reset
Why do appliances with plastic cases not need an earth wire? :: They are double insulated
Why does current cause heating? :: Electrons collide with ions in the wire, transferring energy which heats it
Write the equation for power using current and voltage. :: power = current × voltage ;; P = I × V
Write the equation for energy transferred using current, voltage and time. :: energy transferred = current × voltage × time ;; E = I × V × t
Why are lights in houses connected in parallel? :: Each gets the full voltage; can be switched independently; if one breaks the others stay on
How does current vary with voltage for a resistor, filament lamp and diode? :: Resistor – straight line (constant R) ;; Filament lamp – curve; resistance increases as it gets hotter ;; Diode – current flows in one direction only
How does the resistance of an LDR change? :: Decreases as light intensity increases
How does the resistance of a thermistor change? :: Decreases as temperature increases (NTC)
Write Ohm's law. :: voltage = current × resistance ;; V = I × R
How do current and voltage behave in series circuits? :: Current is the same everywhere; voltage is shared between components; total R = R₁ + R₂
How do current and voltage behave in parallel circuits? :: Voltage is the same across each branch; current splits between branches; total current = sum of branch currents
What is electric current? :: The rate of flow of charge
Write the equation linking charge, current and time. :: charge = current × time ;; Q = I × t
What carries current in metals? :: Negatively charged electrons
What is voltage (potential difference)? :: The energy transferred per unit charge passed
Write the equation linking energy, charge and voltage. :: energy transferred = charge × voltage ;; E = Q × V
Describe how to investigate how current varies with voltage for a component. :: Set up circuit with ammeter in series and voltmeter in parallel, use variable resistor to change V; record I; reverse the supply for negative values; plot I–V graph
How do insulators become charged by friction? :: Electrons are transferred from one material to another – the one gaining electrons becomes negative
How do you test if an object is charged? :: Bring it near small pieces of paper or a charged rod – attraction/repulsion
Give dangers and uses of electrostatic charges. :: Dangers: sparks igniting fuel (refuelling aircraft), lightning ;; Uses: inkjet printers, photocopiers, paint spraying

# 3 Waves
What is the difference between transverse and longitudinal waves? :: Transverse – vibrations perpendicular to direction of energy transfer (light, water) ;; Longitudinal – vibrations parallel (sound), with compressions and rarefactions
Define amplitude, wavefront, frequency, wavelength and period. :: Amplitude – max displacement from rest ;; Wavefront – line joining points in phase ;; Frequency – waves per second (Hz) ;; Wavelength – distance between adjacent crests ;; Period – time for one wave
Write the wave speed equation. :: wave speed = frequency × wavelength ;; v = f × λ
Write the equation linking frequency and period. :: frequency = 1 ÷ time period ;; f = 1/T
What do waves transfer? :: Energy and information, without transferring matter
What is the Doppler effect? :: When a wave source moves, waves are compressed in front (higher frequency) and stretched behind (lower frequency)
What happens when waves are reflected and refracted? :: Reflection – bounce off a surface ;; Refraction – change speed and direction when entering a different medium
List the electromagnetic spectrum in order of increasing frequency. :: Radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays
What do all EM waves have in common? :: Transverse; travel at the same speed in a vacuum (3 × 10⁸ m/s)
Uses of radio waves and microwaves? :: Radio – broadcasting, communications ;; Microwaves – cooking, satellite transmissions
Uses of infrared and visible light? :: Infrared – heaters, night vision, remote controls ;; Visible – optical fibres, photography
Uses of UV, X-rays and gamma rays? :: UV – fluorescent lamps, sterilising, security marking ;; X-rays – medical imaging, airport security ;; Gamma – sterilising food and equipment, cancer treatment
Give the dangers of microwaves, infrared, UV and X/gamma rays. :: Microwaves – internal heating of body tissue ;; Infrared – skin burns ;; UV – damage to surface cells, eyes, skin cancer ;; X-rays and gamma – cell mutation, cancer
State the law of reflection. :: Angle of incidence = angle of reflection
Describe an experiment to investigate refraction in a glass block. :: Shine a ray box through a rectangular glass block, trace incident and emergent rays, draw normal, measure angles i and r with a protractor
Write the equation for refractive index. :: n = sin i ÷ sin r
What is total internal reflection? :: When light travels from a denser medium and the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle, all the light is reflected
Write the equation for the critical angle. :: sin c = 1 ÷ n
Give uses of total internal reflection. :: Optical fibres (communications, endoscopes) and prisms (periscopes, binoculars)
What is the frequency range of human hearing? :: 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz
How does the pitch and loudness of a sound relate to the wave? :: Higher frequency → higher pitch ;; Larger amplitude → louder
Describe how to measure the speed of sound in air. :: Two people a measured distance apart (e.g. 100 m): one makes a loud bang, the other times between seeing and hearing; speed = distance ÷ time ;; or use two microphones and an oscilloscope
How do you use an oscilloscope to find the frequency of a sound? :: Measure the time period from the trace (divisions × timebase), then f = 1/T

# 4 Energy resources and transfers
Name the energy stores. :: Chemical, kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic, thermal, magnetic, electrostatic, nuclear
Name the ways energy is transferred. :: Mechanically (by forces), electrically, by heating, by radiation (light and sound)
State the principle of conservation of energy. :: Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one store to another
Write the efficiency equation. :: efficiency = (useful energy output ÷ total energy input) × 100%
What does a Sankey diagram show? :: Energy transfers – width of arrows proportional to the amount of energy
Explain conduction. :: Vibrating particles pass on kinetic energy to neighbours; in metals free electrons also transfer energy quickly
Explain convection. :: Fluid heated becomes less dense and rises; cooler denser fluid sinks to replace it – a convection current
Explain radiation (thermal). :: Transfer of energy by infrared waves; no medium needed
Which surfaces are best at emitting and absorbing infrared? :: Dull black surfaces; shiny silver surfaces are the worst (best reflectors)
How can heat loss from a house be reduced? :: Loft insulation (trapped air), cavity wall insulation, double glazing, draught excluders, carpets
Write the equation for work done. :: work done = force × distance moved ;; W = F × d
Write the equation for gravitational potential energy. :: GPE = mass × g × height ;; GPE = m × g × h
Write the equation for kinetic energy. :: KE = ½ × mass × speed² ;; KE = ½mv²
Write the equation for power. :: power = work done ÷ time taken ;; P = W ÷ t
How is electricity generated in a thermal power station? :: Fuel burned (or nuclear fission) heats water to steam → steam turns turbine → turbine turns generator
Give advantages and disadvantages of fossil fuels. :: Reliable, high energy output, relatively cheap ;; Non-renewable, release CO₂ (global warming) and SO₂ (acid rain)
Give advantages and disadvantages of nuclear power. :: No CO₂, reliable, lots of energy from small mass of fuel ;; Radioactive waste, expensive to build/decommission, risk of accidents
Give advantages and disadvantages of wind power. :: Renewable, no pollution when running ;; Unreliable (needs wind), visual/noise pollution, many turbines needed
Give advantages and disadvantages of hydroelectric power. :: Renewable, reliable, can respond quickly to demand ;; Floods valleys/habitats, expensive to build
Give advantages and disadvantages of tidal and wave power. :: Renewable, tides predictable ;; Expensive, affects habitats, wave power unreliable
Give advantages and disadvantages of solar power. :: Renewable, no pollution ;; Only works in daylight, low output, expensive per unit
How does geothermal energy work? :: Hot rocks underground heat water to steam to drive turbines; only possible in volcanic areas

# 5 Solids, liquids and gases
Write the equation for density. :: density = mass ÷ volume ;; ρ = m ÷ V
How do you measure the density of an irregular solid? :: Measure mass on a balance; find volume by displacement (eureka can or measuring cylinder); density = m ÷ V
Write the equation for pressure. :: pressure = force ÷ area ;; p = F ÷ A
What is the unit of pressure? :: Pascal (Pa) = N/m²
How does pressure act in a liquid or gas? :: Equally in all directions at a given depth
Write the equation for pressure difference in a fluid. :: pressure difference = height × density × g ;; p = h × ρ × g
What happens to temperature during a change of state? :: It stays constant – energy is used to break bonds between particles, not increase kinetic energy
What is specific heat capacity? :: The energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C
Write the equation for specific heat capacity. :: ΔQ = m × c × ΔT
How do you measure specific heat capacity of water? :: Heat a known mass of water with an electric heater (joulemeter or V × I × t), measure temperature rise; c = E ÷ (m × ΔT)
How does molecular motion explain gas pressure? :: Gas molecules collide with the walls, exerting a force; pressure = force ÷ area
What is absolute zero? :: −273 °C (0 K) – the temperature at which particles have the least kinetic energy
How do you convert between Celsius and kelvin? :: K = °C + 273
What is the relationship between Kelvin temperature and the kinetic energy of gas molecules? :: Average kinetic energy is proportional to Kelvin temperature
Write the equation linking pressure and Kelvin temperature (constant volume). :: p₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂
Write the equation linking pressure and volume (constant temperature) – Boyle's law. :: p₁V₁ = p₂V₂
Why does gas pressure increase when temperature increases (fixed volume)? :: Molecules move faster, hitting the walls harder and more often

# 6 Magnetism and electromagnetism
Which materials are magnetic? :: Iron, steel, nickel and cobalt
What is the difference between soft and hard magnetic materials? :: Soft (iron) – magnetised and demagnetised easily (electromagnet cores) ;; Hard (steel) – keep magnetism (permanent magnets)
How do magnets interact? :: Like poles repel, unlike poles attract
What is magnetic field direction? :: From north pole to south pole
How do you plot a magnetic field? :: Use a plotting compass and mark the direction of the needle at many points, or sprinkle iron filings
Describe the magnetic field around a straight current-carrying wire. :: Concentric circles around the wire; direction given by the right-hand grip rule
How can you make an electromagnet stronger? :: More turns of wire, larger current, iron core
Why does a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field experience a force? :: The magnetic field of the current interacts with the external field (motor effect)
How do you find the direction of the force on a wire (motor effect)? :: Fleming's left-hand rule: First finger – Field, seCond finger – Current, thuMb – Motion
How can you increase the force on a wire (motor effect)? :: Increase current, increase magnetic field strength (stronger magnet), longer wire in field
How does a DC motor work? :: Current in coil in magnetic field produces forces in opposite directions on each side → coil turns; split-ring commutator reverses current every half turn so it keeps rotating
How does a loudspeaker work? :: AC in a coil in a magnetic field makes the coil and cone vibrate, producing sound waves
What is electromagnetic induction? :: A voltage is induced in a conductor when it moves relative to a magnetic field (or the field changes)
How can the induced voltage be increased? :: Move the wire/magnet faster, use a stronger magnet, more turns on the coil
How does a generator (dynamo) work? :: Coil rotates in a magnetic field, inducing an alternating voltage; slip rings connect to the circuit (AC generator)
How does a transformer work? :: AC in primary coil creates a changing magnetic field in the iron core, which induces an alternating voltage in the secondary coil
Write the transformer equation. :: Vp ÷ Vs = Np ÷ Ns
Write the power equation for a 100% efficient transformer. :: Vp × Ip = Vs × Is
Why is electricity transmitted at high voltage? :: Step-up transformers increase voltage and reduce current, so less energy is lost as heat in the cables

# 6 Electromagnetic induction in depth
## Induction basics
C: A voltage is induced in a conductor when it {{c1::cuts}} magnetic field lines, or when the magnetic field through a coil {{c2::changes}}.
What are the two ways to induce a voltage in a coil? :: **1.** Move a magnet towards or away from the coil (or move the coil through a magnetic field) ;; **2.** Keep everything still but change the magnetic field through the coil, e.g. switch an electromagnet on or off, or vary its current
A bar magnet is held still inside a coil connected to a sensitive voltmeter. What does the meter show and why? :: Zero – there is no relative movement (and no changing field), so no voltage is induced
What happens to the meter when a bar magnet is pushed into a coil? :: The needle deflects briefly in one direction – a voltage is induced only while the magnet is moving
What happens to the meter when the magnet is pulled out of the coil again? :: The needle deflects in the opposite direction
What happens to the meter reading if the magnet is pushed in and then stops moving? :: It returns to zero straight away – the voltage lasts only while the magnetic field is changing
Why does a faster-moving magnet induce a larger voltage? :: The coil's wire cuts the magnetic field lines at a faster rate
List the ways to increase the size of an induced voltage. :: Move the magnet (or coil) faster ;; Use a stronger magnet ;; Use more turns of wire on the coil ;; Use a coil with a larger area ;; Add a soft iron core to concentrate the field
Why does using more turns of wire increase the induced voltage? :: A voltage is induced in every turn and the voltages add together, because the turns are connected in series
Why does a soft iron core increase the induced voltage? :: The iron concentrates the magnetic field lines through the coil, so the field changes more strongly
How can you reverse the direction of the induced voltage? :: Reverse the direction of movement (push in instead of pull out), or swap the poles of the magnet ;; Doing both at once leaves it unchanged
What kind of voltage is induced if a magnet is moved in and out of a coil repeatedly? :: An alternating voltage (AC) – it changes direction every time the movement reverses
A coil that is not part of a complete circuit has a magnet pushed into it. Is a voltage induced? Does a current flow? :: A voltage is still induced across its ends, but no current flows because the circuit is not complete
How can a voltage be induced in a single straight wire? :: Move the wire across a magnetic field (e.g. between the poles of a magnet) so that it cuts the field lines, with its ends connected to a sensitive meter
In which direction should a wire be moved through a magnetic field to induce the largest voltage? :: At right angles to the field lines. Moving it parallel to the field lines cuts none, so induces no voltage
Describe an investigation into the factors affecting induced voltage. :: Connect a coil to a voltmeter or data logger ;; Push a bar magnet into the coil at a controlled speed and record the maximum reading ;; Change only one variable (speed, magnet strength or number of turns), keep the others the same ;; Repeat and take an average
Compare the motor effect and electromagnetic induction. :: **Motor effect:** current + magnetic field → force and movement (electrical → kinetic) ;; **Induction:** movement + magnetic field → voltage and current (kinetic → electrical)
Name some uses of electromagnetic induction. :: Generators in power stations, wind turbines and bicycle dynamos ;; Transformers ;; Moving-coil microphones ;; Wireless phone chargers and induction hobs
How does a moving-coil microphone use electromagnetic induction? :: Sound waves make a diaphragm vibrate, moving a coil attached to it (or a magnet) relative to a magnet, which induces a small alternating voltage with the same pattern as the sound
Why is induced voltage described as a form of 'generator effect'? :: Because moving a conductor in a magnetic field generates electricity from kinetic energy – the opposite energy transfer to the motor effect
## Lenz's law and energy
State Lenz's law. :: The direction of an induced voltage (and current) is always such that it **opposes the change** that produces it
C: By Lenz's law, an induced current produces a magnetic field that {{c1::opposes}} the {{c2::change}} that caused it.
A north pole is pushed towards a coil. What pole forms at the end of the coil nearest the magnet? :: A north pole – it repels the approaching magnet, opposing the push
A north pole is pulled away from a coil. What pole forms at the end of the coil nearest the magnet? :: A south pole – it attracts the magnet, opposing its removal
How does Lenz's law show that energy is conserved? :: You must do work against the opposing force to move the magnet, and that work is transferred to electrical energy. If the induced current helped the movement, energy would be created from nothing, which is impossible
Why is it harder to push a magnet into a coil that is part of a complete circuit than into an open coil? :: In a complete circuit a current flows, and its magnetic field opposes the magnet's movement. In an open coil there is no current, so no opposing force
A strong magnet is dropped down a vertical copper tube and falls very slowly. Explain why. :: The moving magnet induces circulating currents in the copper ;; By Lenz's law their magnetic field opposes the magnet's motion ;; This gives an upward force, so the magnet falls more slowly than it would through air
Why would a magnet stop falling slowly if the copper tube had a long slit cut down its side? :: The slit stops the induced current flowing around the tube, so the opposing magnetic field is much weaker
A magnet is pushed faster into a coil. Why is more force needed? :: A bigger voltage and current are induced, so the opposing force is larger, and more work is done each second
What happens to the energy transferred when you push a magnet into a coil connected to a lamp? :: Your work (kinetic) → electrical energy in the coil → light and heat in the lamp
Fleming's right-hand rule (extra – not needed for Edexcel) :: Thumb = Motion of the wire, First finger = Field (N to S), Second finger = induced Current. It gives the direction of induced current in a wire moving through a field ;; In the exam use Lenz's law (opposes the change) to explain direction
## AC generators and dynamos
Name the main parts of a simple AC generator. :: A coil of wire (armature), a magnet or magnets (poles N and S), slip rings, carbon brushes, and connections to an external circuit
Explain how a simple AC generator produces a voltage. :: The coil is turned in the magnetic field, so its sides cut the field lines ;; This induces a voltage across the coil ;; The voltage is delivered to the circuit through slip rings and brushes
What is the job of the slip rings? :: To keep the rotating coil connected to the external circuit all the time, so the wires don't twist
What is the job of the carbon brushes? :: They press against the slip rings and carry current between the rotating coil and the fixed external circuit
Why are carbon brushes used? :: Carbon conducts electricity and is slippery (low friction), so it makes good contact without wearing the rings away too quickly
Why does the output of a simple generator alternate? :: Each side of the coil moves up through the field, then down. The direction in which it cuts the field lines reverses every half turn, so the induced voltage reverses
Describe the voltage–time graph for an AC generator. :: A repeating wave (sine curve) that goes positive, then negative ;; One complete cycle for each complete rotation of the coil
When is the voltage from an AC generator largest? :: When the coil is parallel to the field lines – its sides cut the lines at right angles and cut the most lines per second
When is the voltage from an AC generator zero? :: When the coil is perpendicular to the field lines (facing the magnet poles) – its sides move along the field lines and cut none
How is the frequency of the AC output related to the rotation of the coil? :: The frequency (Hz) equals the number of complete rotations per second – one rotation gives one cycle
What happens to the graph if the coil is spun faster? :: Higher peak voltage (lines cut faster) and a shorter period (higher frequency), so more cycles in a given time
What happens to the graph if the coil has more turns but spins at the same speed? :: The peak voltage is larger but the frequency is unchanged
List the ways to increase the output voltage of a generator. :: Spin the coil faster ;; Use stronger magnets ;; Use more turns on the coil ;; Use a coil with a larger area ;; Use a soft iron core
How does a DC generator differ from an AC generator? :: It uses a split-ring commutator instead of slip rings, so the output always flows in one direction (but rises and falls in pulses)
How does a bicycle dynamo light a lamp? :: The wheel turns a magnet next to a fixed coil, changing the magnetic field through the coil, which induces an alternating voltage that lights the lamp
Why does a bicycle lamp get brighter when you pedal faster and dim when you slow down? :: Faster rotation cuts the field lines more quickly, inducing a larger voltage and current. Slower rotation induces a smaller voltage; if you stop, no voltage is induced and the lamp goes out
Describe the energy transfers in a power station generator. :: Fuel (chemical or nuclear) → thermal → steam turns a turbine (kinetic) → generator → electrical energy (with some wasted as heat)
How do wind turbines and hydroelectric stations generate electricity? :: Moving wind or water turns a turbine, which turns a generator, using electromagnetic induction
## Transformers
What are the parts of a transformer? :: A primary coil, a secondary coil, and a laminated soft iron core that links the two coils
Explain how a transformer works, step by step. :: Alternating current in the primary coil produces a changing magnetic field ;; The iron core carries this changing field through the secondary coil ;; The changing field induces an alternating voltage across the secondary coil
Why must the supply to a transformer be AC? :: Only a changing current makes a changing magnetic field. With DC the field is steady, so no voltage is induced in the secondary coil (apart from a brief pulse when switched on or off)
Why is the core made of iron? :: Iron concentrates the magnetic field and guides it from the primary coil through the secondary coil
Why is soft iron used rather than steel? :: Soft iron is easy to magnetise and demagnetise, so it keeps up with the alternating field and wastes little energy
Why is the core laminated (made of thin insulated sheets)? :: To reduce eddy currents, which would heat the core and waste energy
What is a step-up transformer? :: A transformer that increases the voltage – the secondary coil has more turns than the primary
What is a step-down transformer? :: A transformer that decreases the voltage – the secondary coil has fewer turns than the primary
What does a step-up transformer do to the current? :: It reduces it: the power stays about the same, so if the voltage goes up the current goes down
Write the turns–voltage equation for a transformer. :: Vp ÷ Vs = Np ÷ Ns ;; (voltage across primary ÷ voltage across secondary = turns on primary ÷ turns on secondary)
Write the power equation for an ideal (100% efficient) transformer. :: Vp × Ip = Vs × Is ;; (power in = power out)
Write the equation for the efficiency of a transformer. :: efficiency = (power out ÷ power in) × 100%
Why are real transformers not 100% efficient? :: Heat in the coils (resistance) ;; Eddy currents in the core ;; Some magnetic field leaks and doesn't pass through the secondary ;; Energy used to keep magnetising and demagnetising the core
How are energy losses in transformers reduced? :: Thick, low-resistance copper wire ;; Laminated core to cut eddy currents ;; Soft iron core ;; Winding coils over each other on the same core to cut leakage
Where are transformers used in everyday life? :: Phone and laptop chargers (step down 230 V), power stations and the National Grid, substations, and mains adaptors for appliances
## Power transmission and the National Grid
Write the equation for electrical power. :: power = current × voltage ;; P = I × V
Write the equation for power wasted as heat in a cable. :: power loss = I² × R ;; (current squared × resistance of the cable)
Why is electricity transmitted at very high voltage? :: For the same power (P = IV), a high voltage means a low current. A low current means much less heating in the cables (power loss = I²R), so less energy is wasted
If the current in a cable is halved, what happens to the power wasted? :: It falls to one quarter, because power loss depends on I²
Why not simply use thicker cables with less resistance instead of high voltage? :: Thick cables are very expensive, heavy and need stronger pylons
Why is AC used for the National Grid? :: Its voltage can easily be stepped up and down with transformers (a DC supply cannot use a transformer)
Describe how electricity gets from a power station to a home. :: Generated at about 25 kV → step-up transformer to 275–400 kV → transmission lines (pylons) → step-down transformers at substations (132 kV, 33 kV, 11 kV) → 230 V to homes
Why is the voltage stepped down before reaching homes? :: High voltages are dangerous and unsuitable for household use. Appliances are designed for 230 V
What is the voltage and frequency of UK mains electricity? :: 230 V, 50 Hz
How fast must a generator coil spin to produce a 50 Hz output? :: 50 rotations per second (3000 per minute), if the generator has one pair of poles
## Calculations
A transformer has 500 turns on the primary coil and 100 on the secondary. The primary voltage is 230 V. What is the secondary voltage? :: Vs = Vp × Ns ÷ Np = 230 × 100 ÷ 500 = **46 V** (step-down)
A step-up transformer has 50 turns on the primary coil. How many turns are needed on the secondary to change 12 V to 240 V? :: Ns = Np × Vs ÷ Vp = 50 × 240 ÷ 12 = **1000 turns**
A transformer changes 230 V to 12 V. The primary coil has 920 turns. How many turns does the secondary have? :: Ns = 920 × 12 ÷ 230 = **48 turns**
An ideal transformer changes 240 V to 12 V. The secondary current is 2.0 A. What is the primary current? :: Power out = 12 × 2.0 = 24 W ;; Ip = 24 ÷ 240 = **0.10 A**
A transformer takes 100 W in and gives 96 W out. What is its efficiency? :: 96 ÷ 100 × 100% = **96%**
A power station sends 50 MW of power at 25 kV. What is the current in the cables? :: I = P ÷ V = 50 000 000 ÷ 25 000 = **2000 A**
The same 50 MW is sent at 400 kV. What is the current? :: I = 50 000 000 ÷ 400 000 = **125 A**
The voltage is raised from 25 kV to 400 kV (16 times higher). How many times smaller is the power lost as heat in the cables? :: The current is 16 times smaller, so the loss (I²R) is 16² = **256 times smaller**
A cable of resistance 10 Ω carries 100 A. How much power is wasted as heat? :: P = I²R = 100² × 10 = 100 000 W = **100 kW**
A generator coil turns 25 times each second. What is the frequency of the output? :: **25 Hz** (one cycle per rotation)
How do you work out the turns ratio for a transformer? :: Turns ratio = Np : Ns = Vp : Vs ;; e.g. 230 V to 11.5 V is 20 : 1 (a step-down transformer)
## Exam-style explanations
Explain why a voltmeter connected to a coil shows a reading when a magnet is pushed into the coil, and returns to zero when the magnet stops. :: The coil's wire cuts the magnetic field lines as the magnet moves (the magnetic field through the coil changes) ;; This induces a voltage across the coil ;; When the magnet stops, the field no longer changes, so no voltage is induced
Explain why a transformer does not work with a direct current supply. :: A DC current gives a steady magnetic field in the core ;; The field through the secondary coil does not change ;; So no voltage is induced in the secondary coil
Explain why a step-up transformer is used at a power station. :: It increases the voltage and reduces the current ;; Lower current means less energy lost as heat in the transmission cables (P = I²R) ;; So transmission is more efficient
Describe how a generator produces an alternating voltage. :: A coil rotates between the poles of a magnet ;; The sides of the coil cut the magnetic field lines, inducing a voltage ;; Every half turn the sides cut the field in the opposite direction, so the voltage reverses ;; Slip rings and brushes connect the coil to the circuit
Suggest how the voltage output of a generator can be doubled. :: Double the speed of rotation, or double the number of turns on the coil (also: stronger magnets or a larger coil)
Explain why the output from a transformer is zero when the primary current is constant. :: A constant current produces a steady magnetic field ;; No change in field means no induced voltage in the secondary coil
Explain why the lamp in a bicycle dynamo goes out when the cyclist stops. :: When the wheel stops, the magnet stops moving relative to the coil ;; The magnetic field through the coil no longer changes ;; No voltage is induced, so no current flows
Explain how Lenz's law applies when a magnet is pulled out of a coil. :: The induced current flows in the direction that opposes the removal of the magnet ;; The end of the coil nearest the magnet becomes the opposite pole and attracts it ;; Work is done against this force and transferred to electrical energy

## Revision sheet: questions and mark scheme
(2 marks) What is electromagnetic induction? :: A voltage (emf) is induced across a conductor when it moves through a magnetic field / cuts field lines, or when the magnetic field around it changes (1) ;; If the circuit is complete, a current is induced (1)
(3 marks) Give three ways to increase the induced voltage. :: Move the magnet or coil faster (1) ;; Use a stronger magnet (1) ;; Use more turns of wire on the coil (1) ;; **Extra:** reversing the direction of movement or the magnet's poles reverses the voltage. No movement means no voltage.
(4 marks) Explain why a simple generator (dynamo) produces AC. :: The coil rotates in a magnetic field, so its sides cut field lines (1) ;; The voltage is maximum when the coil is horizontal, cutting field lines fastest (1) ;; The voltage is zero when the coil is vertical, moving parallel to the field (1) ;; Every half turn the sides swap direction through the field, so the voltage reverses (1) ;; **Note:** horizontal/vertical assumes the field runs horizontally between the magnets. In general: maximum when the coil is parallel to the field, zero when it is perpendicular to it
(2 marks) How do you make the generator give DC? :: Replace the slip rings with a split-ring commutator (1) ;; It swaps the connections every half turn, so the current always flows the same way (1)
(2 marks) Sketch the voltage–time graphs for an AC generator and a DC generator. :: **AC:** a sine wave that goes above and below zero ;; **DC:** a series of humps that all stay above zero
(2 marks) How does speeding up the rotation of a generator change the graph? :: Higher peak voltage (1) ;; Higher frequency, so more waves close together (1)
(3 marks) Describe the structure of a transformer. :: Primary coil and secondary coil (1) ;; Both wound on a soft iron core (1) ;; The core is easily magnetised and demagnetised (1)
(4 marks) Explain how a transformer works. :: AC in the primary coil creates a changing magnetic field (1) ;; The iron core carries this field to the secondary coil (1) ;; The changing field cuts the secondary coil (1) ;; This induces an alternating voltage in the secondary (1)
(2 marks) Why won't a transformer work with DC? :: DC gives a constant magnetic field, not a changing one (1) ;; No changing field means no voltage is induced (1)
(2 marks) What is the difference between a step-up and a step-down transformer? :: **Step-up:** more turns on the secondary, so voltage increases (Ns > Np) ;; **Step-down:** fewer turns on the secondary, so voltage decreases (Ns < Np)
Learn both: what are the two transformer equations? :: Vp ÷ Vs = Np ÷ Ns ;; Vp × Ip = Vs × Is ;; (the second one is for a 100% efficient transformer)
(3 marks) A transformer has 1000 primary turns, 50 secondary turns and a 230 V input. Find the output voltage. :: Vs = Vp × Ns ÷ Np ;; Vs = 230 × 50 ÷ 1000 ;; Vs = **11.5 V**
(4 marks) Why are step-up transformers used in the National Grid? :: Stepping up the voltage lowers the current for the same power (P = I × V) (1) ;; Less heat is lost in the cables (P = I²R) (1) ;; So the transmission is more efficient (1) ;; Thinner, cheaper cables can be used (1)
(2 marks) Why are step-down transformers used near homes? :: Lower voltage is safer for homes and users (1) ;; Appliances need lower voltages than the grid carries (1)
(6 marks) Plan an investigation into how drop height affects the voltage induced when a magnet falls through a coil. :: 1. Clamp the tube vertically with the coil wound round it, connected to a data logger or voltmeter (1) ;; 2. Use a metre rule to mark different heights above the coil, e.g. 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 cm (1) ;; 3. Start the logger, drop the magnet from the first height, and record the peak voltage (1) ;; 4. Repeat for each height (1) ;; 5. Repeat each height 3 times and calculate a mean (1) ;; 6. Keep these the same: magnet, coil, tube, coil position, and drop without pushing the magnet (1) ;; **Independent variable:** drop height. **Dependent variable:** peak voltage. **Control variables:** everything in step 6
(3 marks) In the magnet drop experiment, what result do you expect and why? :: Higher drop height gives a higher peak voltage (1) ;; The magnet is moving faster when it reaches the coil (1) ;; So the magnetic field changes faster, inducing more voltage (1)
(2 marks) In the magnet drop experiment, why does the trace show two peaks in opposite directions? :: The magnet entering the coil induces a voltage in one direction (1) ;; The magnet leaving the coil induces a voltage in the opposite direction (1)
(1 mark) Why use a data logger rather than reading a voltmeter by eye? :: The voltage lasts a fraction of a second, and the logger records it accurately and quickly
(2 marks) Extension: why does a magnet fall slowly in a copper tube? :: The moving magnet induces currents in the tube (1) ;; These produce a magnetic field that opposes the magnet's motion, slowing it (1)
Exam answer checklist: what should you always include in induction answers? :: Always say "changing magnetic field", not just "magnetic field" ;; Always say "induces a voltage", then "current if the circuit is complete" ;; For transformers, always mention AC and the iron core ;; For practicals, mention repeats, mean and controls

# 7 Radioactivity and particles
Describe the structure of an atom. :: Small positive nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons in shells
What are isotopes? :: Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons
What is an alpha particle and how penetrating/ionising is it? :: 2 protons + 2 neutrons (helium nucleus); stopped by paper or a few cm of air; strongly ionising
What is a beta particle and how penetrating is it? :: A fast electron from the nucleus; stopped by a few mm of aluminium; moderately ionising
What is gamma radiation and how penetrating is it? :: High-frequency EM wave; reduced by thick lead or concrete; weakly ionising
What happens in alpha decay? :: Mass number decreases by 4, atomic number decreases by 2
What happens in beta decay? :: A neutron becomes a proton and an electron; mass number unchanged, atomic number increases by 1
How can radiation be detected? :: Photographic film or a Geiger–Müller tube
Name sources of background radiation. :: Radon gas, rocks, cosmic rays, food and drink, medical (X-rays), nuclear industry
What is activity and what is its unit? :: The number of nuclei decaying per second; becquerel (Bq)
What is half-life? :: The time taken for half the undecayed nuclei in a sample to decay (or activity to halve)
Why is radioactive decay described as random? :: You cannot predict which nucleus will decay or when
Give uses of radioactivity. :: Medical tracers, radiotherapy, sterilising equipment, smoke alarms (alpha), thickness gauges (beta), carbon dating
What is the difference between irradiation and contamination? :: Irradiation – exposed to radiation from an outside source ;; Contamination – radioactive material gets on or inside the body (more dangerous for alpha)
How should radioactive sources be handled safely? :: Use tongs, keep at arm's length, minimise time, store in lead-lined boxes, never point at people
What is nuclear fission? :: A large nucleus (uranium-235) absorbs a neutron, becomes unstable and splits into two smaller daughter nuclei, releasing 2–3 neutrons and energy
What is a chain reaction? :: Neutrons released by fission go on to cause further fissions
What is the role of control rods in a nuclear reactor? :: Absorb neutrons to control the rate of fission (e.g. boron)
What is the role of the moderator in a nuclear reactor? :: Slows down neutrons so they can be absorbed by uranium-235 (e.g. graphite or water)
What is nuclear fusion? :: Two light nuclei join to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy – happens in stars
Why is fusion hard to achieve on Earth? :: Needs extremely high temperature and pressure to overcome the electrostatic repulsion between positive nuclei

# 8 Astrophysics
What is the universe made of? :: A large collection of billions of galaxies
What is a galaxy? :: A large collection of billions of stars
Which galaxy is our solar system in? :: The Milky Way
What force keeps planets, moons and comets in orbit? :: Gravity
What is the difference between the orbits of planets and comets? :: Planets – nearly circular ;; Comets – highly elliptical
Write the equation for orbital speed. :: orbital speed = 2 × π × orbital radius ÷ time period ;; v = 2πr/T
How does gravitational field strength vary? :: Depends on the mass of the planet/moon; g is different on other planets
Describe the life cycle of a Sun-sized star. :: Nebula → protostar → main sequence star → red giant → white dwarf
Describe the life cycle of a massive star. :: Nebula → protostar → main sequence → red supergiant → supernova → neutron star or black hole
How does the colour of a star relate to its temperature? :: Red (coolest) → orange → yellow → white → blue (hottest)
What does a Hertzsprung–Russell diagram plot? :: Absolute magnitude (luminosity) against surface temperature (hot on the left)
What is absolute magnitude? :: The brightness a star would have at a standard distance (10 parsecs) – lower number = brighter
What is redshift? :: Light from distant galaxies is shifted to longer wavelengths because they are moving away from us
Write the redshift equation. :: Δλ/λ₀ = v/c
What evidence supports the Big Bang theory? :: Redshift of galaxies (further galaxies receding faster) and cosmic microwave background radiation (CMBR)
What is CMBR? :: Cosmic microwave background radiation – microwave radiation from all directions, the remains of energy from the Big Bang

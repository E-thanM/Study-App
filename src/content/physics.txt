Write the equation for acceleration. :: acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time taken ;; a = (v − u) ÷ t

What does the gradient of a distance–time graph show? :: Speed
What does the gradient of a velocity–time graph show? :: Acceleration
What does the area under a velocity–time graph show? :: Distance travelled
What is the difference between speed and velocity? :: Velocity is speed in a given direction (vector); speed is scalar
What is the difference between a scalar and a vector? :: Scalar – magnitude only (mass, speed, energy, distance) ;; Vector – magnitude and direction (force, velocity, acceleration, momentum, displacement)
What is a resultant force? :: The single force that has the same effect as all the forces acting on an object
What is friction? :: A force that opposes motion between surfaces in contact
State Newton's first law. :: An object stays at rest or moves at constant velocity unless a resultant force acts on it

What is the stopping distance of a vehicle? :: Thinking distance + braking distance

Give factors affecting braking distance. :: Speed, mass, condition of brakes and tyres, road surface (wet/icy)
Describe the motion of a falling object reaching terminal velocity. :: Initially accelerates at g; air resistance increases with speed; eventually air resistance = weight, resultant force zero, constant (terminal) velocity
What is Hooke's law? :: The extension of a spring is directly proportional to the force applied, provided the limit of proportionality is not exceeded
Describe how to investigate extension of a spring. :: Hang masses on a spring, measure extension with a ruler (at eye level) each time, plot force vs extension – straight line through origin up to the limit of proportionality
What is elastic behaviour? :: An object returns to its original shape when the force is removed
Write the equation for momentum. :: momentum = mass × velocity ;; p = m × v

Explain how crumple zones, seat belts and airbags reduce injury. :: They increase the time taken for the change in momentum, so the force on passengers is smaller
State Newton's third law. :: When two bodies interact, the forces they exert on each other are equal in size and opposite in direction
Write the equation for the moment of a force. :: moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot
State the principle of moments. :: For an object in equilibrium, the sum of clockwise moments equals the sum of anticlockwise moments
What is the centre of gravity? :: The point through which the weight of an object can be considered to act
How do upward forces on a beam change as a weight moves along it? :: The support nearer the weight carries a larger upward force

## 1b Movement and position
What does a horizontal line on a distance–time graph show? :: The object is stationary (not moving)
What does a straight sloping line on a distance–time graph show? :: The object is moving at a constant speed
What does a steeper straight line on a distance–time graph show? :: A greater (constant) speed
What does a curve that gets steeper on a distance–time graph show? :: The object is accelerating (its speed is increasing)
What does a curve that gets less steep on a distance–time graph show? :: The object is decelerating (its speed is decreasing)
How do you find the speed at one instant from a curved distance–time graph? :: Draw a tangent to the curve at that point and calculate its gradient
What does a horizontal line on a velocity–time graph show? :: Constant velocity (zero acceleration)
What does a straight line sloping upwards on a velocity–time graph show? :: Constant (uniform) acceleration
What does a straight line sloping downwards on a velocity–time graph show? :: Constant deceleration
What does a line along the time axis (v = 0) on a velocity–time graph show? :: The object is stationary
How do you find the distance travelled from a velocity–time graph made of straight lines? :: Split the area under the line into rectangles and triangles and add their areas ;; triangle = ½ × base × height
How do you work out the average speed for a whole journey? :: Total distance ÷ total time (including any time spent stopped)
What is deceleration? :: Negative acceleration – the velocity decreases with time
C: Acceleration is the {{c1::rate of change of velocity}}.
C: In v² = u² + 2as, u is the {{c1::initial velocity}}, v is the {{c2::final velocity}}, a is the acceleration and s is the {{c3::distance moved}}.
How do you rearrange v² = u² + 2as to find s? :: s = (v² − u²) ÷ 2a
How do you rearrange a = (v − u) ÷ t to find the final velocity? :: v = u + at
Calculate: a car accelerates from 5 m/s to 25 m/s in 4 s. What is its acceleration? :: a = (v − u) ÷ t = (25 − 5) ÷ 4 = **5 m/s²**
Calculate: a train travels 1.2 km in 1 minute. What is its average speed? :: Convert first: 1200 m ÷ 60 s = **20 m/s**
Calculate: a runner moves at 8 m/s for 25 s. How far does she run? :: distance = speed × time = 8 × 25 = **200 m**
Calculate: a ball is dropped from rest and falls 20 m. What speed does it reach (g = 10 m/s², ignore air resistance)? :: v² = u² + 2as = 0 + 2 × 10 × 20 = 400 ;; v = √400 = **20 m/s**
Calculate: a velocity–time graph rises in a straight line from 0 to 12 m/s in 6 s. How far does the object travel? :: Area of triangle = ½ × 6 × 12 = **36 m**
## 1b Practical: motion of everyday objects
Describe how to investigate the motion of a toy car rolling down a ramp. :: Mark a start line and measure the distance to a finish line ;; Release the car from rest at the start line (don't push it) ;; Time it with a stopwatch or light gates ;; speed = distance ÷ time ;; Repeat and take a mean, then change the ramp height
In the toy car and ramp practical, name the independent, dependent and control variables. :: **Independent:** height (angle) of the ramp ;; **Dependent:** time taken / speed of the car ;; **Control:** same car, same release point, same distance, same ramp surface
Why are light gates more accurate than a stopwatch for timing a toy car? :: They remove human reaction time error, which is large compared with the short times being measured
How do light gates measure the speed of a trolley? :: A card of known length on the trolley blocks the beam; the data logger times how long the beam is blocked ;; speed = length of card ÷ time
How can you measure the speed of a falling tennis ball? :: Film it falling in front of a metre rule and step through the video frame by frame (known time between frames), or use light gates
Why should you release a toy car rather than push it in a ramp practical? :: So that its starting speed is always zero – pushing gives a different, unknown initial speed each time
How can you use measurements of a toy car at two light gates to find its acceleration? :: Measure the speed at each gate and the time to travel between them ;; a = (v − u) ÷ t
## 1c Forces and their effects
What effects can forces have on a body? :: They can change its speed, its direction of motion or its shape
Name some types of force. :: Gravitational (weight), electrostatic, magnetic, friction, air resistance (drag), tension, upthrust, normal contact (reaction) force
Which forces act at a distance (non-contact forces)? :: Gravitational, electrostatic and magnetic forces
How do you find the resultant of two forces acting in the same direction along a line? :: Add them together
How do you find the resultant of two forces acting in opposite directions along a line? :: Subtract the smaller from the larger; the resultant acts in the direction of the larger force
Calculate: a car has a driving force of 5000 N forwards and drag plus friction of 3500 N backwards. What is the resultant force? :: 5000 − 3500 = **1500 N forwards**
What happens to an object if the resultant force on it is zero? :: It stays at rest, or continues to move at a constant velocity (the forces are balanced)
What happens to an object if there is a non-zero resultant force on it? :: It accelerates in the direction of the resultant force (speeds up, slows down or changes direction)
What is the difference between mass and weight? :: **Mass** – the amount of matter, in kg; the same everywhere (scalar) ;; **Weight** – the gravitational force on a mass, in N; depends on g (vector)
What is gravitational field strength? :: The gravitational force (weight) acting per kilogram of mass, measured in N/kg
Calculate: what is the weight of a 60 kg person on Earth (g = 10 N/kg)? :: W = m × g = 60 × 10 = **600 N**
Calculate: a 1200 kg car has a resultant force of 3000 N on it. What is its acceleration? :: a = F ÷ m = 3000 ÷ 1200 = **2.5 m/s²**
What energy transfer happens when friction acts on a moving object? :: Kinetic energy is transferred to thermal energy, heating the surfaces
How does air resistance (drag) change as an object moves faster? :: It increases
## 1c Stopping distances
C: Thinking distance is the distance travelled during the driver's {{c1::reaction time}}.
C: Braking distance is the distance travelled after the {{c1::brakes are applied}} until the vehicle {{c2::stops}}.
How does doubling the speed of a car affect its thinking distance? :: Thinking distance doubles (the car travels twice as far in the same reaction time)
How does doubling the speed of a car affect its braking distance? :: It roughly quadruples – the kinetic energy (½mv²) to be removed by the brakes is four times larger
Why does a larger vehicle mass increase braking distance? :: For the same braking force, a larger mass gives a smaller deceleration (a = F ÷ m), and it has more kinetic energy to remove, so it travels further before stopping
Why do wet or icy roads increase braking distance? :: There is less friction (grip) between the tyres and the road, so the maximum braking force and deceleration are smaller
Why do worn brakes or bald tyres increase braking distance? :: They produce less friction, so a smaller braking force and deceleration
Does drinking alcohol affect thinking distance or braking distance? :: Thinking distance – it increases the driver's reaction time
## 1c Falling objects and terminal velocity
Which two forces act on an object falling through air? :: Its weight (downwards) and air resistance/drag (upwards)
What is the acceleration of an object falling freely near the Earth with no air resistance? :: About 10 m/s² (equal to g)
Why does a falling object's acceleration decrease as it falls? :: Air resistance increases as its speed increases, so the resultant force (weight − air resistance) gets smaller
C: At terminal velocity the air resistance {{c1::equals the weight}}, so the resultant force is {{c2::zero}} and the object moves at constant velocity.
Explain (4 marks) why a skydiver slows down when she opens her parachute. :: The parachute has a much larger surface area, so air resistance increases greatly ;; Air resistance is now bigger than weight, so the resultant force is upwards ;; She decelerates, and as her speed falls the air resistance decreases ;; When air resistance equals weight again she reaches a new, lower terminal velocity
Describe the velocity–time graph of a skydiver from jumping until landing with a parachute. :: Rises steeply, then curves to a flat line (first terminal velocity) ;; Parachute opens – velocity drops sharply then levels off at a lower terminal velocity ;; Falls to zero on landing
Why does a feather fall more slowly than a hammer on Earth but at the same rate on the Moon? :: On Earth air resistance on the feather is large compared with its weight ;; The Moon has no atmosphere, so there is no air resistance and both accelerate at the same g
## 1c Springs, wires and rubber bands
What is the extension of a spring? :: The increase in length: extension = stretched length − original length
What does the initial straight-line region of a force–extension graph show? :: Extension is directly proportional to force – Hooke's law is obeyed
What is the limit of proportionality? :: The point beyond which extension is no longer proportional to the force (the graph stops being a straight line)
What is plastic (inelastic) deformation? :: The object does not return to its original shape when the force is removed – it is permanently deformed
How can you check whether a spring has been stretched beyond its elastic behaviour? :: Remove the load and see whether it returns to its original length
Describe the force–extension graph for a metal wire. :: A straight line through the origin (Hooke's law) for small loads, then it curves after the limit of proportionality; the wire stretches a lot for little extra force before it breaks
Describe the force–extension graph for a rubber band. :: A curve – rubber does not obey Hooke's law (extension is not proportional to force), but it still returns to about its original length when unloaded
Why is a long wire used when investigating the extension of a metal wire? :: The extension of a metal wire is very small; a longer wire gives a larger, more measurable extension
In the spring extension practical, name the independent, dependent and control variables. :: **Independent:** force (weight of masses added) ;; **Dependent:** extension ;; **Control:** the same spring, temperature, how the masses are added
How can you make length readings more accurate in the spring practical? :: Clamp a ruler vertically next to the spring, use a fixed pointer on the bottom of the spring and read at eye level to avoid parallax error
Give safety precautions for the spring and wire practicals. :: Wear eye protection (spring or wire may snap) ;; Clamp the stand to the bench so it can't topple ;; Keep feet away from under the masses
## 1c Momentum (Paper 2)
C: Momentum is a {{c1::vector}} quantity – it has size and direction.
Calculate: what is the momentum of a 1500 kg car travelling at 20 m/s? :: p = m × v = 1500 × 20 = **30 000 kg m/s**
Why must you choose a positive direction in momentum calculations? :: Momentum is a vector – momentum in the opposite direction is negative
Calculate: a 2 kg trolley moving at 3 m/s hits and sticks to a stationary 1 kg trolley. What is their velocity afterwards? :: Momentum before = 2 × 3 = 6 kg m/s ;; After: (2 + 1) × v = 6 ;; v = **2 m/s**
Calculate: a 3 kg gun fires a 0.01 kg bullet at 300 m/s. What is the recoil velocity of the gun? :: Momentum before = 0, so the momenta after are equal and opposite ;; bullet: 0.01 × 300 = 3 kg m/s ;; gun: v = 3 ÷ 3 = **1 m/s backwards**
What is the total momentum of a system before an explosion if everything starts at rest? :: Zero – so the parts move off with equal and opposite momenta
Write the force and momentum equation using mass and velocities. :: F = (mv − mu) ÷ t ;; force = change in momentum ÷ time taken
Calculate: a 0.5 kg ball at rest is kicked and leaves the foot at 20 m/s after 0.05 s of contact. What is the average force? :: F = (mv − mu) ÷ t = (0.5 × 20 − 0) ÷ 0.05 = **200 N**
Explain (4 marks) how a crumple zone reduces injury in a crash. :: The crumple zone deforms on impact ;; This increases the time taken for the car and passengers to stop ;; The change in momentum is the same ;; So the rate of change of momentum is smaller and the force on the passengers is smaller (F = change in momentum ÷ time)
Give other safety features that work by increasing the time for a change in momentum. :: Cycle and motorcycle helmets, crash mats, soft playground surfaces, padded gloves, bending your knees when landing
## 1c Newton's third law (Paper 2)
What are the features of a Newton's third law force pair? :: Equal in size ;; Opposite in direction ;; Same type of force ;; Act on **different** objects
A book rests on a table. What is the Newton's third law partner of the book's weight? :: The gravitational pull of the book on the Earth (acting upwards on the Earth)
Why are a book's weight and the table's upward push on it NOT a Newton's third law pair? :: They act on the same object (the book) and are different types of force
How does Newton's third law explain the recoil of a gun? :: The gun pushes the bullet forwards; the bullet pushes the gun backwards with an equal force
## 1c Moments and centre of gravity
What is the unit of a moment? :: newton metre (N m)
Calculate: what is the moment of a 20 N force acting 0.5 m from a pivot (at right angles)? :: moment = 20 × 0.5 = **10 N m**
What two conditions are needed for an object to be in equilibrium? :: No resultant force ;; No resultant moment (clockwise moments = anticlockwise moments)
Why is it easier to open a door by pushing far from the hinge? :: A larger perpendicular distance from the pivot gives a larger moment for the same force
Calculate: a 300 N child sits 2 m from the pivot of a see-saw. How far from the pivot must a 400 N child sit on the other side to balance it? :: 300 × 2 = 400 × d ;; d = 600 ÷ 400 = **1.5 m**
Where is the centre of gravity of a uniform beam or ruler? :: At its midpoint
Describe how to find the centre of gravity of an irregular flat shape (lamina). :: Hang it from a pin so it swings freely ;; Hang a plumb line from the pin and draw along it ;; Repeat from a different hole ;; The centre of gravity is where the lines cross
Calculate: a light 4 m beam rests on supports at each end, A and B. A 1000 N load is 1 m from A. Find the upward force at each support. :: Moments about A: F(B) × 4 = 1000 × 1, so F(B) = **250 N** ;; Upward forces = downward forces, so F(A) = 1000 − 250 = **750 N**
How do you find the upward forces on a beam supported at both ends? :: Take moments about one support to find the force at the other ;; Then use total upward force = total downward force
## 1c Practical: forces on falling objects
Describe an experiment to investigate the forces acting on falling objects such as paper parachutes or sycamore seeds. :: Drop the object from a fixed, measured height ;; Time the fall with a stopwatch, light gates or video ;; Change one variable (e.g. canopy area, or mass by adding paper clips) and keep the rest the same ;; Repeat each drop and take a mean ;; average speed = height ÷ time
In a paper parachute practical, how does a larger canopy area affect the fall? :: More air resistance at a given speed, so a lower terminal velocity and a longer fall time
In a paper parachute practical, how does adding mass (paper clips) affect the fall? :: Greater weight, so it must fall faster before air resistance balances the weight – higher terminal velocity, shorter fall time
Why is a large drop height useful in falling-object experiments? :: The fall time is longer, so human reaction time is a smaller fraction of it (smaller percentage error), and the object has time to reach terminal velocity

Calculate: a car at 20 m/s has a thinking distance of 14 m and a braking distance of 40 m. What is the stopping distance? :: stopping distance = thinking + braking = 14 + 40 = **54 m**

What is a closed system in momentum questions? :: A system on which no external forces act, so total momentum is conserved
How does Newton's third law explain how a rocket accelerates? :: The rocket pushes exhaust gases backwards; the gases push the rocket forwards with an equal and opposite force

# 2 Electricity
What is the difference between AC and DC? :: AC – direction of current continually reverses (mains); DC – flows in one direction only (cells/batteries)
What is the UK mains supply? :: 230 V AC, 50 Hz
How does a fuse work? :: Thin wire that melts if current is too high, breaking the circuit
How does a circuit breaker work? :: Switch that trips (opens) when the current is too large; can be reset
Why does current cause heating? :: Electrons collide with ions in the wire, transferring energy which heats it
Write the equation for power using current and voltage. :: power = current × voltage ;; P = I × V
Write the equation for energy transferred using current, voltage and time. :: energy transferred = current × voltage × time ;; E = I × V × t
How does current vary with voltage for a resistor, filament lamp and diode? :: Resistor – straight line (constant R) ;; Filament lamp – curve; resistance increases as it gets hotter ;; Diode – current flows in one direction only
How does the resistance of an LDR change? :: Decreases as light intensity increases
How does the resistance of a thermistor change? :: Decreases as temperature increases (NTC)
Write Ohm's law. :: voltage = current × resistance ;; V = I × R
How do current and voltage behave in series circuits? :: Current is the same everywhere; voltage is shared between components; total R = R₁ + R₂
How do current and voltage behave in parallel circuits? :: Voltage is the same across each branch; current splits between branches; total current = sum of branch currents
What is electric current? :: The rate of flow of charge
Write the equation linking charge, current and time. :: charge = current × time ;; Q = I × t
What carries current in metals? :: Negatively charged electrons
What is voltage (potential difference)? :: The energy transferred per unit charge passed
Write the equation linking energy, charge and voltage. :: energy transferred = charge × voltage ;; E = Q × V
Describe how to investigate how current varies with voltage for a component. :: Set up circuit with ammeter in series and voltmeter in parallel, use variable resistor to change V; record I; reverse the supply for negative values; plot I–V graph
How do you test if an object is charged? :: Bring it near small pieces of paper or a charged rod – attraction/repulsion

## 2b Mains electricity and safety
How does insulation on wires protect the user? :: The plastic covering stops anyone touching the live conducting metal inside
What is meant by double insulation? :: The appliance has an insulating (plastic) case and no exposed metal parts that could become live, so it needs no earth wire
What colours are the live, neutral and earth wires in a UK plug? :: Live – brown ;; Neutral – blue ;; Earth – green and yellow stripes
In which wire is the fuse (or switch) placed, and why? :: The live wire – so when it blows (or is switched off) the appliance is disconnected from the high voltage
Explain (4 marks) how the earth wire and fuse protect a user if a fault makes the metal case of a kettle live. :: The earth wire gives a low-resistance path from the case to earth ;; A very large current flows through the live and earth wires ;; This melts the fuse, breaking the circuit ;; The case is no longer live, so the user does not get an electric shock
How do you choose the correct fuse for an appliance? :: Calculate the normal current (I = P ÷ V) and choose the fuse with the next rating above it (e.g. 3 A, 5 A or 13 A)
Calculate: which fuse (3 A, 5 A or 13 A) should be fitted to a 2300 W kettle on a 230 V supply? :: I = P ÷ V = 2300 ÷ 230 = 10 A ;; so use the **13 A** fuse
Why should a fuse not have a rating much higher than the normal current? :: It would not melt when a dangerous fault current flows, so it would not protect the appliance or user
Give advantages of a circuit breaker over a fuse. :: It acts faster ;; It can be reset rather than replaced
Give domestic uses of the heating effect of a current. :: Kettles, toasters, electric fires/heaters, irons, hair dryers, immersion heaters
Why is the heating effect of current sometimes a disadvantage? :: Energy is wasted as heat in cables and in devices such as computers and chargers
Calculate: a 230 V kettle draws 8 A. What is its power? :: P = I × V = 8 × 230 = **1840 W**
Calculate: a 12 V heater draws 2 A for 5 minutes. How much energy is transferred? :: Convert: t = 5 × 60 = 300 s ;; E = I × V × t = 2 × 12 × 300 = **7200 J**
Why must time be in seconds when using E = I × V × t? :: The joule is a watt × second, so energy comes out in joules only if time is in seconds
## 2c Series and parallel circuits
Why is a parallel circuit better than a series circuit for house lighting? :: Each lamp gets the full mains voltage ;; Each can be switched on and off independently ;; If one lamp fails the others stay on
Give disadvantages of connecting lamps in series. :: If one lamp breaks, they all go out ;; The voltage is shared, so each lamp is dimmer ;; They can't be switched independently
How does adding more lamps in series affect the current? :: The total resistance increases, so the current decreases (lamps get dimmer)
How does increasing the supply voltage affect the current in a series circuit? :: The current increases
What happens to the total resistance when another resistor is added in parallel? :: It decreases (there are more paths for the current), so the total current from the supply increases
C: At a junction, the total current {{c1::entering}} equals the total current {{c2::leaving}}.
Why is current conserved at a junction? :: Charge is not used up or created, so the charge flowing in each second equals the charge flowing out
How is the supply voltage shared in a series circuit? :: The voltages across the components add up to the supply voltage; the larger resistance gets the larger share
Calculate: a 4 Ω and an 8 Ω resistor are in series with a 6 V battery. Find the current and the voltage across each. :: Total R = 4 + 8 = 12 Ω ;; I = V ÷ R = 6 ÷ 12 = **0.5 A** ;; V(4 Ω) = 0.5 × 4 = **2 V**, V(8 Ω) = 0.5 × 8 = **4 V**
Calculate: two branches in parallel carry 0.3 A and 0.5 A. What current flows from the supply? :: 0.3 + 0.5 = **0.8 A**
How should an ammeter and a voltmeter be connected in a circuit? :: Ammeter – in series with the component ;; Voltmeter – in parallel across the component
## 2c Resistance and components

What is the effect of increasing resistance on the current (for the same voltage)? :: The current decreases
Describe the I–V graph for a wire or resistor at constant temperature. :: A straight line through the origin – current is directly proportional to voltage (constant resistance)
Explain why the resistance of a filament lamp increases as the current increases. :: The filament gets hotter ;; The metal ions vibrate more ;; Electrons collide with the ions more often, so resistance increases
Describe the I–V graph for a diode. :: Zero current for reverse (negative) voltages – very high resistance ;; In the forward direction, current rises steeply once the voltage passes about 0.6 V
How do you find the resistance at a point on a curved I–V graph? :: Read off V and I at that point and calculate R = V ÷ I (not the gradient)
C: Lamps and {{c1::LEDs}} can be used to show that a {{c2::current}} is flowing in a circuit.
Give uses of an LDR. :: Automatic street lights, security lights, light meters
Give uses of a thermistor. :: Thermostats, fire alarms, electronic thermometers and temperature sensors
In the I–V practical, why should the current be switched off between readings? :: To stop the component heating up, which would change its resistance
In the I–V practical, what is the variable resistor for? :: To change the current and voltage so that a range of readings can be taken
Why is a resistor placed in series with a diode or LED? :: To limit the current so the diode isn't damaged
In the I–V practical, name the independent and dependent variables. :: **Independent:** voltage (potential difference) ;; **Dependent:** current

## 2c Charge, current and energy

## 2d Electric charge (Paper 2)
Name common electrical conductors. :: Metals (e.g. copper, aluminium) and graphite (carbon)
Name common electrical insulators. :: Plastics (e.g. polythene, PVC), rubber, glass, dry wood
C: An insulator becomes positively charged when it {{c1::loses electrons}} and negatively charged when it {{c2::gains electrons}}.
Why are only electrons transferred when insulators are rubbed together? :: Protons are held in the nucleus; electrons in the outer parts of atoms can be rubbed off
A polythene rod is rubbed with a dry cloth. What charge does it get and why? :: Negative – electrons are transferred from the cloth to the rod (the cloth becomes positive)
An acetate rod is rubbed with a dry cloth. What charge does it get and why? :: Positive – electrons are transferred from the rod to the cloth
C: Like charges {{c1::repel}}; unlike charges {{c2::attract}}.
Describe the practical to investigate charging insulators by friction. :: Rub a polythene or acetate rod with a dry cloth ;; Hang it in a paper cradle on a thread so it can turn ;; Bring a second charged rod near it ;; Same materials repel (like charges); polythene and acetate attract (unlike charges)
Why can't a metal rod held in your hand be charged by rubbing? :: Electrons flow through the metal and your body to earth, so no charge builds up
Explain why a charged balloon sticks to a wall. :: The negative balloon repels electrons in the wall's surface away from it ;; This leaves the nearby surface of the wall positive ;; Unlike charges attract, so the balloon sticks
Explain the danger of electrostatic charge when refuelling an aircraft or fuel tanker. :: Fuel rubbing on the pipe causes charge to build up by friction ;; A large voltage builds up and a spark may jump ;; The spark could ignite the fuel vapour and cause an explosion
How is the danger of sparks reduced when refuelling aircraft? :: The aircraft and tanker are connected (bonded) to earth with a conducting cable, so charge flows away and cannot build up
Explain how a photocopier uses electrostatic charge. :: A drum is charged ;; An image of the page is projected onto it – light areas lose their charge ;; Black toner powder is attracted to the charged (dark) areas ;; The toner is transferred to paper and heated to fix it
Explain how an inkjet printer uses electrostatic charge. :: Tiny ink droplets are given a charge ;; They pass between charged deflecting plates ;; The plates attract or repel them so they land in the right place on the paper
Explain how electrostatic paint spraying works. :: Paint droplets get the same charge, so they repel and form a fine spray ;; The object is given the opposite charge, so it attracts the paint ;; Gives an even coat, reaches hidden areas and wastes less paint
## 2b Electrical hazards

## 3d Light: refraction practicals and refractive index

Describe how to measure the refractive index of glass using a rectangular block. :: Draw round the block on paper ;; Shine a narrow ray from a ray box into it at an angle and mark the incident and emergent rays ;; Remove the block, join the points and draw the normal ;; Measure i and r with a protractor ;; Repeat for several angles; n = sin i ÷ sin r

How can a semi-circular glass block be used to measure the critical angle? :: Aim the ray through the curved side at the centre of the flat face ;; Increase the angle of incidence until the refracted ray just travels along the flat face ;; That angle of incidence is the critical angle
What happens when white light passes through a triangular prism? :: It is dispersed into a spectrum, because each colour is refracted by a different amount (violet most, red least)
What is the unit of refractive index? :: It has no unit – it is a ratio
Calculate: a ray enters glass at 50° and refracts at 30°. What is the refractive index (sin 50° = 0.77, sin 30° = 0.50)? :: n = sin i ÷ sin r = 0.77 ÷ 0.50 = **1.5** (1.54)
Calculate: light hits glass (n = 1.5) at an angle of incidence of 40°. What is the angle of refraction? :: sin r = sin i ÷ n = 0.643 ÷ 1.5 = 0.429 ;; r = sin⁻¹(0.429) = **25°**
Calculate: what is the critical angle for glass with refractive index 1.5? :: sin c = 1 ÷ n = 1 ÷ 1.5 = 0.667 ;; c = sin⁻¹(0.667) = **42°**
## 3d Total internal reflection
C: The critical angle is the angle of incidence in the denser medium at which the angle of refraction is {{c1::90°}}.
C: Total internal reflection happens when light travels from a {{c1::denser to a less dense medium}} (e.g. glass to air) and the angle of incidence is {{c2::greater than the critical angle}}.
What happens to light that hits a glass–air boundary at an angle less than the critical angle? :: Most is refracted out into the air, with a weak partial reflection
What happens to light that hits a glass–air boundary at exactly the critical angle? :: The refracted ray travels along the boundary (angle of refraction = 90°)
Explain how an optical fibre transmits information. :: Light enters one end of the glass fibre ;; It hits the inside surface at an angle greater than the critical angle ;; It is totally internally reflected again and again ;; So it travels along the fibre, even round bends, carrying the signal
How does a 45° prism turn a ray through 90° in a periscope? :: The ray enters a short face at 90° (no refraction) ;; It hits the long face at 45°, which is more than the critical angle (about 42°) ;; It is totally internally reflected through 90°
How can a prism turn light through 180° (e.g. binoculars, bicycle reflectors)? :: The light is totally internally reflected twice, at two faces, each turning it through 90°

Describe how to find the frequency of a sound using an oscilloscope. :: Connect a signal generator to a loudspeaker; place a microphone connected to an oscilloscope nearby ;; Adjust the timebase to show a few waves ;; Measure the divisions for one wave (or several) × timebase = T ;; f = 1 ÷ T

How does the oscilloscope trace change for a higher-pitched sound? :: More waves fit on the screen (shorter time period, higher frequency)
How does the oscilloscope trace change for a louder sound? :: The waves are taller (larger amplitude); their spacing is unchanged

# 4 Energy resources and transfers
Name the energy stores. :: Chemical, kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic, thermal, magnetic, electrostatic, nuclear

## 4b Energy stores and transfers
Describe the energy transfers in a battery-powered torch. :: Chemical store of the battery → transferred electrically → light (radiation) and thermal store of the surroundings (heating)
Describe the energy transfer when an object falls (with air resistance). :: Gravitational potential store → kinetic store, with some transferred to the thermal store of the air and object by air resistance
Describe the energy transfer when a car brakes to a stop. :: Kinetic store of the car → thermal store of the brakes (and surroundings), transferred mechanically by friction
Describe the energy transfer when a stretched catapult is released. :: Elastic potential store → kinetic store of the stone
Describe the energy transfer in an electric kettle. :: Transferred electrically to the heating element → thermal store of the water (by heating), with some to the surroundings

Why can efficiency never be more than 100%? :: Energy cannot be created, so the useful energy output can never exceed the total energy input

How do you draw a Sankey diagram? :: Draw the input as a wide arrow ;; Split it into a useful arrow (straight on) and wasted arrows (bending away) ;; Make each arrow's width proportional to the energy it represents

## 4b Thermal energy transfer
Why are metals the best thermal conductors? :: They have free (delocalised) electrons that move through the metal and carry energy quickly, as well as vibrating ions
Why are gases poor thermal conductors? :: The particles are far apart, so energy is passed on by collisions only rarely
Why can't convection happen in a solid? :: The particles can only vibrate about fixed positions – they can't move from place to place

Explain (3 marks) how a sea breeze forms on a sunny day. :: The land heats up faster than the sea ;; Air above the land gets warmer, less dense and rises ;; Cooler, denser air from over the sea moves in to replace it
How does the temperature of an object affect the infrared it emits? :: The hotter the object, the more infrared radiation it emits each second
C: All objects {{c1::emit and absorb}} infrared radiation.
Describe how to use a Leslie cube to compare infrared emission from different surfaces. :: Fill the cube (faces: matt black, shiny black, white, silver) with hot water ;; Measure the infrared from each face with an infrared detector at the same distance ;; The matt black face emits the most, the shiny silver face the least

How does double glazing reduce energy loss? :: The gap between the two panes contains air (or a vacuum), which is a poor conductor, reducing conduction

## 4c Work and power

How should you read a measuring cylinder? :: At eye level, reading from the bottom of the meniscus
Calculate: a 600 N person stands on an area of 0.03 m². What pressure do they exert? :: p = F ÷ A = 600 ÷ 0.03 = **20 000 Pa**
Why do tractors and snowshoes have a large area in contact with the ground? :: A larger area gives a smaller pressure for the same force, so they don't sink in
Why are knives and drawing pins sharp? :: The small area gives a large pressure for a given force
Why does pressure in a liquid increase with depth? :: There is a greater weight of liquid above pushing down
Calculate: what is the pressure difference between the surface and 10 m deep in water (ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 10 N/kg)? :: p = h × ρ × g = 10 × 1000 × 10 = **100 000 Pa**
What two things does the pressure in a liquid depend on? :: The depth and the density of the liquid (not the shape of the container)
## 5c Change of state (Paper 2)
Describe the arrangement and motion of particles in a solid. :: Close together in a regular arrangement ;; Vibrate about fixed positions
Describe the arrangement and motion of particles in a liquid. :: Close together in a random arrangement ;; Move around each other
Describe the arrangement and motion of particles in a gas. :: Far apart in a random arrangement ;; Move quickly in all directions
C: Heating a system increases the energy stored in it, which either {{c1::raises its temperature}} or produces a {{c2::change of state}}.
What happens to the particles when a solid melts? :: Energy weakens/overcomes the forces holding them in fixed positions, so they can move around each other
What is the difference between evaporation and boiling? :: **Evaporation** – only at the surface, at any temperature ;; **Boiling** – throughout the liquid, at the boiling point
Why does evaporation cool the liquid left behind? :: The fastest (most energetic) particles escape from the surface, so the average kinetic energy of the remaining particles falls – the temperature drops
Describe the temperature–time graph when ice is heated until it becomes steam. :: Temperature rises ;; Flat at 0 °C while it melts ;; Rises ;; Flat at 100 °C while it boils ;; Rises again
Describe how to obtain a temperature–time graph showing a change of state. :: Heat crushed ice in a beaker (or let melted stearic acid cool in a tube) with a thermometer in it ;; Stir and record the temperature every 30 s ;; Plot temperature against time – the flat section is the change of state
Why is crushed ice used in the change of state practical? :: Small pieces give good contact with the thermometer and heat more evenly
Give a safety precaution when using stearic acid or hot water baths. :: Wear eye protection and handle hot apparatus with care (tongs/gloves) to avoid burns
## 5c Specific heat capacity (Paper 2)
Calculate: how much energy is needed to heat 2 kg of water by 10 °C (c = 4200 J/kg °C)? :: ΔQ = m × c × ΔT = 2 × 4200 × 10 = **84 000 J**
Calculate: 18 000 J raises the temperature of a 2 kg block by 10 °C. What is its specific heat capacity? :: c = ΔQ ÷ (m × ΔT) = 18 000 ÷ (2 × 10) = **900 J/kg °C**
Describe how to measure the specific heat capacity of a metal block. :: Measure the block's mass ;; Insulate it and put a heater and thermometer in its holes ;; Record the start temperature ;; Heat for a set time, measuring energy with a joulemeter (or E = I × V × t) ;; Record the final temperature ;; c = E ÷ (m × ΔT)
Why is the measured specific heat capacity usually higher than the true value? :: Energy is lost to the surroundings, so not all the energy supplied heats the block – the temperature rise is smaller than it should be
How can you reduce errors in the specific heat capacity practical? :: Insulate the block (or beaker) ;; Put a drop of oil or water in the thermometer hole for good thermal contact ;; Use a lid on water ;; Record the highest temperature reached after switching off
Why does water make a good substance for storing energy in central heating systems? :: It has a high specific heat capacity (4200 J/kg °C), so it stores a lot of energy for each degree of temperature rise
In the specific heat capacity practical, how do you work out the energy supplied without a joulemeter? :: Measure the current and voltage of the heater and the heating time ;; E = I × V × t
## 5d Ideal gas molecules
Explain why there is an absolute zero of temperature. :: As a gas cools its particles slow down and have less kinetic energy ;; At −273 °C they have the least possible kinetic energy, so the temperature cannot go any lower
Calculate: convert 27 °C to kelvin. :: 27 + 273 = **300 K**
Why does increasing the temperature of a gas increase the average speed of its molecules? :: Heating transfers energy to the molecules' kinetic energy stores, so they move faster
C: Gas molecules move with {{c1::random}} motion and exert a force, and hence a pressure, on the walls of their container.
Explain (3 marks) why decreasing the volume of a gas at constant temperature increases its pressure. :: The molecules are closer together ;; They hit the walls more often (the speed of the molecules is unchanged) ;; A greater force on each unit area of wall, so higher pressure
What is the qualitative relationship between pressure and volume for a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature? :: As volume decreases, pressure increases (pressure is inversely proportional to volume)
What is the qualitative relationship between pressure and Kelvin temperature for a fixed mass of gas at constant volume? :: As temperature increases, pressure increases (pressure is directly proportional to Kelvin temperature)
Describe a graph of pressure against temperature in °C for a gas at constant volume. :: A straight line that, if extended backwards, crosses the temperature axis at −273 °C (absolute zero)
Why must temperatures be in kelvin when using p₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂? :: Pressure is proportional to absolute (Kelvin) temperature; 0 °C is not zero energy, so Celsius values give wrong answers
Calculate: a gas at 100 kPa is compressed from 2.0 m³ to 0.5 m³ at constant temperature. What is the new pressure? :: p₂ = p₁V₁ ÷ V₂ = 100 × 2.0 ÷ 0.5 = **400 kPa**
Calculate: a gas at 27 °C and 100 kPa is heated to 127 °C at constant volume. What is the new pressure? :: Convert: T₁ = 300 K, T₂ = 400 K ;; p₂ = p₁ × T₂ ÷ T₁ = 100 × 400 ÷ 300 = **133 kPa**
What condition must hold for both gas laws (p₁V₁ = p₂V₂ and p₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂) to apply? :: A fixed mass (amount) of gas; plus constant temperature for Boyle's law, or constant volume for the pressure law
## 5b More density and pressure
Calculate: a stone has a mass of 54 g and displaces 20 cm³ of water. What is its density in g/cm³ and kg/m³? :: ρ = 54 ÷ 20 = **2.7 g/cm³** ;; × 1000 = **2700 kg/m³**
Calculate: the atmospheric pressure is 100 kPa. What is the total pressure on a diver 5 m below the surface of water (ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 10 N/kg)? :: Pressure difference = 5 × 1000 × 10 = 50 000 Pa = 50 kPa ;; Total = 100 + 50 = **150 kPa**
Why does an object denser than water sink? :: Its density is greater than the density of water (its weight is greater than the upthrust when fully submerged)
## 5c More changes of state
C: Solid → liquid is {{c1::melting}}; liquid → gas is {{c2::boiling or evaporating}}; gas → liquid is {{c3::condensing}}; liquid → solid is {{c4::freezing (solidifying)}}.
Why do two objects of the same mass but different materials heat up by different amounts when given the same energy? :: They have different specific heat capacities – the one with the lower specific heat capacity has the larger temperature rise
## 5d More gas calculations
Why must a gas be compressed slowly in Boyle's law questions? :: So the temperature stays constant (rapid compression would heat the gas)
Explain (3 marks) why heating a sealed can of gas can make it explode. :: The gas molecules gain kinetic energy and move faster ;; They hit the walls more often and with more force ;; The pressure rises until the can bursts

# 6 Magnetism and electromagnetism
Which materials are magnetic? :: Iron, steel, nickel and cobalt
What is the difference between soft and hard magnetic materials? :: Soft (iron) – magnetised and demagnetised easily (electromagnet cores) ;; Hard (steel) – keep magnetism (permanent magnets)
How do magnets interact? :: Like poles repel, unlike poles attract
What is magnetic field direction? :: From north pole to south pole
How do you plot a magnetic field? :: Use a plotting compass and mark the direction of the needle at many points, or sprinkle iron filings
Describe the magnetic field around a straight current-carrying wire. :: Concentric circles around the wire; direction given by the right-hand grip rule
How can you make an electromagnet stronger? :: More turns of wire, larger current, iron core
Why does a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field experience a force? :: The magnetic field of the current interacts with the external field (motor effect)
How do you find the direction of the force on a wire (motor effect)? :: Fleming's left-hand rule: First finger – Field, seCond finger – Current, thuMb – Motion
How can you increase the force on a wire (motor effect)? :: Increase current, increase magnetic field strength (stronger magnet), longer wire in field
How does a DC motor work? :: Current in coil in magnetic field produces forces in opposite directions on each side → coil turns; split-ring commutator reverses current every half turn so it keeps rotating
How does a loudspeaker work? :: AC in a coil in a magnetic field makes the coil and cone vibrate, producing sound waves
What is electromagnetic induction? :: A voltage is induced in a conductor when it moves relative to a magnetic field (or the field changes)
How can the induced voltage be increased? :: Move the wire/magnet faster, use a stronger magnet, more turns on the coil
How does a generator (dynamo) work? :: Coil rotates in a magnetic field, inducing an alternating voltage; slip rings connect to the circuit (AC generator)
How does a transformer work? :: AC in primary coil creates a changing magnetic field in the iron core, which induces an alternating voltage in the secondary coil
Write the transformer equation. :: Vp ÷ Vs = Np ÷ Ns
Write the power equation for a 100% efficient transformer. :: Vp × Ip = Vs × Is
Why is electricity transmitted at high voltage? :: Step-up transformers increase voltage and reduce current, so less energy is lost as heat in the cables
## 6b Magnets and magnetic fields
What do magnets attract? :: Other magnets (unlike poles) and magnetic materials – iron, steel, nickel and cobalt
Why is repulsion the only sure test that an object is a magnet? :: A magnet attracts unmagnetised magnetic materials as well as other magnets, but only two magnets can repel each other
What is a magnetic field? :: The region around a magnet where a magnetic material or another magnet experiences a force
What is a magnetic field line? :: A line showing the direction of the force that would act on a north pole placed in the field (drawn with arrows from N to S)
C: The closer together the magnetic field lines, the {{c1::stronger}} the magnetic field.
Where is the magnetic field of a bar magnet strongest? :: At the poles, where the field lines are closest together
Describe the magnetic field pattern of a bar magnet. :: Lines come out of the north pole and curve round to the south pole ;; They are most concentrated at the poles and never cross
What is induced magnetism? :: Magnetism produced in a magnetic material when it is placed in a magnetic field – the material becomes a magnet itself
Why is an iron nail always attracted (never repelled) by either pole of a magnet? :: Magnetism is induced in the nail – the end nearest the magnet becomes the opposite pole, so it is attracted
What happens to induced magnetism in soft iron and in steel when the magnet is removed? :: Soft iron loses it almost completely ;; Steel keeps it (becomes a permanent magnet)
Why can a chain of iron paper clips hang from a magnet? :: Each clip has magnetism induced in it, so it attracts the next clip
C: Magnetically {{c1::soft}} materials (e.g. iron) are easily magnetised and lose their magnetism easily; magnetically {{c2::hard}} materials (e.g. steel) are hard to magnetise but keep their magnetism.
Give uses of magnetically soft and hard materials. :: Soft (iron) – cores of electromagnets and transformers ;; Hard (steel) – permanent magnets, e.g. in loudspeakers, motors and compasses
## 6b Practical: magnetic field patterns
Describe how to plot the magnetic field of a bar magnet with a plotting compass. :: Put the magnet on paper and draw round it ;; Place a plotting compass near the N pole and mark a dot at the tip of the needle ;; Move the compass so its tail is on the dot and mark the new tip ;; Repeat until you reach the S pole, then join the dots into a line ;; Repeat from other starting points; add arrows N to S
Describe the field pattern between two bar magnets with unlike poles (N and S) facing. :: Field lines go straight across the gap from the N pole to the S pole – the magnets attract
Describe the field pattern between two bar magnets with like poles (N and N) facing. :: Field lines from each pole curve away from each other, with a neutral point midway between the poles – the magnets repel
What is a neutral point in a magnetic field pattern? :: A point where the fields of two magnets cancel out, so there is no resultant magnetic field (a compass placed there does not point any particular way)
What is a uniform magnetic field? :: A field with the same strength and direction everywhere – shown by parallel, equally spaced field lines
How can two permanent magnets be used to produce a uniform magnetic field? :: Place them with opposite poles (N facing S) close together and parallel; the field in the gap between them is uniform
Give an advantage and a disadvantage of using iron filings rather than plotting compasses to show a field. :: Advantage – show the whole pattern quickly ;; Disadvantage – they don't show the direction of the field
## 6c Magnetic effect of a current
C: An electric current in a conductor produces a {{c1::magnetic field}} around it.
State the right-hand grip rule for a straight wire. :: Grip the wire with your right hand with the thumb pointing in the direction of the (conventional) current – your fingers curl in the direction of the magnetic field
How does the magnetic field around a straight wire change further from the wire? :: It gets weaker – the circles are drawn further apart
What happens to the magnetic field around a wire if the current is reversed? :: The field direction reverses
What happens to the magnetic field around a wire if the current is increased? :: The field becomes stronger
Describe the magnetic field pattern of a flat circular coil carrying a current. :: Circles around each side of the coil (in opposite directions) ;; Near the centre the lines are straight and pass through the coil at right angles to it
Describe the magnetic field pattern of a current-carrying solenoid. :: Inside – strong and uniform (parallel, equally spaced lines) ;; Outside – the same shape as the field of a bar magnet
How do you work out which end of a solenoid is the north pole? :: Curl the fingers of your right hand in the direction of the current around the coils – your thumb points to the north pole ;; (Viewed end-on: current anticlockwise = N, clockwise = S)
How can the polarity of a solenoid be reversed? :: Reverse the direction of the current
## 6c Electromagnets
Describe the construction of an electromagnet. :: A coil (solenoid) of insulated wire wound around a soft iron core, connected to a DC supply and a switch
Why does an electromagnet have a soft iron core? :: Iron is easily magnetised, making the field much stronger ;; It is easily demagnetised, so the magnetism disappears when the current is switched off
Why would a steel core be unsuitable for an electromagnet? :: Steel is magnetically hard – it would stay magnetised when the current was switched off
Give advantages of an electromagnet over a permanent magnet. :: It can be switched on and off ;; Its strength can be changed by changing the current
Give uses of electromagnets. :: Cranes for lifting scrap iron, relays, electric bells, circuit breakers, MRI scanners
Why must electromagnet wire be insulated? :: So the current flows around every turn of the coil, rather than short-circuiting between touching turns or through the core
How can you investigate the strength of an electromagnet? :: Count how many paper clips (or the mass of iron) it can pick up ;; Change the current or the number of turns, one at a time, keeping other variables the same
## 6c The motor effect
C: A charged particle moving in a magnetic field experiences a force, as long as its motion is not {{c1::parallel}} to the field.
When is the force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field largest, and when is it zero? :: Largest when the wire is at right angles (perpendicular) to the field ;; Zero when the wire is parallel to the field
What happens to the force on a current-carrying wire if the current or the field is reversed? :: The force reverses direction ;; Reversing both leaves the force in the same direction
How does the force on a wire depend on the current and the field? :: A larger current or a stronger magnetic field gives a larger force
A wire carries a current from left to right across the page. The magnetic field points down the page. Which way is the force on the wire? :: Into the page (Fleming's left-hand rule: First finger down, seCond finger right, thuMb points into the page)
C: In Fleming's left-hand rule, the first finger shows the {{c1::field}}, the second finger the {{c2::current}} and the thumb the {{c3::motion (force)}}.
Describe a simple demonstration of the motor effect. :: Place a loose wire or metal rod across two rails between the poles of a magnet and pass a current through it ;; The rod moves (catapults) along the rails at right angles to the field and current
## 6c Electric motors and loudspeakers
Explain (5 marks) how a simple DC motor works. :: Current flows through the coil, which is in a magnetic field ;; The current flows in opposite directions along the two sides of the coil ;; So the forces on the two sides are in opposite directions (left-hand rule), producing a turning effect ;; The split-ring commutator reverses the current in the coil every half turn ;; So the forces keep turning the coil in the same direction
What is the job of the split-ring commutator in a DC motor? :: It reverses the direction of the current in the coil every half turn, so the coil keeps rotating in the same direction
What is the job of the brushes in a DC motor? :: They press on the commutator and make electrical contact between the fixed supply and the rotating coil
How can the speed of a DC motor be increased? :: Increase the current (voltage), use a stronger magnet, use more turns on the coil, wind the coil on a soft iron core
How can the direction of rotation of a DC motor be reversed? :: Reverse the current (swap the battery connections) or swap the magnetic poles
What is the main energy transfer in an electric motor? :: Electrical energy → kinetic energy (with some wasted as heat and sound)
Explain (4 marks) how a loudspeaker produces sound. :: An alternating current flows through a coil attached to a paper cone, in the field of a permanent magnet ;; There is a force on the coil (motor effect) ;; Because the current keeps reversing, the force keeps reversing, so the coil and cone vibrate ;; The cone makes the air vibrate, producing sound waves at the same frequency as the AC
How does a loudspeaker produce a higher-pitched or a louder sound? :: Higher pitch – increase the frequency of the AC ;; Louder – increase the size (amplitude) of the current, so the cone vibrates with a larger amplitude
Why does a loudspeaker need alternating current? :: The force on the coil must keep reversing direction to make the cone vibrate backwards and forwards; DC would just push the cone one way and hold it

# 6 Electromagnetic induction in depth
## Induction basics
C: A voltage is induced in a conductor when it {{c1::cuts}} magnetic field lines, or when the magnetic field through a coil {{c2::changes}}.
What are the two ways to induce a voltage in a coil? :: **1.** Move a magnet towards or away from the coil (or move the coil through a magnetic field) ;; **2.** Keep everything still but change the magnetic field through the coil, e.g. switch an electromagnet on or off, or vary its current
A bar magnet is held still inside a coil connected to a sensitive voltmeter. What does the meter show and why? :: Zero – there is no relative movement (and no changing field), so no voltage is induced
What happens to the meter when a bar magnet is pushed into a coil? :: The needle deflects briefly in one direction – a voltage is induced only while the magnet is moving
What happens to the meter when the magnet is pulled out of the coil again? :: The needle deflects in the opposite direction
What happens to the meter reading if the magnet is pushed in and then stops moving? :: It returns to zero straight away – the voltage lasts only while the magnetic field is changing
Why does a faster-moving magnet induce a larger voltage? :: The coil's wire cuts the magnetic field lines at a faster rate
List the ways to increase the size of an induced voltage. :: Move the magnet (or coil) faster ;; Use a stronger magnet ;; Use more turns of wire on the coil ;; Use a coil with a larger area ;; Add a soft iron core to concentrate the field
Why does using more turns of wire increase the induced voltage? :: A voltage is induced in every turn and the voltages add together, because the turns are connected in series
Why does a soft iron core increase the induced voltage? :: The iron concentrates the magnetic field lines through the coil, so the field changes more strongly
How can you reverse the direction of the induced voltage? :: Reverse the direction of movement (push in instead of pull out), or swap the poles of the magnet ;; Doing both at once leaves it unchanged
What kind of voltage is induced if a magnet is moved in and out of a coil repeatedly? :: An alternating voltage (AC) – it changes direction every time the movement reverses
A coil that is not part of a complete circuit has a magnet pushed into it. Is a voltage induced? Does a current flow? :: A voltage is still induced across its ends, but no current flows because the circuit is not complete
How can a voltage be induced in a single straight wire? :: Move the wire across a magnetic field (e.g. between the poles of a magnet) so that it cuts the field lines, with its ends connected to a sensitive meter
In which direction should a wire be moved through a magnetic field to induce the largest voltage? :: At right angles to the field lines. Moving it parallel to the field lines cuts none, so induces no voltage
Describe an investigation into the factors affecting induced voltage. :: Connect a coil to a voltmeter or data logger ;; Push a bar magnet into the coil at a controlled speed and record the maximum reading ;; Change only one variable (speed, magnet strength or number of turns), keep the others the same ;; Repeat and take an average
Compare the motor effect and electromagnetic induction. :: **Motor effect:** current + magnetic field → force and movement (electrical → kinetic) ;; **Induction:** movement + magnetic field → voltage and current (kinetic → electrical)
Name some uses of electromagnetic induction. :: Generators in power stations, wind turbines and bicycle dynamos ;; Transformers ;; Moving-coil microphones ;; Wireless phone chargers and induction hobs
How does a moving-coil microphone use electromagnetic induction? :: Sound waves make a diaphragm vibrate, moving a coil attached to it (or a magnet) relative to a magnet, which induces a small alternating voltage with the same pattern as the sound
Why is induced voltage described as a form of 'generator effect'? :: Because moving a conductor in a magnetic field generates electricity from kinetic energy – the opposite energy transfer to the motor effect
## Lenz's law and energy
State Lenz's law. :: The direction of an induced voltage (and current) is always such that it **opposes the change** that produces it
C: By Lenz's law, an induced current produces a magnetic field that {{c1::opposes}} the {{c2::change}} that caused it.
A north pole is pushed towards a coil. What pole forms at the end of the coil nearest the magnet? :: A north pole – it repels the approaching magnet, opposing the push
A north pole is pulled away from a coil. What pole forms at the end of the coil nearest the magnet? :: A south pole – it attracts the magnet, opposing its removal
How does Lenz's law show that energy is conserved? :: You must do work against the opposing force to move the magnet, and that work is transferred to electrical energy. If the induced current helped the movement, energy would be created from nothing, which is impossible
Why is it harder to push a magnet into a coil that is part of a complete circuit than into an open coil? :: In a complete circuit a current flows, and its magnetic field opposes the magnet's movement. In an open coil there is no current, so no opposing force
A strong magnet is dropped down a vertical copper tube and falls very slowly. Explain why. :: The moving magnet induces circulating currents in the copper ;; By Lenz's law their magnetic field opposes the magnet's motion ;; This gives an upward force, so the magnet falls more slowly than it would through air
Why would a magnet stop falling slowly if the copper tube had a long slit cut down its side? :: The slit stops the induced current flowing around the tube, so the opposing magnetic field is much weaker
A magnet is pushed faster into a coil. Why is more force needed? :: A bigger voltage and current are induced, so the opposing force is larger, and more work is done each second
What happens to the energy transferred when you push a magnet into a coil connected to a lamp? :: Your work (kinetic) → electrical energy in the coil → light and heat in the lamp
Fleming's right-hand rule :: Thumb = Motion of the wire, First finger = Field (N to S), Second finger = induced Current. It gives the direction of induced current in a wire moving through a field ;; In the exam use Lenz's law (opposes the change) to explain direction
## AC generators and dynamos
Name the main parts of a simple AC generator. :: A coil of wire (armature), a magnet or magnets (poles N and S), slip rings, carbon brushes, and connections to an external circuit
Explain how a simple AC generator produces a voltage. :: The coil is turned in the magnetic field, so its sides cut the field lines ;; This induces a voltage across the coil ;; The voltage is delivered to the circuit through slip rings and brushes
What is the job of the slip rings? :: To keep the rotating coil connected to the external circuit all the time, so the wires don't twist
What is the job of the carbon brushes? :: They press against the slip rings and carry current between the rotating coil and the fixed external circuit
Why are carbon brushes used? :: Carbon conducts electricity and is slippery (low friction), so it makes good contact without wearing the rings away too quickly
Why does the output of a simple generator alternate? :: Each side of the coil moves up through the field, then down. The direction in which it cuts the field lines reverses every half turn, so the induced voltage reverses
Describe the voltage–time graph for an AC generator. :: A repeating wave (sine curve) that goes positive, then negative ;; One complete cycle for each complete rotation of the coil
When is the voltage from an AC generator largest? :: When the coil is parallel to the field lines – its sides cut the lines at right angles and cut the most lines per second
When is the voltage from an AC generator zero? :: When the coil is perpendicular to the field lines (facing the magnet poles) – its sides move along the field lines and cut none
How is the frequency of the AC output related to the rotation of the coil? :: The frequency (Hz) equals the number of complete rotations per second – one rotation gives one cycle
What happens to the graph if the coil is spun faster? :: Higher peak voltage (lines cut faster) and a shorter period (higher frequency), so more cycles in a given time
What happens to the graph if the coil has more turns but spins at the same speed? :: The peak voltage is larger but the frequency is unchanged
List the ways to increase the output voltage of a generator. :: Spin the coil faster ;; Use stronger magnets ;; Use more turns on the coil ;; Use a coil with a larger area ;; Use a soft iron core
How does a DC generator differ from an AC generator? :: It uses a split-ring commutator instead of slip rings, so the output always flows in one direction (but rises and falls in pulses)
How does a bicycle dynamo light a lamp? :: The wheel turns a magnet next to a fixed coil, changing the magnetic field through the coil, which induces an alternating voltage that lights the lamp
Why does a bicycle lamp get brighter when you pedal faster and dim when you slow down? :: Faster rotation cuts the field lines more quickly, inducing a larger voltage and current. Slower rotation induces a smaller voltage; if you stop, no voltage is induced and the lamp goes out
Describe the energy transfers in a power station generator. :: Fuel (chemical or nuclear) → thermal → steam turns a turbine (kinetic) → generator → electrical energy (with some wasted as heat)
How do wind turbines and hydroelectric stations generate electricity? :: Moving wind or water turns a turbine, which turns a generator, using electromagnetic induction
## Transformers
What are the parts of a transformer? :: A primary coil, a secondary coil, and a laminated soft iron core that links the two coils
Explain how a transformer works, step by step. :: Alternating current in the primary coil produces a changing magnetic field ;; The iron core carries this changing field through the secondary coil ;; The changing field induces an alternating voltage across the secondary coil
Why must the supply to a transformer be AC? :: Only a changing current makes a changing magnetic field. With DC the field is steady, so no voltage is induced in the secondary coil (apart from a brief pulse when switched on or off)
Why is the core made of iron? :: Iron concentrates the magnetic field and guides it from the primary coil through the secondary coil
Why is soft iron used rather than steel? :: Soft iron is easy to magnetise and demagnetise, so it keeps up with the alternating field and wastes little energy
Why is the core laminated (made of thin insulated sheets)? :: To reduce eddy currents, which would heat the core and waste energy
What is a step-up transformer? :: A transformer that increases the voltage – the secondary coil has more turns than the primary
What is a step-down transformer? :: A transformer that decreases the voltage – the secondary coil has fewer turns than the primary
What does a step-up transformer do to the current? :: It reduces it: the power stays about the same, so if the voltage goes up the current goes down
Write the turns–voltage equation for a transformer. :: Vp ÷ Vs = Np ÷ Ns ;; (voltage across primary ÷ voltage across secondary = turns on primary ÷ turns on secondary)
Write the power equation for an ideal (100% efficient) transformer. :: Vp × Ip = Vs × Is ;; (power in = power out)
Write the equation for the efficiency of a transformer. :: efficiency = (power out ÷ power in) × 100%
Why are real transformers not 100% efficient? :: Heat in the coils (resistance) ;; Eddy currents in the core ;; Some magnetic field leaks and doesn't pass through the secondary ;; Energy used to keep magnetising and demagnetising the core
How are energy losses in transformers reduced? :: Thick, low-resistance copper wire ;; Laminated core to cut eddy currents ;; Soft iron core ;; Winding coils over each other on the same core to cut leakage
Where are transformers used in everyday life? :: Phone and laptop chargers (step down 230 V), power stations and the National Grid, substations, and mains adaptors for appliances
## Power transmission and the National Grid
Write the equation for electrical power. :: power = current × voltage ;; P = I × V
Write the equation for power wasted as heat in a cable. :: power loss = I² × R ;; (current squared × resistance of the cable)
Why is electricity transmitted at very high voltage? :: For the same power (P = IV), a high voltage means a low current. A low current means much less heating in the cables (power loss = I²R), so less energy is wasted
If the current in a cable is halved, what happens to the power wasted? :: It falls to one quarter, because power loss depends on I²
Why not simply use thicker cables with less resistance instead of high voltage? :: Thick cables are very expensive, heavy and need stronger pylons
Why is AC used for the National Grid? :: Its voltage can easily be stepped up and down with transformers (a DC supply cannot use a transformer)
Describe how electricity gets from a power station to a home. :: Generated at about 25 kV → step-up transformer to 275–400 kV → transmission lines (pylons) → step-down transformers at substations (132 kV, 33 kV, 11 kV) → 230 V to homes
Why is the voltage stepped down before reaching homes? :: High voltages are dangerous and unsuitable for household use. Appliances are designed for 230 V
What is the voltage and frequency of UK mains electricity? :: 230 V, 50 Hz
How fast must a generator coil spin to produce a 50 Hz output? :: 50 rotations per second (3000 per minute), if the generator has one pair of poles
## Calculations
A transformer has 500 turns on the primary coil and 100 on the secondary. The primary voltage is 230 V. What is the secondary voltage? :: Vs = Vp × Ns ÷ Np = 230 × 100 ÷ 500 = **46 V** (step-down)
A step-up transformer has 50 turns on the primary coil. How many turns are needed on the secondary to change 12 V to 240 V? :: Ns = Np × Vs ÷ Vp = 50 × 240 ÷ 12 = **1000 turns**
A transformer changes 230 V to 12 V. The primary coil has 920 turns. How many turns does the secondary have? :: Ns = 920 × 12 ÷ 230 = **48 turns**

A transformer takes 100 W in and gives 96 W out. What is its efficiency? :: 96 ÷ 100 × 100% = **96%**

The voltage is raised from 25 kV to 400 kV (16 times higher). How many times smaller is the power lost as heat in the cables? :: The current is 16 times smaller, so the loss (I²R) is 16² = **256 times smaller**

How do you work out the turns ratio for a transformer? :: Turns ratio = Np : Ns = Vp : Vs ;; e.g. 230 V to 11.5 V is 20 : 1 (a step-down transformer)
## Exam-style explanations
Explain why a voltmeter connected to a coil shows a reading when a magnet is pushed into the coil, and returns to zero when the magnet stops. :: The coil's wire cuts the magnetic field lines as the magnet moves (the magnetic field through the coil changes) ;; This induces a voltage across the coil ;; When the magnet stops, the field no longer changes, so no voltage is induced
Explain why a transformer does not work with a direct current supply. :: A DC current gives a steady magnetic field in the core ;; The field through the secondary coil does not change ;; So no voltage is induced in the secondary coil
Explain why a step-up transformer is used at a power station. :: It increases the voltage and reduces the current ;; Lower current means less energy lost as heat in the transmission cables (P = I²R) ;; So transmission is more efficient
Describe how a generator produces an alternating voltage. :: A coil rotates between the poles of a magnet ;; The sides of the coil cut the magnetic field lines, inducing a voltage ;; Every half turn the sides cut the field in the opposite direction, so the voltage reverses ;; Slip rings and brushes connect the coil to the circuit
Suggest how the voltage output of a generator can be doubled. :: Double the speed of rotation, or double the number of turns on the coil (also: stronger magnets or a larger coil)
Explain why the output from a transformer is zero when the primary current is constant. :: A constant current produces a steady magnetic field ;; No change in field means no induced voltage in the secondary coil
Explain why the lamp in a bicycle dynamo goes out when the cyclist stops. :: When the wheel stops, the magnet stops moving relative to the coil ;; The magnetic field through the coil no longer changes ;; No voltage is induced, so no current flows
Explain how Lenz's law applies when a magnet is pulled out of a coil. :: The induced current flows in the direction that opposes the removal of the magnet ;; The end of the coil nearest the magnet becomes the opposite pole and attracts it ;; Work is done against this force and transferred to electrical energy

# 7 Radioactivity and particles
Describe the structure of an atom. :: Small positive nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons in shells
What are isotopes? :: Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons
What is an alpha particle and how penetrating/ionising is it? :: 2 protons + 2 neutrons (helium nucleus); stopped by paper or a few cm of air; strongly ionising
What is a beta particle and how penetrating is it? :: A fast electron from the nucleus; stopped by a few mm of aluminium; moderately ionising
What is gamma radiation and how penetrating is it? :: High-frequency EM wave; reduced by thick lead or concrete; weakly ionising
What happens in alpha decay? :: Mass number decreases by 4, atomic number decreases by 2
What happens in beta decay? :: A neutron becomes a proton and an electron; mass number unchanged, atomic number increases by 1
How can radiation be detected? :: Photographic film or a Geiger–Müller tube
Name sources of background radiation. :: Radon gas, rocks, cosmic rays, food and drink, medical (X-rays), nuclear industry
What is activity and what is its unit? :: The number of nuclei decaying per second; becquerel (Bq)
What is half-life? :: The time taken for half the undecayed nuclei in a sample to decay (or activity to halve)
Why is radioactive decay described as random? :: You cannot predict which nucleus will decay or when
Give uses of radioactivity. :: Medical tracers, radiotherapy, sterilising equipment, smoke alarms (alpha), thickness gauges (beta), carbon dating
What is the difference between irradiation and contamination? :: Irradiation – exposed to radiation from an outside source ;; Contamination – radioactive material gets on or inside the body (more dangerous for alpha)
What is nuclear fission? :: A large nucleus (uranium-235) absorbs a neutron, becomes unstable and splits into two smaller daughter nuclei, releasing 2–3 neutrons and energy
What is nuclear fusion? :: Two light nuclei join to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy – happens in stars
## 7a Units
In what units can half-life be given? :: Any unit of time – seconds, minutes, hours, days or years (use whatever fits the isotope)
## 7b Atoms and nuclei
Give the relative charge and mass of a proton, neutron and electron. :: Proton: charge +1, mass 1 ;; Neutron: charge 0, mass 1 ;; Electron: charge −1, mass about 1/2000 (negligible)
Why is an atom neutral overall? :: It has the same number of electrons (negative) as protons (positive)
What is the atomic (proton) number? :: The number of protons in the nucleus (symbol Z)
What is the mass (nucleon) number? :: The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus (symbol A)
In the symbol ¹⁴₆C, what do the two numbers mean? :: Top (14) = mass (nucleon) number – protons + neutrons ;; Bottom (6) = atomic (proton) number – protons ;; So carbon-14 has 6 protons and 8 neutrons
Calculate: how many protons and neutrons are in a nucleus of ²³⁵₉₂U? :: Protons = 92 ;; Neutrons = 235 − 92 = **143**
Give an example of a pair of isotopes. :: Carbon-12 and carbon-14 (both 6 protons; 6 and 8 neutrons) ;; or uranium-235 and uranium-238
## 7b Radioactive decay and nuclear equations
C: Alpha particles, beta particles and gamma rays are {{c1::ionising}} radiations emitted from {{c2::unstable nuclei}} in a {{c3::random}} process.
What does ionising mean? :: The radiation knocks electrons out of atoms, turning them into charged ions
Why is alpha radiation the most ionising? :: It has a large charge (+2) and mass, so it collides with many atoms and loses its energy quickly – which is also why it has a short range
What are the charges of alpha, beta and gamma radiation? :: Alpha +2 ;; Beta −1 ;; Gamma 0 (no charge)
Write the nuclear symbols for an alpha particle, a beta particle, a gamma ray and a neutron. :: Alpha ⁴₂He (or ⁴₂α) ;; Beta ⁰₋₁e (or ⁰₋₁β) ;; Gamma ⁰₀γ ;; Neutron ¹₀n
What happens to the mass and atomic numbers when a nucleus emits gamma radiation? :: Neither changes – the nucleus just loses energy
What happens to the mass and atomic numbers when a nucleus emits a neutron? :: Mass number decreases by 1 ;; Atomic number unchanged (it becomes a different isotope of the same element)
How do you balance a nuclear equation? :: The mass numbers (top) must add up to the same total on each side ;; The atomic numbers (bottom, the charge) must add up to the same total on each side
Complete the alpha decay: ²²⁶₈₈Ra → ? + ⁴₂He :: ²²²₈₆Rn (radon) ;; 226 = 222 + 4 and 88 = 86 + 2
Complete the beta decay: ¹⁴₆C → ? + ⁰₋₁e :: ¹⁴₇N (nitrogen) ;; 14 = 14 + 0 and 6 = 7 + (−1)
Why does a nucleus often emit gamma rays after alpha or beta decay? :: The new nucleus is left with excess energy, which it gives out as a gamma ray
Why does alpha or beta decay change the element? :: The number of protons (atomic number) changes, and the element is defined by its proton number
## 7b Detecting radiation and background
What is meant by a corrected count rate? :: The measured count rate minus the background count rate
How does a Geiger–Müller tube detect radiation? :: Radiation entering the tube ionises the gas inside, producing a pulse of current that is counted by a counter or ratemeter
How does photographic film detect radiation? :: Radiation darkens the film – the more radiation, the darker it gets (used in film badges worn by workers)
## 7b Practical: penetrating power
Describe how to investigate the penetrating power of alpha, beta and gamma radiation. :: Measure the background count rate with a GM tube and counter ;; Place the source a fixed short distance from the tube and measure the count rate ;; Place sheets of paper, then aluminium (a few mm), then lead between them and measure the count rate each time ;; Subtract background; a big drop shows the radiation has been absorbed
In the penetration practical, how do you know a source emits alpha? :: The count rate falls to (almost) background when a sheet of paper is put in the way
In the penetration practical, how do you know a source emits beta? :: Paper has little effect, but a few mm of aluminium reduce the count rate to (almost) background
In the penetration practical, how do you know a source emits gamma? :: The count rate is only reduced by thick lead (or concrete); paper and aluminium have little effect
Give safety precautions when using radioactive sources in the lab. :: Handle with tongs, never with fingers ;; Keep the source pointing away from people ;; Keep it as far away as possible and use it for as short a time as possible ;; Return it to its lead-lined box straight after use
## 7b Activity and half-life
What happens to the activity of a radioactive source over time? :: It decreases, because there are fewer undecayed nuclei left
C: Half-life is different for different radioactive {{c1::isotopes}} – from fractions of a second to billions of years.
How do you find the half-life from a graph of activity against time? :: Read the starting activity, halve it, and read the time it takes to fall to that value ;; Repeat from another point (e.g. half to a quarter) and take the average
Calculate: a sample has an activity of 800 Bq and a half-life of 2 hours. What is its activity after 6 hours? :: 6 ÷ 2 = 3 half-lives ;; 800 → 400 → 200 → **100 Bq**
Calculate: the activity of a sample falls from 400 Bq to 50 Bq in 24 days. What is its half-life? :: 400 → 200 → 100 → 50 is 3 half-lives ;; 24 ÷ 3 = **8 days**
What fraction of a sample remains undecayed after 3 half-lives? :: ½ × ½ × ½ = **1/8**
Calculate: a sample contains 1000 undecayed nuclei. How many have decayed after 2 half-lives? :: Remaining = 1000 → 500 → 250 ;; Decayed = 1000 − 250 = **750**
How should background radiation be dealt with in half-life calculations? :: Subtract the background count rate from every reading before halving
Why can you not use half-life to predict when one particular nucleus will decay? :: Decay is random – half-life only describes what happens on average to a very large number of nuclei
## 7b Uses of radioactivity
Why do medical tracers use gamma emitters with a short half-life? :: Gamma passes out of the body to be detected and is only weakly ionising ;; A short half-life (hours) means it doesn't stay radioactive in the body for long, but lasts long enough for the test
How is radiotherapy used to treat cancer? :: Beams of gamma rays (or X-rays) from different directions are focused on the tumour to kill the cancer cells, while limiting the dose to healthy tissue
Why is gamma radiation used to sterilise medical equipment and food? :: It kills bacteria and other microbes, and it is penetrating enough to pass through the packaging, so items can be sterilised once sealed
Explain how a smoke alarm uses an alpha source. :: The alpha particles ionise the air, so a small current flows across a gap ;; Smoke absorbs the alpha particles, so the current falls ;; The fall in current sets off the alarm
How does a beta source control the thickness of paper or aluminium sheet? :: A beta source is on one side and a detector on the other ;; If the sheet gets thicker, less radiation gets through and the count rate falls ;; The rollers are adjusted to keep the count rate (thickness) constant
Why is beta, not alpha or gamma, used to monitor paper thickness? :: Alpha would be stopped completely by the paper ;; Gamma would pass straight through with almost no change ;; Beta is partly absorbed, so the count rate changes with thickness
How are radioactive tracers used to find leaks in underground pipes? :: A gamma emitter is added to the fluid in the pipe ;; A detector above ground finds a high count rate where the fluid has leaked out
How does carbon dating work? :: Living things take in carbon-14 while alive ;; After death no more is taken in and the carbon-14 decays (half-life about 5700 years) ;; Measuring the activity remaining shows how long ago the organism died
## 7b Dangers of ionising radiation
How can ionising radiation harm living organisms? :: It can damage or kill cells and tissue (burns, radiation sickness) ;; It can damage DNA, causing mutations that may lead to cancer
Why is an alpha source most dangerous inside the body but less dangerous outside it? :: Outside – alpha is stopped by the skin (dead outer layer) or air ;; Inside – it is very strongly ionising, so all its energy is absorbed by nearby cells, causing damage
Why is a gamma source most dangerous outside the body? :: It is very penetrating, so it can pass through the skin and reach internal organs
What problems arise from the disposal of radioactive waste? :: Some waste stays radioactive for thousands of years ;; It must be stored safely so it can't leak into water supplies or the environment, or be stolen
How are the risks of radioactive waste reduced? :: Seal it in containers (e.g. glass, steel, concrete) and store it securely, often deep underground in stable rock, and monitor it
How can workers reduce their exposure to ionising radiation? :: Increase distance from the source, reduce time of exposure, use shielding (lead, concrete), and wear a film badge to monitor the dose
## 7c Nuclear fission (Paper 2)
C: Nuclear reactions, including {{c1::fission}}, {{c2::fusion}} and {{c3::radioactive decay}}, can be a source of energy.
In nuclear fission, in what form is the energy released? :: As kinetic energy of the fission products (the daughter nuclei and neutrons), which heats the reactor
Write a typical fission equation for uranium-235. :: ²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴¹₅₆Ba + ⁹²₃₆Kr + 3 ¹₀n + energy
Explain (3 marks) how a chain reaction is set up. :: A neutron is absorbed by a U-235 nucleus, which splits ;; The fission releases 2–3 neutrons ;; These neutrons are absorbed by other U-235 nuclei, causing more fissions, and so on
How do control rods control the rate of fission? :: They absorb neutrons (e.g. boron or cadmium) ;; Lowering them further in absorbs more neutrons, slowing the reaction; raising them speeds it up ;; Aim: on average exactly one neutron from each fission causes another fission
How does the moderator slow down neutrons? :: The fast neutrons collide with the atoms of the moderator (graphite or water) and lose kinetic energy
Why must neutrons be slowed down in a reactor? :: Slow (thermal) neutrons are much more likely to be absorbed by U-235 nuclei and cause fission
What is the role of shielding around a nuclear reactor? :: Thick concrete (and steel) absorbs the neutrons and gamma radiation produced, protecting workers and the environment
What would happen in a reactor if the control rods were removed? :: The chain reaction would become uncontrolled – the number of fissions would increase very rapidly, overheating the reactor (possible meltdown/explosion)
What does the coolant do in a nuclear reactor? :: It carries the thermal energy from the reactor core to a heat exchanger, where it turns water into steam to drive the turbines
Why is the waste from a nuclear reactor radioactive? :: The daughter nuclei produced by fission are radioactive
## 7c Nuclear fusion (Paper 2)
C: Nuclear fusion is the creation of larger nuclei from smaller nuclei, resulting in a loss of {{c1::mass}} accompanied by a release of {{c2::energy}}.
What is the energy source of stars? :: Nuclear fusion – mainly hydrogen nuclei fusing to form helium
What is the difference between nuclear fission and nuclear fusion? :: **Fission** – a large nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei ;; **Fusion** – two small (light) nuclei join to form a larger nucleus ;; Both release energy
Explain (3 marks) why fusion only happens at very high temperatures and pressures. :: Nuclei are positively charged, so they repel each other (electrostatic repulsion) ;; Very high temperatures give them enough kinetic energy to get close enough to fuse ;; High pressure (density) makes collisions frequent enough

## 8b Motion in the universe

## 8c Stellar evolution
How does a star form? :: A nebula (cloud of gas, mainly hydrogen, and dust) is pulled together by gravity ;; It heats up to form a protostar ;; When the core is hot and dense enough, hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium and it becomes a main sequence star
Why is a main sequence star stable? :: The inward force of gravity is balanced by the outward pressure from the energy released by fusion in the core
What happens when a Sun-sized star leaves the main sequence? :: The hydrogen in its core runs out ;; It swells and its surface cools to become a red giant
How does a red giant become a white dwarf? :: The outer layers drift away into space, leaving a small, hot, dense core – a white dwarf, which slowly cools and fades

What is a supernova? :: The huge explosion of a red supergiant at the end of its life, which scatters elements (including the heaviest ones) into space
What is a black hole? :: The remains of a very massive star, so dense that its gravity is strong enough to stop even light escaping
What is a neutron star? :: The extremely dense core left behind after a supernova, made almost entirely of neutrons
C: Stars can be classified by their {{c1::colour}}, which depends on their {{c2::surface temperature}}.

## 8c The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram

Where are red giants and red supergiants on an HR diagram? :: Top right – cool but very bright
Where are white dwarfs on an HR diagram? :: Bottom left – hot but dim
Why are red giants very bright even though they are cool? :: They are very large, so they have a huge surface area giving out light
Why are white dwarfs dim even though they are very hot? :: They are very small, so they have a tiny surface area
How does the Sun's position on the HR diagram change during its life? :: It stays on the main sequence for billions of years, then moves to the top right as a red giant, then to the bottom left as a white dwarf
## 8d Cosmology (Paper 2)
What does the Big Bang theory say about the past of the universe? :: The universe began about 13.8 billion years ago from a tiny, extremely hot and dense point, and has been expanding and cooling ever since
Write the full redshift equation. :: Δλ ÷ λ₀ = Δf ÷ f₀ = v ÷ c
In the redshift equation, what do the symbols mean? :: Δλ – change in wavelength; λ₀ – reference (laboratory) wavelength ;; Δf – change in frequency; f₀ – reference frequency ;; v – speed of the galaxy away from us; c – speed of light (3 × 10⁸ m/s)
Why is light from a galaxy moving away from us red-shifted? :: The source is moving away, so the waves are stretched (Doppler effect) – the observed wavelength is longer and the frequency lower, shifted towards the red end of the spectrum
How is redshift measured? :: The dark absorption lines in a galaxy's spectrum are compared with those from the same element in the laboratory – they are shifted towards the red end (longer wavelength)

How is the redshift of a galaxy related to its distance from us? :: More distant galaxies have a greater redshift, so they are moving away faster
Explain (4 marks) how redshift provides evidence for the Big Bang. :: Light from almost all distant galaxies is red-shifted, so they are moving away from us ;; More distant galaxies show greater redshift, so they are moving away faster ;; This means the universe is expanding ;; Tracing this back, the universe must once have been very small, hot and dense – starting from a Big Bang
Explain how the cosmic microwave background radiation (CMBR) supports the Big Bang theory. :: The Big Bang theory predicts high-energy radiation from the hot early universe ;; As the universe expanded and cooled, its wavelength was stretched into microwaves ;; CMBR is detected from all directions with the predicted properties ;; The Steady State theory cannot explain it
What is blueshift? :: A decrease in observed wavelength (shift towards the blue end) because a source is moving towards us – e.g. the Andromeda galaxy

## Equations: recall and rearrange
What should you do if you cannot remember whether an equation is given? :: Learn every equation in the specification – the paper only prints a few, and "know and use" equations are never given
Which equations are usually printed on the formulae page of 4PH1 papers (but are still worth learning)? :: v² = u² + 2as ;; f = 1/T ;; P = W/t ;; E = I × V × t ;; p₁V₁ = p₂V₂ and p₁/T₁ = p₂/T₂ ;; F = (mv − mu)/t ;; v = 2πr/T ;; ΔQ = m × c × ΔT
Rearrange V = I × R to find I and R. :: I = V ÷ R ;; R = V ÷ I
Rearrange P = I × V to find the current. :: I = P ÷ V
Rearrange KE = ½mv² to find v. :: v = √(2 × KE ÷ m)
Rearrange GPE = m × g × h to find h. :: h = GPE ÷ (m × g)
Rearrange ρ = m ÷ V to find mass and volume. :: m = ρ × V ;; V = m ÷ ρ
Rearrange ΔQ = m × c × ΔT to find ΔT. :: ΔT = ΔQ ÷ (m × c)
Calculate: a 1000 kg car has 200 000 J of kinetic energy. How fast is it going? :: v² = 2 × KE ÷ m = 2 × 200 000 ÷ 1000 = 400 ;; v = **20 m/s**
## Calculation technique
What steps should you follow in a physics calculation? :: 1. Write the equation ;; 2. Convert to standard units (m, kg, s, A, V…) ;; 3. Substitute the numbers ;; 4. Rearrange and calculate ;; 5. Give the answer with a unit and sensible significant figures
How many significant figures should you give in an answer? :: The same as the data in the question (usually 2 or 3) – and don't round until the final answer
Why should you always show your working? :: Method marks can be awarded for the correct equation and substitution even if the final answer is wrong
In KE = ½mv², what must you remember to square? :: Only the speed (v), not the mass or the ½ – work out v² first
How do you calculate the gradient of a graph accurately? :: Draw a large triangle on the straight line (or tangent), using more than half the line ;; gradient = change in y-value ÷ change in x-value, with units (y unit ÷ x unit)
What does a straight line through the origin on a graph show? :: The two quantities are directly proportional
How should you plot a graph in a practical? :: Independent variable on the x-axis, dependent on the y-axis ;; Label axes with quantity and unit ;; Use a sensible scale filling over half the grid ;; Plot points accurately (small crosses) ;; Draw a smooth line or curve of best fit
## Practical skills
What is the independent variable? :: The variable you deliberately change
What is the dependent variable? :: The variable you measure (it depends on the independent variable)
What are control variables? :: Variables kept the same so the test is fair and only the independent variable affects the result
Why are readings repeated and a mean taken? :: To reduce the effect of random errors and to spot anomalous results, making the result more reliable
What is an anomalous result and what should you do with it? :: A result that does not fit the pattern of the others ;; Check or repeat it, and leave it out when calculating the mean
What is a random error? :: An unpredictable variation between readings (e.g. reaction time) – reduced by repeating and averaging
What is a systematic error? :: An error that shifts every reading by the same amount in the same direction (e.g. a zero error on a balance) – repeating does not remove it
What is a zero error? :: When an instrument does not read zero when it should (e.g. a newtonmeter reading 0.2 N with nothing on it) – correct it by subtracting the offset from every reading
What is parallax error and how is it avoided? :: An error from reading a scale at an angle ;; Read with your eye level with the scale, perpendicular to it
What is the resolution of an instrument? :: The smallest change it can measure (e.g. 1 mm on a ruler, 0.01 g on a balance)
What is the difference between accurate and precise results? :: Accurate – close to the true value ;; Precise – repeated readings are close to each other (little spread)
How many values of the independent variable should you use? :: At least five, spread evenly over a suitable range, so a clear pattern or graph can be seen
How do you calculate a mean from repeat readings? :: Add the readings (leaving out any anomalies) and divide by the number of readings used

# 1 Identifying requirements
What is user-centred design? :: Designing with the needs, wants and values of the end user at the centre, involving them throughout the process
What is a design brief? :: A short statement describing the problem/need, the target user and what is to be designed
What is a design specification? :: A list of measurable criteria (requirements) the product must meet – used to evaluate the final design
What does ACCESS FM stand for? :: Aesthetics, Cost, Customer, Environment, Size, Safety, Function, Materials
What is primary research? :: Research you collect yourself – interviews, questionnaires, observations, product analysis, testing
What is secondary research? :: Using existing information – books, internet, magazines, data from others
Why is it important to consider ergonomics? :: So the product is comfortable, safe and efficient to use – suits the human body and interaction
What is anthropometrics? :: Data about the sizes of the human body, used to design products to fit users (often 5th–95th percentile)
Why do designers design for the 5th to 95th percentile? :: It covers 90% of the population – designing for extremes is impractical and costly
What is inclusive design? :: Designing products that can be used by as many people as possible, including those with disabilities, the elderly and children

## Users, stakeholders and context
What is a stakeholder in design? :: Anyone with an interest in, or affected by, a product – users, clients, manufacturers, retailers, maintainers, the wider community
C: The {{c1::client}} commissions or pays for a product; the {{c2::end user}} is the person who actually uses it.
Why must designers consider the context in which a product will be used? :: The environment (indoors/outdoors, wet, hot), who uses it, how often and where all affect the choice of materials, size, safety features and aesthetics
Give five methods of finding out what users need. :: Interviews, questionnaires/surveys, focus groups, observing users, user trials of existing products, product analysis, anthropometric data
What is a focus group? :: A small group of target users discussing a product or idea in depth, led by a researcher – gives detailed qualitative opinions
Give one advantage and one disadvantage of a questionnaire. :: **Advantage** – reaches many people quickly; easy to turn into charts/statistics ;; **Disadvantage** – answers can be shallow, rushed or untruthful; limited to the questions asked
Give one advantage and one disadvantage of an interview. :: **Advantage** – detailed answers; can ask follow-up questions ;; **Disadvantage** – time-consuming, small sample, may be biased by interviewer
Why is observing users a useful research method? :: Shows how people really use a product (problems, workarounds, misuse) that they might not mention in a survey
C: {{c1::Closed}} questions have fixed answers (yes/no, tick boxes) and give {{c2::quantitative}} data; {{c3::open}} questions let people answer in their own words and give {{c4::qualitative}} data.
What is a target market? :: The specific group of people a product is aimed at, defined by age, gender, income, lifestyle, interests or location
What is market segmentation? :: Dividing a market into groups of users with similar needs or characteristics (e.g. age, income, lifestyle) so products can be targeted
What is a user persona (user profile)? :: A fictional but realistic description of a typical user – age, job, lifestyle, needs, likes – used to keep their needs in mind while designing
What is a design context? :: The situation or setting in which a design problem exists, e.g. 'storage in a small student bedroom'
What should a design brief include? :: The problem/need, the context, the target user/client, what is to be designed and any key constraints
What is a design constraint? :: A limit the design must work within, e.g. budget, size, materials, time, manufacturing processes available, regulations
Why must specification points be measurable/testable? :: So the final product can be objectively tested and evaluated against them – you can prove whether each point has been met
Give a poor and a good specification point for weight. :: **Poor** – 'It must be light' ;; **Good** – 'It must weigh less than 400 g so a child can carry it'
What is the difference between a user need and a user want? :: A need is essential for the product to work for the user (function, safety); a want is desirable but not essential (colour, extra features)
What is a gap in the market? :: A user need or product type that existing products do not yet meet – an opportunity for a new product
Explain why a designer should identify the needs of the user before designing. (3 marks) :: Product will meet the user's real needs **so** it is more likely to sell ;; Avoids wasting time/money on ideas users don't want ;; Gives clear criteria for the specification and for evaluating designs
How does market research help a designer? :: Identifies competitors and price points, user preferences and trends, gaps in the market and whether there is demand

## Ergonomics, anthropometrics and inclusive design
C: {{c1::Anthropometrics}} is the measurement of the human body; {{c2::ergonomics}} is using that data to design products that are comfortable, safe and efficient for people to use.
What is a percentile? :: The value below which a given percentage of the population falls, e.g. the 95th percentile height is taller than 95% of people
Which percentile should be used for the height of a doorway, and why? :: 95th percentile (or above) – so that almost all people, including tall users, can pass through
Which percentile should be used for the reach to a control or shelf, and why? :: 5th percentile – so that even small users can reach it (larger users can reach it too)
Which body measurement is used to set the seat height of a chair? :: Popliteal height (floor to the back of the knee when seated)
Give examples of ergonomic features on a hand tool. :: Handle shaped to fit the hand (grip diameter from anthropometric data), soft-grip material, non-slip texture, balanced weight, controls within reach of the thumb
Give features that make a product more accessible to elderly or disabled users. :: Large, high-contrast buttons/text, lever instead of knob handles, braille/tactile markings, audio feedback, lightweight, easy-grip
What is universal design? :: Designing products that are usable by everyone, to the greatest extent possible, without the need for adaptation (similar to inclusive design)
What is exclusive design? :: Designing for a specific group of users with particular needs (e.g. a product for wheelchair users) rather than for everyone
Why is anthropometric data often presented in tables by age and gender? :: Body sizes differ between groups, so designers choose the data for their target user group

# 2 Learning from existing products
What is product analysis? :: Examining existing products to understand function, materials, manufacture, aesthetics and user needs – to inform new designs
What is iterative design? :: A cyclical process of designing, prototyping, testing and evaluating, then improving, repeated many times
What is market pull? :: Products developed in response to consumer demand/needs
What is technology push? :: New technology/materials drive the development of new products
What is planned obsolescence? :: Designing a product to have a limited lifespan so consumers must replace it
What is functional obsolescence vs style obsolescence? :: Functional – product stops working or can't be repaired/updated ;; Style – product goes out of fashion
What are Dieter Rams' principles of good design (examples)? :: Innovative, useful, aesthetic, understandable, unobtrusive, honest, long-lasting, thorough, environmentally friendly, as little design as possible

## Investigating existing products
List headings for analysing an existing product. :: Function, user, aesthetics (form, colour, texture), ergonomics, materials, manufacturing processes, cost, safety, sustainability, how it could be improved
What is disassembly in product analysis? :: Taking a product apart to see its components, materials, joining methods and how it was manufactured and assembled
Why do designers analyse competitors' products? :: To find strengths to build on, weaknesses to improve, typical prices and features, and gaps in the market – and to avoid copying (IP)
Give reasons why products evolve over time. :: New materials and technologies, changes in fashion and lifestyle, user feedback, cost reductions, new legislation, environmental pressures, competition
Give an example of product evolution caused by technology push. :: Lighting: incandescent bulb → compact fluorescent (CFL) → LED – each driven by new technology using less energy
Describe the evolution of personal music players. :: Vinyl record player → cassette Walkman (1979) → CD player → MP3 player/iPod → smartphone streaming – each smaller, more portable, more storage
Give an example of a product created by market pull. :: Bagless vacuum cleaners, child-proof medicine caps, ready-meal packaging that can go in a microwave – created because users demanded a solution
C: {{c1::Technology push}} is when new technology or materials drive new products; {{c2::market pull}} is when consumer demand drives new products.
What is innovation in design? :: Creating something new or significantly improved – a new product, function, material use or way of manufacturing

## Designers and companies
What is Dieter Rams known for? :: German industrial designer for **Braun** (radios, shavers, SK4 record player); minimalist functional style, 'less but better', ten principles of good design
What is Philippe Starck known for? :: French designer – **Juicy Salif** lemon squeezer (for Alessi), Louis Ghost chair (polycarbonate, Kartell); playful, post-modern, sculptural forms
What is Marcel Breuer known for? :: Bauhaus designer – **Wassily chair** (1925) made of bent tubular steel inspired by bicycle handlebars; also the Cesca chair
What are Charles and Ray Eames known for? :: American designers – **moulded plywood** chairs, the Eames Lounge Chair and moulded plastic/fibreglass chairs; mass-produced, ergonomic modern furniture
What is Sir Alec Issigonis known for? :: Designer of the **Mini** (1959) – transverse engine and front-wheel drive, so 80% of the floor space was for passengers and luggage
What is James Dyson known for? :: The **dual cyclone bagless vacuum cleaner** (over 5,000 prototypes – iterative design); also Airblade hand dryer and bladeless fan
What is Harry Beck known for? :: The **London Underground map** (1931/33) – a diagram not to scale, using only horizontal, vertical and 45° lines, inspired by electrical circuit diagrams
What is William Morris known for? :: Leader of the **Arts and Crafts movement** – hand-crafted furniture, wallpaper and textile designs based on nature (e.g. 'Strawberry Thief')
What is Charles Rennie Mackintosh known for? :: Glasgow architect/designer (Art Nouveau/Glasgow Style) – tall **high-backed ladder chairs**, the Hill House, Glasgow School of Art, stylised rose motif
What is Gerrit Rietveld known for? :: De Stijl designer – **Red Blue Chair** (1918) using straight lines and primary colours; Zig-Zag chair
What is Ettore Sottsass known for? :: Founder of the **Memphis Group** (1981) – bold colours, clashing patterns, plastic laminates (Carlton bookcase); also the Olivetti Valentine typewriter
What is Aldo Rossi known for? :: Italian post-modern architect – designed for **Alessi**, e.g. La Conica coffee maker and Il Conico kettle with simple geometric architectural forms
What is Norman Foster known for? :: British high-tech architect – 30 St Mary Axe ('the **Gherkin**'), Millennium Bridge, Reichstag dome; steel and glass, energy-efficient buildings
What is Coco Chanel known for? :: French fashion designer – used **jersey** fabric for women's clothing, the little black dress and Chanel suit; comfortable, practical, elegant clothing for women
What is Mary Quant known for? :: 1960s London fashion designer – popularised the **mini skirt** and hot pants; used new materials such as PVC; youth fashion
What is Vivienne Westwood known for? :: British designer of **punk** fashion in the 1970s (tartan, safety pins); later a campaigner for sustainability and buying less
What is Alexander McQueen known for? :: British fashion designer – dramatic tailoring, 'bumster' trousers, armadillo shoes, use of digital printing and technology in catwalk shows
What is Louis Comfort Tiffany known for? :: American Art Nouveau designer – **stained-glass lamps** made using the copper-foil technique, with nature-inspired designs
What is Raymond Templier known for? :: French **Art Deco** jewellery designer – bold geometric shapes using platinum, silver, diamonds and lacquer
What are Margaret Calvert and Jock Kinneir known for? :: Designing the **UK road sign system** (1960s) and the Transport and Rail Alphabet typefaces – clear, legible graphic communication
What is Jonathan (Jony) Ive known for? :: Apple's chief designer – iMac G3 (translucent coloured polycarbonate), iPod, iPhone, iPad; minimalist, user-friendly design
What is Kenneth Grange known for? :: British industrial designer – Kenwood Chef food mixer, InterCity 125 train nose, Anglepoise lamp redesign, Parker pens
What is Robin Day known for? :: The **Polyprop chair** (1963) – one of the first injection-moulded polypropylene chairs; cheap, stackable, mass-produced in millions
What is Trevor Baylis known for? :: The **wind-up (clockwork) radio** – no batteries needed; designed for areas without electricity
What is Apple known for as a design company? :: Minimalist, user-friendly products (iPod, iPhone, MacBook), integration of hardware and software, strong brand, premium materials (aluminium unibody)
What is Dyson known for as a company? :: Engineering-led innovation – cyclone vacuum cleaners, bladeless fans, hand dryers; heavy investment in research and prototyping
What is Braun known for as a company? :: German manufacturer of electrical products (shavers, radios, clocks) – clean functional design under Dieter Rams
What is Alessi known for as a company? :: Italian homeware company that works with famous designers (Starck, Rossi, Sapper) to make playful, sculptural, iconic kitchen products
What is Under Armour known for? :: Performance sportswear using **technical fabrics** – moisture-wicking, compression clothing (HeatGear/ColdGear)
What is Gap known for? :: US clothing retailer – mass-produced casual basics (jeans, T-shirts) sold worldwide; global supply chains
What is Primark known for? :: Low-cost **fast fashion** retailer – very cheap clothing using bulk buying and global suppliers; criticised over working conditions (e.g. Rana Plaza collapse, 2013)
What is Zara known for? :: **Fast fashion** – design to shop in about 2–3 weeks using a fast, responsive supply chain, small batches and just-in-time
What is IKEA known for? :: **Flat-pack** self-assembly furniture – reduces transport and storage costs, uses manufactured boards and knock-down fittings, low prices

## Design movements
What were the key features of the Arts and Crafts movement (c.1880–1910)? :: Reaction against industrial mass production; hand-crafted quality, honest construction, natural materials and nature-inspired patterns (William Morris)
What were the key features of Art Nouveau (c.1890–1910)? :: Flowing organic curves ('whiplash' lines), plant and insect forms, stained glass and ironwork (Mackintosh, Tiffany)
What were the key features of Art Deco (1920s–30s)? :: Bold geometric shapes, symmetry, sunbursts and zigzags, luxury materials (chrome, lacquer, ivory), streamlined forms (Templier)
What were the key features of the Bauhaus (1919–1933)? :: German design school – 'form follows function', simple geometric forms, no decoration, new materials such as tubular steel, design for mass production (Breuer, Gropius)
What were the key features of De Stijl? :: Dutch movement – straight horizontal and vertical lines, primary colours plus black and white (Rietveld, Mondrian)
What were the key features of the Memphis Group (1980s)? :: Bright clashing colours, bold patterns, plastic laminates, playful asymmetric shapes; rejected 'good taste' and pure function (Sottsass)
What is post-modernism in design? :: A reaction against modernism – playful, witty, decorative designs that mix styles and use bold colour (Starck, Rossi, Memphis)
What is modernism in design? :: Early–mid 20th-century approach – function over decoration, simple forms, new materials and mass production
Who said 'form follows function' and what does it mean? :: Louis Sullivan (architect) – a product's shape should be based on its purpose rather than decoration

## Product life cycle (market)
State the stages of a product's market life cycle. :: Introduction → growth → maturity → decline (sales rise, level off, then fall)
How can a company extend the life of a product in the maturity stage? :: Restyle or update it, add new features, lower the price, new marketing or target new markets
Explain how analysing an existing product can inform a new design. (3 marks) :: Identifies features users like, which can be kept ;; Reveals weaknesses that the new design can improve ;; Shows suitable materials, processes and price points for the target market

# 3 Wider issues – sustainability and ethics
What are the 6Rs of sustainability? :: Reduce, Reuse, Recycle, Rethink, Refuse, Repair
What is a life cycle assessment (LCA)? :: An evaluation of the environmental impact of a product from raw material extraction, manufacture, distribution, use, to disposal
What is the circular economy? :: Keeping resources in use for as long as possible, recovering and regenerating materials at end of life rather than 'take–make–dispose'
What are finite and non-finite resources? :: Finite – will run out (oil, metal ores) ;; Non-finite – renewable (timber from managed forests, wool, cotton)
What is a carbon footprint? :: Total greenhouse gas emissions caused by a product over its life
What is Fairtrade? :: A scheme ensuring producers in developing countries receive fair pay and working conditions
What are ethical considerations in design? :: Working conditions/fair pay, child labour, environmental damage, safety, cultural sensitivity, honest marketing
What is the impact of globalisation on manufacturing? :: Production moved to countries with cheaper labour; global supply chains; increased transport emissions; cheaper products; job loss locally
What is the WEEE directive? :: Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment regulations – manufacturers responsible for recycling electronic products
What is design for disassembly? :: Designing products so parts can be easily separated for repair, reuse or recycling (e.g. screws instead of glue)

## Social, moral and cultural issues
What are social issues in design? :: How products affect people and society, e.g. smartphones changing how people communicate, products for an ageing population, safety, access for all
What are cultural issues in design? :: Respecting beliefs, traditions and customs – colours, symbols, words, images and religious meanings differ between cultures
Give an example of a colour having different cultural meanings. :: White – purity/weddings in the West but mourning in parts of East Asia; red – luck and celebration in China, danger in the West
What are moral issues in design? :: Whether a product or the way it is made is right or wrong – e.g. weapons, products encouraging waste, exploiting workers, misleading advertising
What is a 'throwaway society'? :: A culture where products are cheap and quickly discarded rather than repaired or reused, creating large amounts of waste
How do fashion and trends affect product design? :: Products are restyled frequently to follow trends, which drives sales but shortens product life (style obsolescence) and increases waste
What is a sweatshop? :: A factory where workers are paid very little, work long hours in unsafe conditions, sometimes including child labour
What was the Rana Plaza disaster? :: The 2013 collapse of a garment factory building in Bangladesh that killed over 1,100 workers – highlighted unsafe conditions in fast-fashion supply chains
What is the Ethical Trading Initiative (ETI)? :: An alliance of companies, trade unions and charities that promotes workers' rights in global supply chains
What does the FSC logo mean? :: Forest Stewardship Council – the timber or paper comes from responsibly managed forests where trees are replanted and workers/communities are respected
What are conflict minerals? :: Minerals (tin, tantalum/coltan, tungsten, gold) used in electronics that may be mined in war zones, funding armed groups and using forced labour
Explain the social benefits and drawbacks of automation in manufacturing. :: **Benefits** – safer (robots do dangerous jobs), consistent quality, cheaper products, new skilled jobs (programming, maintenance) ;; **Drawbacks** – fewer unskilled jobs, deskilling, unemployment in some communities
Why might a company choose to manufacture locally rather than abroad? :: Lower transport costs and emissions, faster delivery, easier quality control, supports local jobs, better ethical oversight, 'Made in Britain' marketing

## Protecting designs – intellectual property
What is intellectual property (IP)? :: Creations of the mind (inventions, designs, brand names, artwork) that can be legally protected so others cannot copy them
What does a patent protect? :: A new invention – how it works or is made; must be applied for; lasts up to 20 years in the UK
What does a registered design protect? :: The appearance of a product – shape, pattern, colour, decoration; up to 25 years (renewed every 5 years)
What does copyright protect? :: Original written, artistic, musical and software work – automatic (no application), lasts for the creator's life plus 70 years
What does a trademark protect? :: Brand names, logos and slogans that identify a company; registered (®), renewable every 10 years
Why is intellectual property protection important to designers? :: It stops others copying their work, so they can earn money from their ideas and recover research and development costs

## Environmental issues and sustainability
Explain 'Reduce' in the 6Rs. :: Use less material and energy in manufacture and use, e.g. lighter-weight packaging, fewer parts, energy-efficient products
Explain 'Reuse' in the 6Rs. :: Use a product again for the same or a different purpose instead of throwing it away, e.g. refillable bottles, jam jars as storage
Explain 'Recycle' in the 6Rs. :: Process waste materials so they can be used to make new products, e.g. melting PET bottles into fleece fibre
Explain 'Rethink' in the 6Rs. :: Question whether there is a better, more sustainable way to meet the need, e.g. a product-sharing scheme or a different material
Explain 'Refuse' in the 6Rs. :: Don't buy or use products that are unnecessary, over-packaged, unethical or bad for the environment
Explain 'Repair' in the 6Rs. :: Fix products rather than replacing them; design with replaceable parts, standard fixings and available spares
C: {{c1::Primary}} recycling is reusing a product in its original form (e.g. refilled glass milk bottles); {{c2::secondary}} recycling breaks materials down physically to make new products; {{c3::tertiary}} recycling breaks materials down chemically into their raw chemicals or fuel.
What does the Möbius loop symbol mean? :: Three chasing arrows – the product or packaging can be recycled (with a % in the middle it contains that much recycled material)
What does the Green Dot symbol mean? :: The producer has paid towards the cost of recovering and recycling packaging – it does NOT mean the item is recyclable
State the polymer identification codes 1–7. :: 1 PET ;; 2 HDPE ;; 3 PVC ;; 4 LDPE ;; 5 PP (polypropylene) ;; 6 PS (polystyrene) ;; 7 Other (e.g. acrylic, polycarbonate, nylon)
State the stages of a product life cycle assessment. :: Raw material extraction → processing → manufacture → distribution/transport → use → end of life (reuse, recycle, landfill, incineration)
What is 'cradle to grave' vs 'cradle to cradle'? :: Cradle to grave – assessing a product from raw material to disposal ;; Cradle to cradle – designing so materials are fully recycled into new products at end of life (no waste)
What is an ecological footprint? :: The amount of land and natural resources needed to produce a product (or support a lifestyle) and absorb its waste
What is a social footprint? :: The effect of a product on people and communities – working conditions, health, wages, local jobs throughout its life cycle
What is embodied energy? :: The total energy used to extract, process, manufacture and transport a material or product
Give environmental impacts of extracting raw materials. :: Deforestation and habitat loss (timber), scarred landscapes and pollution from mining (metal ores), oil spills and emissions (polymers), large energy and water use
Give environmental problems caused by landfill. :: Uses land, materials take years to decompose (polymers hundreds of years), toxic chemicals leach into soil/water, methane (greenhouse gas) released
What are microplastics? :: Tiny polymer particles (under 5 mm) from broken-down plastic waste and from washing synthetic textiles – pollute oceans and enter food chains
C: A {{c1::biodegradable}} material can be broken down by micro-organisms; a {{c2::compostable}} material breaks down into nutrient-rich compost within a set time under composting conditions.
What is upcycling? :: Turning waste materials or unwanted products into new products of higher value or quality, e.g. pallets into furniture
What does an energy label (A–G) show? :: How energy-efficient an electrical appliance is – A is the most efficient – helping consumers choose lower running costs and emissions
What is carbon offsetting? :: Compensating for carbon dioxide emissions by funding schemes that reduce or absorb CO2 elsewhere, e.g. tree planting
Why does transporting products affect sustainability? :: Burns fossil fuels, releasing CO2 and pollutants; long global supply chains increase the carbon footprint – sourcing locally reduces this
Explain how a designer can reduce the environmental impact of a product. (4 marks) :: Use recycled/recyclable or renewable materials ;; Use less material (thin sections, efficient nesting) ;; Design for disassembly and repair to extend life ;; Choose low-energy processes and components ;; Reduce packaging and source locally ;; Label materials for recycling
Why does planned obsolescence harm the environment? :: Products are replaced sooner, using more raw materials and energy and creating more waste/e-waste
What is the 'right to repair'? :: Laws/campaigns requiring manufacturers to make spare parts, tools and repair information available so products can be fixed rather than replaced

## New and emerging technologies
What is automation? :: Using machines and computer control to carry out tasks with little or no human involvement
What is Computer Integrated Manufacture (CIM)? :: Using computers to control and link the whole manufacturing process – design, ordering, production, stock control and quality checks
What is a Flexible Manufacturing System (FMS)? :: Groups of computer-controlled machines and robots that can be quickly reprogrammed to make different products
Give advantages of using robots in manufacturing. :: Work 24/7 without breaks, consistent accuracy and quality, work in hazardous conditions, fast, lower long-term labour costs
Give disadvantages of using robots in manufacturing. :: High initial cost, need programming and maintenance, fewer jobs for unskilled workers, can't adapt like humans, breakdowns stop production
What is the Internet of Things (IoT)? :: Everyday products connected to the internet that send and receive data, e.g. smart thermostats, smart speakers, fitness trackers
Give a benefit and a concern of 'smart' connected products. :: **Benefit** – remote control, automation, energy saving, useful data ;; **Concern** – privacy/security of data, hacking, dependence on internet, shorter lifespan
What is crowdfunding? :: Raising money for a new product from many members of the public online (e.g. Kickstarter) – also tests market demand before manufacture
What is virtual marketing? :: Promoting products online through social media, websites and influencers to reach a global audience cheaply
What is cloud-based collaboration in design? :: Designers in different locations sharing and editing the same CAD files and documents online in real time
What is co-operative (co-op) organisation? :: A business owned and run by its members, who share the profits and decisions, often with ethical aims
What is a sustainable business practice? :: Running a business in ways that reduce environmental harm and treat people fairly – e.g. renewable energy, recycling, fair pay
How has the growth of the internet changed how products are sold? :: Online shopping and global marketplaces, direct-to-consumer brands, customer reviews, personalised/custom products, faster trends
What is mass customisation? :: Mass-producing products that can be individually personalised for each customer, using flexible CAD/CAM (e.g. custom trainers)

# 4 Design thinking and communication
What is an isometric drawing? :: A 3D drawing with vertical lines and horizontal lines at 30°
What is an orthographic projection? :: 2D views of a product (front, side, plan) drawn to scale with dimensions – third angle in the UK
What is an exploded view? :: A drawing showing parts of a product separated to show how they fit together
What is a working drawing? :: A detailed technical drawing with dimensions, scale and materials for manufacture
Give benefits of CAD. :: Accurate, easy to modify, can be shared electronically, 3D modelling and virtual testing, links to CAM, rendering
Give drawbacks of CAD. :: Expensive software and training; time to learn; files can be corrupted; can stifle creativity
What is modelling and why is it used? :: Making 3D prototypes (card, foam, 3D print) to test ideas, proportion, ergonomics and function before final manufacture
What is the purpose of a flowchart in manufacture? :: To show the sequence of manufacturing stages, including quality control checks (diamonds)
What is a mood board? :: A collection of images, colours and textures to inspire design and show the target market/style

## Approaches to design
What is a systems approach to design? :: Breaking a product down into subsystems (input → process → output) that can be designed and tested separately – common in electronics and easy for non-specialists to follow
Give advantages of iterative design. :: Problems found and fixed early, constant user feedback, product improves with each cycle, reduces risk of failure at launch
Give disadvantages of iterative design. :: Can be time-consuming and costly (many prototypes), hard to know when to stop, may lose sight of the original brief
What is collaborative design? :: Working with others (users, clients, specialists, other designers) to share expertise and ideas – often using shared/cloud CAD
What is biomimicry? :: Taking inspiration from nature to solve design problems
Give three examples of biomimicry. :: Velcro – burrs sticking to fur ;; Speedo Fastskin swimsuit – shark skin ;; Shinkansen bullet train nose – kingfisher's beak
What is design fixation? :: Sticking to one early idea or copying existing products, which limits creativity
Give strategies to avoid design fixation. :: Mind mapping, brainstorming with others, SCAMPER, mood boards, biomimicry, random word/object links, looking at products from other fields, quick modelling
What does SCAMPER stand for? :: Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify (magnify/minify), Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse/Rearrange
What is a design strategy? :: A planned method used to generate and develop ideas and avoid design fixation, e.g. collaboration, user-centred design, systems thinking, iterative design

## Communication techniques
Why do designers use freehand sketching? :: Quick, cheap way to explore and record ideas in 2D or 3D and communicate them to others
C: {{c1::Annotation}} explains and evaluates a design (why decisions were made); {{c2::labelling}} simply names parts or materials.
What is crating? :: Drawing a 3D box (crate) first and then cutting away or adding to it to create an accurate 3D sketch of a product
What is rendering? :: Adding colour, tone, shading and texture to a drawing to show the form and the material (e.g. wood grain, shiny plastic)
What is oblique drawing? :: The front view is drawn true shape; depth lines go back at 45°, often at half length (cabinet oblique) to look more realistic
What is one-point perspective? :: A 3D drawing where all depth lines go back to a single vanishing point on the horizon line – good for interiors and simple objects
What is two-point perspective? :: A 3D drawing using two vanishing points on the horizon line – the most realistic view of an object seen from a corner
What angle are receding lines drawn at in isometric drawing? :: 30° to the horizontal (vertical lines stay vertical)
In third-angle orthographic projection, where are the views placed? :: Plan view above the front view; the side (end) view placed on the side it is viewed from (right view to the right of the front)
What line type shows hidden detail on a working drawing? :: Thin dashed line
What line type shows a centre line? :: Thin chain line (long dash, short dash)
What line type shows outlines on a working drawing? :: Thick continuous line (thin continuous lines are used for dimensions, projection and construction)
State the conventions for dimensioning a working drawing. :: In millimetres (no 'mm' after each number); numbers placed above the dimension line, read from the bottom or right; arrowheads touching projection lines; small gap between projection line and object
What do the symbols ⌀ and R mean on a drawing? :: ⌀ = diameter ;; R = radius
What does a scale of 1:2 mean on a drawing? :: The drawing is half the real size (1 mm on the drawing = 2 mm in real life); 2:1 means twice real size
What is a sectional view? :: A view of an object as if it has been cut through, with the cut surfaces shown by hatching at 45° – shows internal detail
What is an assembly drawing? :: A drawing showing how all the parts fit together, often with a parts list
What is a schematic (circuit) diagram? :: A diagram using standard symbols to show how electronic components are connected – not a picture of the layout
Name the standard flowchart symbols. :: Rounded rectangle/oval – start/end ;; Rectangle – process ;; Diamond – decision/question (e.g. quality check) ;; Parallelogram – input/output
What is a storyboard used for? :: Showing a sequence of how a product is used or how a user interacts with it, frame by frame
Name modelling materials and methods. :: Card and paper, foam (Styrofoam), clay, MDF, cardboard; 3D printing; breadboard (electronics); toile (textiles); virtual CAD models
What is the difference between a mock-up and a prototype? :: Mock-up – a quick model to show size/appearance (may not work) ;; Prototype – a working model to test function before manufacture
What is a toile? :: A test version of a garment made from cheap fabric (calico) to check fit and pattern before cutting the final fabric
Give the benefits of physical modelling. :: Test size, proportion, ergonomics and function; get user/client feedback; find problems early; cheaper than making the final product
What is virtual modelling? :: Creating a 3D model in CAD to view it from all angles, render it realistically, test it (e.g. stress analysis, simulation) before making anything
What is a cutting list? :: A table listing each part with quantity, material and finished dimensions – used to order and prepare materials
How does a designer communicate a design to a manufacturer? :: Working drawings with dimensions and tolerances, specification, cutting lists, parts lists, CAD files, flowcharts of manufacture

Why use charts and graphs to present research data? :: Make trends and comparisons quick to see, easier for clients to understand than raw figures

## More properties
C: {{c1::Tensile}} strength is the ability to resist being pulled apart; {{c2::compressive}} strength is the ability to resist being squashed.
C: {{c1::Density}} is the mass of a material per unit volume (mass ÷ volume).
C: {{c1::Stiffness}} is the ability of a material to resist bending or flexing.
C: {{c1::Brittleness}} means a material breaks or shatters suddenly without bending (e.g. glass, cast iron, acrylic).
C: {{c1::Fusibility}} is the ability of a material to be melted and joined or cast.
C: {{c1::Durability}} is the ability to withstand wear, weathering and deterioration over time.
C: {{c1::Corrosion resistance}} is the ability of a material to resist chemical attack such as rusting or oxidation.
What is the difference between physical and working properties? :: Physical – characteristics of the material itself (density, conductivity, fusibility, absorbency) ;; Working (mechanical) – how it reacts to forces and processes (strength, hardness, toughness, malleability, ductility, elasticity)
What is an insulator? :: A material that does not conduct heat or electricity well (e.g. polymers, wood, air in foams)

## Selecting materials
State factors that influence the selection of materials. :: Function and properties; aesthetics; environmental impact; availability/stock form; cost; social, cultural and ethical factors; manufacturing processes available; scale of production
How do aesthetics influence material choice? :: Colour, texture, grain, finish and how the material looks and feels must appeal to the target user and suit the product style
How does scale of production affect material choice? :: Mass production needs materials suited to high-speed processes (e.g. polymer granules for injection moulding) and bought in bulk cheaply
Explain why a specific material is suitable for a product. (exam method) :: Name the specific material (e.g. 'polypropylene', not 'plastic') ;; State a property ;; Link it to the product's requirement using 'because/so that' ;; e.g. 'Polypropylene is flexible **so** it can form a living hinge on the lid'
Why are cultural and ethical factors important in material choice? :: Some materials may be unacceptable to users (e.g. leather or silk for vegans, materials from conflict areas or endangered forests)

Name stock forms of textiles. :: Fabric on rolls/bolts (common widths 90, 115, 150 cm), yarn, thread, fibres
What are standard components? :: Pre-manufactured parts made in large numbers to standard sizes, e.g. screws, nuts and bolts, hinges, zips, buttons, resistors, ICs
Give reasons manufacturers use standard components. :: Cheaper (made in bulk), consistent quality, save design and manufacturing time, easy to replace for repair, reliable

## Enhancing and manipulating materials
Give ways that materials can be manipulated to enhance their properties. :: Laminating, heat treatment (annealing, hardening, tempering), additives (plasticisers, stabilisers, flame retardants), coatings and finishes, combining into composites, blending fibres, folding/corrugating to stiffen
What is a plasticiser? :: An additive mixed with a polymer (e.g. PVC) to make it softer and more flexible
What is a stabiliser (UV stabiliser)? :: An additive that protects a polymer from breaking down in sunlight (UV light)
What is a flame retardant? :: An additive or finish that makes a material less likely to catch fire or slows the spread of flames

# 5 Smart and modern materials
What is a smart material? :: A material whose properties change in response to an external stimulus (heat, light, stress) and change back
Give examples of smart materials and uses. :: Shape memory alloy (nitinol) – glasses frames, stents ;; Thermochromic pigment – kettles, baby spoons ;; Photochromic pigment – sunglasses ;; Quantum tunnelling composite (QTC) – touch switches
What is a modern material? :: A material developed through new technology with enhanced properties, e.g. graphene, metal foams, titanium, nanomaterials (Kevlar and carbon fibre are often classed as composites/technical materials)
What are composite materials? :: Two or more materials combined to give improved properties ;; e.g. carbon fibre reinforced polymer (CFRP), glass reinforced plastic (GRP), concrete
What is a technical textile? :: Fabrics engineered for performance, e.g. Kevlar (bulletproof), Gore-Tex (breathable waterproof), conductive fabrics, microencapsulated fabrics
What is graphene? :: A single layer of carbon atoms – extremely strong, lightweight and conductive

## Smart materials in detail
C: {{c1::Thermochromic}} pigments change colour in response to a change in {{c2::temperature}}.
C: {{c1::Photochromic}} pigments change colour in response to changes in {{c2::light (UV) levels}}.
C: A {{c1::shape memory alloy}} such as nitinol returns to its original 'remembered' shape when {{c2::heated}}.
C: {{c1::Quantum tunnelling composite (QTC)}} is an insulator that becomes a {{c2::conductor}} when it is squeezed or pressed.
C: A {{c1::piezoelectric}} material produces a small voltage when pressure is applied, and changes shape slightly when a voltage is applied.
Give uses of piezoelectric materials. :: Gas lighter/BBQ igniters, buzzers and speakers, microphones, quartz watch crystals, pressure sensors
What is nitinol made from and where is it used? :: An alloy of nickel and titanium – flexible glasses frames, dental braces, medical stents, actuators/robot 'muscles'
Give uses of thermochromic pigments. :: Baby spoons and bath ducks (too hot warning), kettles, mugs that reveal images, forehead thermometers, battery testers
What is phosphorescent pigment? :: A pigment that absorbs light energy and slowly releases it, glowing in the dark (safety signs, watch faces)
What is microencapsulation? :: Tiny capsules of a substance (fragrance, moisturiser, antibacterial agent) attached to fabric that release it slowly with friction/wear
What is a phase change material? :: A material that absorbs heat as it melts and releases it as it solidifies, helping regulate temperature (e.g. in outdoor clothing)
What is the difference between a smart material and a modern material? :: Smart – reacts to an external stimulus (heat, light, pressure) and reverses ;; Modern – developed through new technology with improved properties, but doesn't react to stimuli

## Modern materials in detail
What is polymorph? :: A thermoplastic supplied as granules that softens in hot water (about 62 °C) and can be moulded by hand, then sets hard – used for modelling and custom grips
What are nanomaterials? :: Materials with structures on a nanometre scale (1–100 nm) giving special properties, e.g. self-cleaning glass, antibacterial silver nanoparticles in socks/dressings
What are metal foams? :: Metals (usually aluminium) with gas bubbles trapped inside – very light, stiff and good at absorbing impact (car crash structures)
Give properties and uses of titanium. :: Very strong, lightweight, highly corrosion resistant, biocompatible ;; Aircraft parts, medical implants, high-end bikes, watches
What is Kevlar? :: An aramid fibre that is about five times stronger than steel by weight, heat resistant and lightweight – body armour, helmets, gloves, tyres, sports equipment
What is carbon fibre (CFRP)? :: Carbon fibres set in epoxy resin – very strong, stiff and light but expensive – racing bikes, F1 cars, aircraft, tennis rackets
What is glass reinforced plastic (GRP)? :: Glass fibre matting set in polyester resin – strong, light, weather-resistant, can be moulded into large shapes – boat hulls, car bodies, kayaks
What is Flexiply? :: Plywood made from thin veneers that can be bent into curves without steaming
What is a liquid crystal display (LCD)? :: A thin, low-power screen where liquid crystals twist to block or let light through when a voltage is applied – calculators, watches, phones
What is PLA and why is it considered more sustainable? :: Polylactic acid – a polymer made from plant starch (e.g. corn); renewable source and biodegradable/compostable under industrial conditions; used for 3D printing and packaging
What is Potatopak? :: A biodegradable material made from potato starch, moulded into disposable plates and food trays as an alternative to polystyrene
Why are graphene's properties important? :: Extremely strong, light, flexible, transparent and an excellent conductor – potential for flexible screens, faster electronics, better batteries

# 6 Technical understanding – energy and systems
How is electricity generated from fossil fuels? :: Burning coal/oil/gas heats water into steam → turns turbines → generators
Name renewable energy sources. :: Solar, wind, hydroelectric, tidal, wave, geothermal, biomass
What is kinetic energy storage in products? :: A flywheel stores kinetic energy as it spins; a wind-up (clockwork) mechanism stores elastic potential energy in a spring, released to drive the product (e.g. wind-up torch/radio)
Compare batteries: alkaline vs rechargeable. :: Alkaline – single use, cheap, disposal issues ;; Rechargeable (Li-ion, NiMH) – reusable, more expensive, better for environment
What is a system diagram? :: Input → process → output (with feedback)
Give examples of input components. :: Switches, light-dependent resistor (LDR), thermistor, pressure sensor, microphone
Give examples of process components. :: Microcontroller (PIC/Arduino/micro:bit), timer (555), counter, logic gates
Give examples of output components. :: LED, buzzer, speaker, motor, bulb, solenoid, LCD screen
What is a microcontroller? :: A small programmable computer on a single chip that processes inputs and controls outputs
Why use programmable components? :: Flexible, reprogrammable, fewer components, smaller circuits, can carry out complex functions

## Energy generation
Name non-renewable energy sources. :: Fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) and nuclear (uranium)
Give advantages and disadvantages of fossil fuels. :: **Advantages** – reliable, on demand, existing infrastructure ;; **Disadvantages** – finite, release CO2 (global warming) and pollutants (acid rain), mining/drilling damage
Describe how nuclear power generates electricity. :: Nuclear fission of uranium releases heat → boils water to steam → turns turbines → generators
Give advantages and disadvantages of nuclear power. :: **Advantages** – no CO2 when running, lots of reliable energy from little fuel ;; **Disadvantages** – radioactive waste stored for thousands of years, risk of accidents, very expensive to build and decommission
How do solar photovoltaic (PV) cells work? :: They convert light energy directly into electricity
Give advantages and disadvantages of solar power. :: **Advantages** – renewable, no emissions in use, low running cost, can be used in remote products ;; **Disadvantages** – only works in daylight, less in cloud, high set-up cost, large area needed
Give advantages and disadvantages of wind power. :: **Advantages** – renewable, no emissions, low running cost ;; **Disadvantages** – intermittent (no wind), visual and noise impact, can harm birds
How does hydroelectric power work? :: Water stored behind a dam flows through turbines that drive generators
How does tidal power work? :: The rise and fall of tides drives water through turbines (e.g. in a barrage) – predictable but expensive and affects habitats
What is biomass energy? :: Burning plant material, wood or waste to produce heat/electricity; renewable and considered roughly carbon neutral as plants absorb CO2 as they grow
What is geothermal energy? :: Using heat from hot rocks underground to produce steam for turbines or to heat buildings

## Energy storage
What is pumped storage? :: Surplus electricity pumps water uphill to a reservoir; when demand is high the water is released through turbines to generate electricity quickly
Why is energy storage important for renewable energy? :: Renewable sources are intermittent, so energy must be stored when supply is high and released when demand is high
How is energy stored using pneumatics? :: Air is compressed into a tank and released to do work (e.g. drive actuators, air tools)
C: Battery capacity is measured in {{c1::milliamp-hours (mAh)}} – the higher the value, the {{c2::longer}} the battery lasts.
What is a flywheel used for in energy storage? :: A heavy spinning wheel stores kinetic energy and releases it to keep a machine running smoothly or provide bursts of power
Why might a product designer choose a rechargeable battery? :: Cheaper over the product's life, less waste, can be built in to make the product slimmer – but higher initial cost and needs a charger

## Electronic systems – core
C: In a light-dependent resistor (LDR), resistance {{c1::decreases}} as light level increases.
C: In an NTC thermistor, resistance {{c1::decreases}} as temperature increases.
What is a push-to-make (PTM) switch? :: A momentary switch that completes the circuit only while it is pressed (e.g. doorbell)
What is a push-to-break (PTB) switch? :: A momentary switch that breaks the circuit while it is pressed (e.g. fridge light switch)
What is a reed switch? :: A switch whose contacts close when a magnet is brought near – used in door/window alarms
What is a tilt switch? :: A switch that opens or closes when it is tilted – used in alarms and toys
What is a microswitch? :: A small switch with a lever that needs little force to operate – used as a limit switch in machines, mouse buttons
What does a solenoid do? :: An electromagnet that produces linear (push/pull) movement when current flows – used in electric locks and valves
What is the difference between open-loop and closed-loop control? :: Open loop – no feedback, output not checked (e.g. a simple timer) ;; Closed loop – feedback from the output adjusts the system (e.g. thermostat)
What is feedback in a system? :: Information from the output returned to the input/process so the system can adjust itself
Draw a system diagram for an automatic night light. :: Input: LDR (senses darkness) → Process: microcontroller/transistor → Output: LED/lamp turns on
What is a conductor? :: A material that allows electricity or heat to flow through it easily, e.g. copper, aluminium

# 6 Mechanical devices and forces
Name the four types of motion. :: Linear (straight line), reciprocating (back and forth), rotary (circular), oscillating (swinging in an arc)
Name the three classes of lever and give examples. :: 1st class – fulcrum in middle (see-saw, scissors) ;; 2nd class – load in middle (wheelbarrow, nutcracker) ;; 3rd class – effort in middle (tweezers, fishing rod)
What is mechanical advantage? :: Load ÷ effort – how much a mechanism multiplies force
What is velocity ratio (gear ratio)? :: Teeth on driven gear ÷ teeth on driver gear
What does a cam and follower do? :: Converts rotary motion into reciprocating motion
What does a crank and slider do? :: Converts rotary motion to reciprocating motion (or vice versa), e.g. pistons
What does a rack and pinion do? :: Converts rotary motion into linear motion (e.g. car steering)
What is the purpose of an idler gear? :: Makes the driver and driven gears turn in the same direction without changing the ratio
Name the five types of force. :: Tension, compression, torsion, bending, shear
How can materials be reinforced or stiffened? :: Laminating, ribs, webs, folding/corrugating, triangulation, adding composite fibres

## Mechanisms – core
What is a mechanism? :: A device that changes an input motion and force into a different output motion and force
What does a bevel gear do? :: Transfers rotary motion through 90° (changes the axis of rotation), e.g. hand drill
What does a worm and wheel do? :: Gives a very large speed reduction and changes the axis through 90°; the wheel cannot drive the worm (self-locking)
What is a simple gear train? :: Two or more spur gears meshed together; adjacent gears turn in opposite directions
What is a compound gear train? :: A gear train where two gears are fixed on the same shaft – gives a large change in speed in a small space
What is the effect of a small driver gear turning a large driven gear? :: Output speed is reduced and torque (turning force) is increased
What do pulleys and belts do? :: Transfer rotary motion between shafts some distance apart; both pulleys turn the same way (crossing the belt reverses direction)
What is an advantage and a disadvantage of a belt drive? :: **Advantage** – quiet, cheap, belt slips if jammed (safety) ;; **Disadvantage** – can slip, so not precise; belts wear and stretch
Why does a bicycle use a chain and sprocket instead of a belt? :: The chain can't slip, so it transmits large forces efficiently and precisely
What is a linkage? :: A system of levers connected by pivots to change the direction, size or type of motion
What does a reverse motion linkage do? :: Makes the output move in the opposite direction to the input
What does a parallel motion linkage do? :: Makes the output move in the same direction as the input (e.g. cantilever toolbox trays)
What does a bell crank do? :: Changes the direction of motion through 90° (e.g. bicycle brakes)
What does a ratchet and pawl do? :: Allows rotation in one direction only (e.g. winch, socket wrench, clock)
Name types of cam. :: Pear (dwell then rise and fall), eccentric/circular (smooth continuous rise and fall), snail/drop (gradual rise then sudden drop – turns one way only), heart (uniform rise and fall)
Name types of cam follower. :: Knife-edge (precise but wears), flat (hard-wearing), roller (low friction)
What is a dwell on a cam? :: Part of the rotation where the follower does not move up or down

## Structures and forces
C: {{c1::Tension}} is a pulling force that stretches a material.
C: {{c1::Compression}} is a pushing force that squashes a material.
C: {{c1::Torsion}} is a twisting force.
C: {{c1::Shear}} forces act in opposite directions across a material, making it slide apart (e.g. scissors cutting).
C: {{c1::Bending}} causes one side of a material to be in tension and the other side in compression.
What is the difference between a static and a dynamic load? :: Static – a constant, unmoving load (e.g. weight of a shelf) ;; Dynamic – a moving or changing load (e.g. wind, people walking)
What is triangulation? :: Adding diagonal members to make triangles in a frame – triangles cannot change shape, making the structure rigid
What is the difference between a frame structure and a shell structure? :: Frame – made of joined members (bike frame, pylon) ;; Shell – a thin outer skin carries the load (egg, drinks can, car body)
How does corrugation stiffen a material? :: Folding a thin sheet into ridges/flutes greatly increases its resistance to bending (corrugated card, roofing sheet)
How does laminating make plywood stronger? :: Veneers are glued with the grain of each layer at 90° to the next, so it is strong in all directions and resists warping
What are ribs and webs in a moulded product? :: Thin internal strengthening walls that add stiffness without making the whole wall thick (avoids sink marks and saves material)
What is a gusset? :: A triangular plate added to a corner joint to strengthen it
How are textiles stiffened or reinforced? :: Interfacing, quilting/wadding, boning, double layers, top-stitching, bonding to other materials

# 7 Manufacturing processes
What are the scales of production? :: One-off (bespoke), batch, mass production, continuous production
What is one-off production? :: A single, unique product made to order – highly skilled labour, expensive (e.g. wedding dress, bridge)
What is batch production? :: A set number of identical products made together; jigs and templates used (e.g. bread, furniture)
What is mass production? :: Large numbers of identical products made on a production line, often automated; cheaper per unit (e.g. cars, phones)
What is continuous production? :: 24/7 production of products in huge quantities (e.g. steel, chemicals, paper)
What is just-in-time (JIT) manufacturing? :: Materials and components arrive exactly when needed – reduces storage costs and waste, but vulnerable to supply delays
What is lean manufacturing? :: Minimising waste (materials, time, energy) at every stage
What is a jig? :: A device that holds work and guides a tool to make repeated identical operations (e.g. drilling jig)
What is a template? :: A shape used to mark out repeated identical outlines
What is quality control? :: Checks during manufacture to make sure products meet the specification (e.g. tolerance checks, go/no-go gauges)
What is quality assurance? :: The overall system/procedures to ensure quality throughout design and manufacture
What is tolerance? :: The acceptable range of variation from a stated dimension (e.g. 50 mm ± 0.5 mm)
Describe injection moulding. :: Polymer granules heated and forced by a screw into a mould under pressure, cooled, ejected – high volume, complex shapes
Describe vacuum forming. :: Thermoforming sheet heated until soft, mould raised, air sucked out so sheet forms over the mould
Describe blow moulding. :: A heated tube (parison) of polymer is placed in a mould and air blown in to form a hollow shape (bottles)
Describe extrusion. :: Material forced through a shaped die to make a continuous profile (pipes, rods, window frames)
Describe sand casting. :: Molten metal poured into a sand mould made from a pattern; cooled and removed
Describe die casting. :: Molten metal forced into a steel die under pressure – accurate, high volume
Describe line bending of acrylic. :: Heat along a line with a strip heater until soft, then bend using a former/jig
Name methods of joining wood. :: Butt, dowel, mortise and tenon, dovetail, finger/comb, housing joints; knock-down fittings; adhesives (PVA)
Name methods of joining metal. :: Welding, brazing, soldering, rivets, nuts and bolts, adhesives (epoxy resin)
What is laminating (wood)? :: Gluing thin layers of veneer together over a former to create curved shapes
Name printing processes for paper/board. :: Offset lithography (high volume, CMYK), screen printing, flexography, digital printing
What is CMYK? :: Cyan, magenta, yellow, key (black) – the four process colours used in printing
Give benefits of CAM. :: Accurate, repeatable, fast, can run continuously, reduced labour costs and waste
Give examples of CAM machines. :: Laser cutter, CNC router, 3D printer, CNC lathe, vinyl cutter, embroidery machine
What is additive vs subtractive manufacture? :: Additive – building up layers (3D printing) ;; Subtractive – removing material (milling, laser cutting)
Why are finishes applied to materials? :: Protect from moisture/corrosion/UV, improve appearance, improve hygiene or grip
Name finishes for wood. :: Varnish, paint, wood stain, oil, wax, sanding sealer
Name finishes for metals. :: Painting, powder coating, galvanising, anodising (aluminium), electroplating, dip coating
Name finishes for paper and board. :: Lamination, varnishing (UV), embossing, debossing, foil blocking, spot varnish

## Scales of production in more detail
Give advantages and disadvantages of one-off production. :: **Advantages** – unique, made exactly to the client's needs, high quality ;; **Disadvantages** – expensive per item, slow, needs highly skilled labour
Give advantages and disadvantages of batch production. :: **Advantages** – flexible (can switch products between batches), cheaper per unit than one-off, jigs speed up work ;; **Disadvantages** – downtime when resetting machines between batches, storage of stock
Give advantages and disadvantages of mass production. :: **Advantages** – very low unit cost, consistent quality, fast ;; **Disadvantages** – very high set-up costs (moulds, machines), inflexible, repetitive work for staff, needs high demand
What is a production line? :: A sequence of workstations where each worker/machine does one stage, with the product moving along between them
C: {{c1::Economies of scale}} mean the cost per unit falls as more products are made, because set-up costs are spread and materials are bought in bulk.
What is outsourcing? :: Paying another company (often abroad) to make parts or carry out processes rather than doing it in-house
What is sub-assembly? :: Building parts of a product separately (sometimes by other companies) which are then brought together for final assembly
How have CAD/CAM changed manufacturing? :: Faster design-to-production, accurate repeatable parts, automation and fewer workers, easy customisation, rapid prototyping
What is the role of a Gantt chart in manufacturing? :: A bar chart showing the order and timing of tasks so production can be planned and deadlines met

## Processes – categories, tools and equipment
C: {{c1::Wasting}} processes remove material (cutting, drilling, sawing, milling); {{c2::addition}} processes join or build up material (welding, gluing, 3D printing).
C: {{c1::Deforming}} processes change shape without adding or removing material (bending, vacuum forming, forging); {{c2::reforming}} processes melt and reshape material (casting, injection moulding).
Name tools used for marking out. :: Steel rule, try square/engineer's square, pencil/marking knife, scriber, marking gauge, odd-leg calipers, dividers, centre punch, templates, sliding bevel
What is a datum edge/surface? :: A straight, true reference edge or face from which all measurements are taken, to keep marking out accurate
What is vernier calipers used for? :: Accurately measuring internal and external dimensions and depths, to about 0.02 mm
What is a micrometer used for? :: Measuring external dimensions very precisely, to about 0.01 mm
What is a pillar drill used for and what safety precautions apply? :: Drilling accurate vertical holes ;; Clamp work in a vice/clamp, guard down, goggles, hair tied back, remove chuck key, never hold small work by hand
Name types of drill bit. :: Twist drill (general holes), countersink (recess for screw heads), flat/spade bit and Forstner bit (large holes in wood), hole saw, centre drill
Name saws and what they cut. :: Tenon saw – straight cuts in wood ;; Coping saw – curves in wood/polymers ;; Hacksaw/junior hacksaw – metal and polymers ;; Scroll saw – intricate shapes ;; Bandsaw – fast straight and curved cuts

## Digital design and manufacture
What is the difference between vector and raster settings on a laser cutter? :: Vector – the laser follows lines to cut or score ;; Raster – the laser scans back and forth to engrave areas/images
Which materials can be laser cut and which must not? :: Can cut: acrylic, plywood, MDF, card, paper, some fabrics ;; Must not cut: PVC (gives off toxic chlorine gas), polycarbonate (burns), metals in school cutters
Give advantages and disadvantages of laser cutting. :: **Advantages** – very accurate, intricate shapes, quick, repeatable, polished edges on acrylic ;; **Disadvantages** – expensive, burn marks on wood, fumes need extraction, limited thickness, 2D only
What is a CNC router? :: A computer-controlled machine with a rotating cutter that moves in x, y and z axes to cut and carve sheet materials (wood, MDF, plastics)
What does CNC stand for? :: Computer Numerical Control – machines controlled by programmed coordinates (G-code)
Describe fused deposition modelling (FDM) 3D printing. :: Thermoplastic filament (PLA/ABS) is melted and extruded through a heated nozzle, building the object layer by layer
Give advantages and disadvantages of 3D printing. :: **Advantages** – complex shapes, no tooling, ideal for prototypes and one-offs, little waste ;; **Disadvantages** – slow, limited size and materials, visible layer lines/weaker between layers, high cost per part for large quantities
What is rapid prototyping? :: Quickly making physical models directly from CAD data (e.g. 3D printing, laser cutting) to test designs early
What is finite element analysis (FEA)? :: A CAD simulation that tests how a product will react to forces and stresses before it is made
Give examples of CAD software uses. :: 2D design for laser cutting, 3D solid modelling (e.g. Fusion 360, SolidWorks), circuit design and simulation, pattern/lay planning for textiles, packaging nets

## Quality control
Give examples of quality control checks. :: Measuring dimensions against tolerances, go/no-go gauges, visual inspection, testing samples, registration marks and colour bars in printing, checking seams/stitch length in textiles
What is a go/no-go gauge? :: A gauge with two ends – the 'go' end must fit and the 'no-go' end must not, showing quickly if a part is within tolerance
C: {{c1::Quality control}} involves inspecting and testing products during and after manufacture; {{c2::quality assurance}} is the overall system that makes sure quality standards are met at every stage.
What is ISO 9001? :: An international standard for quality management systems – shows a company has quality assurance procedures in place
Why are tolerances needed in manufacture? :: No process is perfectly accurate; tolerances define the acceptable range so parts still fit and work together while keeping costs reasonable
What is a critical control point in quality checking? :: A stage in manufacture where a check must be made to prevent faults, e.g. after cutting or before assembly

## Jigs, fixtures and accuracy
What is a fixture? :: A device that holds work securely in a fixed position on a machine during processing (but, unlike a jig, doesn't guide the tool)
Explain why jigs are used in batch production. (3 marks) :: Every part is identical/accurate **so** parts fit and quality is consistent ;; Faster because no repeated marking out **so** lower labour cost ;; Less skill needed and fewer errors **so** less waste
What is a former? :: A shaped block around or over which material is bent, laminated or vacuum formed to give a repeatable shape
What is a pattern (in manufacturing)? :: A model of the final part used to make a mould (e.g. a sand-casting pattern) or a template for cutting textile pieces

# 8 Viability and 9 Health and safety
How do you evaluate the viability of a design? :: Test against the specification, user feedback, cost/profit, manufacturability, environmental impact, market need
What is a risk assessment? :: Identifying hazards, who might be harmed, the level of risk and control measures to reduce risk
What does COSHH stand for? :: Control of Substances Hazardous to Health – regulations for handling chemicals safely
Give PPE used in workshops. :: Goggles, aprons, gloves, ear defenders, dust masks, protective footwear
What safety legislation applies to products? :: Consumer Rights Act, General Product Safety Regulations, Toy Safety Regulations, Furniture and Furnishings (Fire Safety) Regulations; plus BSI Kitemark/CE/UKCA marks ;; (the Health and Safety at Work Act 1974 covers workplaces, not products)
What does the BSI Kitemark show? :: The product has been tested by the British Standards Institution and meets safety and quality standards
What does the CE/UKCA mark show? :: The product meets safety requirements to be sold in Europe/the UK
What is a lifecycle cost? :: All costs of a product over its life including materials, manufacture, transport, use and disposal
What is the difference between a prototype and a final product? :: A prototype is a model to test the design; the final product is manufactured for the user
How can you calculate the area of material needed? :: Length × width (convert units carefully; add waste allowance)
What is stock form? :: The standard forms materials are supplied in, e.g. sheets, rods, bars, tubes, planks, granules, rolls

## Viability of design solutions
How can a prototype be tested? :: User trials, testing each function, measuring against the specification, getting client/expert feedback, stress or durability testing
What is a user trial? :: Target users test the prototype in realistic conditions while the designer observes and records their feedback
Why is third-party (client/expert) feedback valuable? :: It is unbiased and may spot problems or opportunities the designer hasn't noticed
Why should an evaluation refer back to the specification? :: The specification contains the measurable criteria, so each point can be objectively checked as met, partly met or not met
What does 'fit for purpose' mean? :: The product does the job it was designed for, safely and reliably, for its intended user
What is marketability? :: How likely a product is to sell – depends on demand, price, competition, brand, aesthetics and how well it meets user needs
What factors affect the cost of a product? :: Materials, labour, energy, equipment/tooling, overheads (rent, bills), scale of production, transport, packaging, marketing, profit margin
What is the difference between cost price and selling price? :: Cost price – what it costs to make ;; Selling price – what the customer pays (cost + overheads + profit + VAT)
What does feasibility mean in design? :: Whether a design can realistically be made with the available materials, processes, skills, time and budget
Explain how prototypes help to make a design commercially viable. (3 marks) :: Problems found and fixed before expensive tooling is made ;; Users/clients can give feedback so the product better meets their needs ;; Can test manufacturing methods and estimate costs ;; Can be shown to investors/retailers
What is a modification in the design process? :: A change made to a design after testing or feedback to improve it

## Health and safety in more detail
C: A {{c1::hazard}} is something with the potential to cause harm; {{c2::risk}} is the likelihood of that harm happening and how serious it would be.
State the five steps of a risk assessment. :: 1 Identify the hazards ;; 2 Decide who might be harmed and how ;; 3 Evaluate the risks and decide on control measures ;; 4 Record the findings ;; 5 Review and update regularly
Give safe working practices in a workshop. :: Tie hair back and remove loose clothing/jewellery, wear PPE, use machine guards, clamp work, know where emergency stops are, use dust extraction, never leave machines running, keep area tidy
What do safety sign colours mean? :: **Red** – prohibition/stop/fire equipment ;; **Blue** – mandatory (must do, e.g. wear goggles) ;; **Yellow** – warning/hazard ;; **Green** – safe condition (fire exit, first aid)
What does the Health and Safety at Work Act (1974) require? :: Employers must provide a safe workplace, training, equipment and PPE; employees must take care of themselves and others and follow safety rules
Why is ventilation/extraction needed in a workshop? :: To remove harmful dust (e.g. MDF), fumes from soldering, laser cutting, adhesives and finishes
Give ways electrical safety is ensured in school workshops. :: Low-voltage tools, RCD circuit breakers, PAT testing of equipment, emergency stop buttons, checking cables for damage
What safety features are required in toys? :: No small parts for under-3s (choking), non-toxic paints/finishes, no sharp edges or points, strong secure parts, CE/UKCA mark (Toy Safety Regulations)
What are the Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations? :: UK regulations requiring upholstered furniture and furnishings to pass fire-resistance tests and carry labels
Why do products carry safety warnings and instructions? :: To inform users how to use the product safely, warn about hazards and age limits, and protect the manufacturer legally
What does COSHH require you to do with hazardous substances? :: Identify them, store them safely, label them, use PPE and ventilation, and know what to do in an emergency (check the safety data sheet)

## Commercial viability
What is a unique selling point (USP)? :: A feature or benefit that makes a product different from and better than competitors' products
Why might a technically good product fail commercially? :: Too expensive, no real demand, poor marketing, strong competition, unappealing aesthetics, too costly to manufacture at scale

# 10 In depth – Papers and boards

## Sources and origins
What raw material is most paper made from? :: **Cellulose fibres** from wood pulp (mainly softwoods such as spruce and pine); also recycled paper, cotton rag or bamboo
Describe how paper is made from trees. :: Logs debarked and chipped → pulped (mechanically or chemically) → bleached and mixed with water, fillers and size → spread on a moving mesh where water drains → pressed between rollers → dried over heated rollers → calendered (smoothed) → wound on reels
C: Passing paper between heavy rollers to give a smooth, even surface is called {{c1::calendering}}.
Why are fillers such as china clay added to paper pulp? :: To make the paper smoother, whiter and more opaque so it prints better
What is sizing in paper making? :: Adding starch or resin to reduce absorbency so ink doesn't spread (bleed) into the paper
How is paper recycled? :: Collected and sorted → pulped with water → de-inked and cleaned → made into new paper (often mixed with new fibres)
Why can paper only be recycled a limited number of times? :: The cellulose fibres get shorter and weaker each time (about 5–7 times), so new fibres must be added
C: Paper becomes {{c1::board}} when its weight is above about {{c2::170–200 gsm}}.

## Types of paper
Give properties and uses of copier (printer) paper. :: About 80 gsm, smooth, bright white, cheap ;; Printing, photocopying, sketching
Give properties and uses of cartridge paper. :: About 120–150 gsm, slightly textured, thick and opaque, takes pencil, paint and ink well ;; Drawing, sketching, rendering
Give properties and uses of tracing paper. :: About 60–90 gsm, translucent, smooth, hard surface ;; Tracing and copying images, overlays
Give properties and uses of layout paper. :: Thin (about 50 gsm), lightweight, semi-translucent, cheap ;; Quick sketching and developing ideas – can trace over earlier drawings
Give properties and uses of bleed-proof paper. :: Smooth, coated so marker ink doesn't spread (bleed) ;; Rendering with spirit-based marker pens
What is grid paper used for? :: Paper printed with square or isometric grids to guide accurate sketching, orthographic and isometric drawings

## Types of board
Give properties and uses of folding boxboard (cartonboard). :: Rigid, good surface for printing, scores and folds without cracking ;; Food and cosmetic packaging, cereal boxes
Give properties and uses of corrugated card. :: A fluted (wavy) layer glued between two flat liners – strong, stiff, lightweight, cushions impact and insulates ;; Shipping boxes, pizza boxes, protective packaging
Give properties and uses of duplex board. :: Two layers – a smooth white side for printing and a grey/recycled side ;; Food packaging such as cereal and frozen-food boxes
Give properties and uses of solid white board. :: High-quality bleached board, white throughout, excellent print surface, expensive ;; Luxury packaging, cosmetics boxes, book covers
Give properties and uses of foil-lined board. :: Board with a thin aluminium foil layer – reflects heat, keeps food hot, moisture barrier ;; Takeaway container lids, drinks cartons
Give properties and uses of foam board (foamcore). :: Polystyrene foam sandwiched between two thin card sheets – very light, rigid, easy to cut ;; Architectural models, mounting displays, point-of-sale stands
What is mounting board? :: Thick, rigid coloured board used for mounting and framing artwork and photographs
Why are liquid food cartons (e.g. Tetra Pak) difficult to recycle? :: They are laminated layers of board, polyethylene and aluminium foil that must be separated by a special process
What is Corriflute? :: Corrugated polypropylene sheet – lightweight, waterproof and rigid – used for outdoor signs, estate agent boards and models

## Stock forms and sizes
C: An {{c1::A0}} sheet has an area of 1 m²; each smaller A size is {{c2::half}} the area of the size above.
State the sizes of A5, A4 and A3 paper. :: A5 = 148 × 210 mm ;; A4 = 210 × 297 mm ;; A3 = 297 × 420 mm
In what forms are papers and boards supplied? :: Flat sheets in standard sizes (A and B series), rolls/reels (for printing presses), pads; specified by weight (gsm) or thickness (microns)

## Ecological and social footprint
Give environmental problems caused by paper and board production. :: Deforestation if forests are not managed, large amounts of water and energy, chemicals (bleach) polluting rivers, transport emissions
Give environmental benefits of paper and board. :: Renewable source (if trees are replanted), biodegradable, widely recyclable, can be made from recycled fibres
Why is laminated or foil-lined board less sustainable than plain board? :: The plastic film or foil is bonded to the board, making it harder or impossible to recycle in normal paper recycling
What does an FSC or PEFC label on packaging tell the consumer? :: The paper or board comes from responsibly managed (certified) forests

## Commercial printing
Describe offset lithography. :: An aluminium plate carries the image; image areas attract oil-based ink and non-image areas attract water (oil and water don't mix); the ink is transferred (offset) to a rubber blanket roller, then onto the paper
Give advantages and disadvantages of offset lithography. :: **Advantages** – high quality, fast, cheap per copy for large runs ;; **Disadvantages** – expensive to set up (plates), not economical for short runs
Describe flexography. :: Flexible rubber or polymer relief plates on rotating cylinders apply fast-drying ink – prints on corrugated card, plastic films and labels for packaging
Describe screen printing. :: Ink is pushed through a fine mesh with a squeegee; a stencil blocks areas where ink isn't wanted; one screen per colour ;; Posters, T-shirts, signs – good for short runs
Describe digital printing. :: Inkjet or laser printers print directly from a computer file with no plates ;; Ideal for short runs and personalised printing, but cost per copy is higher for large runs
What is gravure printing? :: The image is engraved into a metal cylinder; ink fills the recesses and is transferred to the paper – very high quality and very long runs (magazines, stamps)
Which printing process is best for 100,000 full-colour magazines and why? :: Offset lithography (or gravure) – high quality and very low cost per copy once set up
Which printing process is best for 20 personalised invitations and why? :: Digital printing – no plates or set-up costs, each copy can be different

## Printing quality control
What are registration marks? :: Cross-hair marks printed outside the trim area – if the CMYK colours line up exactly the print is in register
What are colour bars (colour control strips)? :: Strips of solid colours and tints printed outside the trim area, checked by eye or with a densitometer to ensure consistent colour density
What are crop marks? :: Lines at the corners of a printed sheet showing where it should be trimmed
What is bleed in printing? :: Extending the printed image about 3 mm beyond the trim line so no white edges show after cutting
What is a printer's proof? :: A sample print checked and approved by the client before the full print run, to spot errors in text, colour or layout

## Cutting, scoring and forming
What is die cutting? :: A press uses a steel-rule die (sharp blades to cut, blunt rules to crease) to cut and crease packaging nets in one action – used in high-volume production
Give tools for cutting paper and board in school. :: Scissors, craft knife with safety rule on a cutting mat, rotary trimmer, guillotine, compass cutter, laser cutter, vinyl/craft cutter
Why should card be scored before folding? :: Gives a clean, accurate, straight fold and stops the card cracking or creasing in the wrong place
What is a net (development)? :: A flat shape that folds up to make a 3D form such as a box – includes glue tabs and flaps
What is perforation? :: A line of small cuts or holes so a section can be torn off easily (tickets, tear strips on packaging)
What are the main pop-up mechanisms? :: V-fold, parallel (floating layer), box/step fold, lever, slider and rotary (wheel) mechanisms
What is a crash-lock base? :: A pre-glued box base that locks into place automatically when the box is opened – quick to assemble on packing lines

## Finishes and treatments
C: {{c1::Embossing}} presses a design so it is raised from the surface; {{c2::debossing}} presses it in below the surface.
What is foil blocking (hot foil stamping)? :: A heated die presses metallic foil onto the surface to add a shiny gold/silver design – adds a luxury feel
What is spot UV varnish? :: A glossy varnish applied only to selected areas and cured (hardened) by UV light – highlights logos or images
What is lamination of paper/board? :: Bonding a thin plastic film to the surface – protects from moisture, dirt and wear and gives a gloss or matt finish
Why are finishes applied to printed packaging? :: Protect against moisture, scuffing and grease, improve durability and add visual/tactile appeal to attract customers

## Joining and standard components
Give adhesives used for paper and board. :: Glue stick (paper, clean, not very strong), PVA (strong, slow drying, can wrinkle paper), spray mount (repositionable, needs ventilation), hot-melt glue (fast, models), double-sided tape
Give standard components used with paper and board. :: Paper fasteners (split pins), staples, eyelets, plastic rivets, Velcro dots, bulldog clips, ring binder mechanisms, adhesive pads
How can nets be joined without glue? :: Tabs and slots, tuck-in flaps, interlocking (crash-lock) bases, slot-together joints

## Packaging
Give the functions of packaging. :: **Protect** the product ;; **Contain** it ;; **Preserve** (food) ;; **Inform** (ingredients, instructions, barcodes, legal symbols) ;; **Attract**/advertise the product ;; Make it easy to transport, stack and display
What information must often appear on packaging? :: Product name and brand, ingredients/contents, weight, barcode, safety warnings, recycling symbols, manufacturer details, age warnings
What is a blister pack? :: A thermoformed plastic bubble sealed onto a printed card backing – lets customers see the product and is hard to tamper with

# 11 In depth – Timber-based materials

## Sources, conversion and seasoning
C: {{c1::Hardwoods}} come from deciduous, broad-leaved trees; {{c2::softwoods}} come from coniferous trees with needles and cones.
Where do tropical hardwoods such as mahogany and teak come from and why is this a concern? :: Rainforests (e.g. South America, Africa, South-East Asia) – illegal or unmanaged logging causes deforestation, habitat loss and harms local communities
What is conversion of timber? :: Sawing felled logs into usable planks and boards
Compare through-and-through (plain) sawing and quarter sawing. :: **Through-and-through** – parallel cuts, quick, cheap, little waste, but boards more likely to warp ;; **Quarter sawn** – cut radially, more stable and decorative but more waste and expensive
What is seasoning of timber? :: Drying timber to reduce its moisture content, so it is stable and suitable for use
Why must timber be seasoned? :: Reduces shrinking, warping and splitting; increases strength; reduces risk of rot and fungal attack; makes it easier to glue and finish
Describe air seasoning. :: Planks stacked with spacers (stickers) under a roof so air circulates – cheap, little energy, but slow (years) and only reaches about 15–20% moisture
Describe kiln seasoning. :: Timber dried in a heated chamber with controlled temperature and humidity – fast (days/weeks), can reach 6–12% moisture for indoor use, but uses more energy and costs more
What tool checks the moisture content of timber? :: A moisture meter (two probes measure electrical resistance)
Name common defects in timber. :: Knots, splits/shakes, cupping, bowing, twisting (winding), springing, insect and fungal damage (rot)
C: {{c1::Cupping}} is when a board curls across its width; {{c2::bowing}} is when it curves along its length.

## Properties and uses of specific woods
Give properties and uses of ash. :: Tough, flexible, shock-absorbing, straight grain, pale ;; Tool handles, sports equipment (hockey sticks, bats), ladders, bent furniture
Give properties and uses of beech. :: Hard, tough, close fine grain, pale, food-safe, can be steam bent ;; Toys, kitchen utensils, chopping boards, workbenches, chair frames
Give properties and uses of mahogany. :: Reddish-brown, fairly easy to work, durable, attractive grain ;; High-quality furniture, panelling, veneers
Give properties and uses of balsa. :: Extremely light and soft, easy to cut with a knife, low strength ;; Modelling, model aircraft, rafts
Give properties and uses of teak. :: Very durable, naturally oily so resists water and rot, heavy ;; Outdoor furniture, boat decks, kitchen worktops
Give properties and uses of larch. :: Durable, tough, resinous, resists rot better than most softwoods ;; Cladding, fencing, boat building, outdoor structures
Give properties and uses of spruce. :: Light, pale, fairly strong for its weight, straight grain ;; Construction timber, musical instruments, paper pulp
Give properties and uses of cedar (western red cedar). :: Lightweight, naturally resistant to rot and insects, aromatic ;; Sheds, cladding, outdoor furniture, roof shingles
Why is softwood generally cheaper than hardwood? :: Softwood trees grow faster and straighter (ready in about 30 years), so timber is plentiful and quick to replace

## Manufactured boards in detail
Give properties and uses of plywood. :: Odd number of veneers glued with grain at 90° to each other – strong in all directions, stable, doesn't split ;; Furniture, flooring, boat building (marine ply), shelving
Give properties and uses of chipboard. :: Wood chips bonded with resin – cheap, stable, but weak, crumbles at edges and swells when wet; often veneered or melamine faced ;; Kitchen units, worktops, flat-pack furniture
Give properties and uses of hardboard. :: Compressed wood fibres, one smooth face and one textured – thin, cheap, not very strong ;; Drawer bottoms, cupboard backs, clipboards
What is OSB (oriented strand board)? :: Large wood strands layered in different directions and bonded with resin – strong, cheap ;; Building sheathing, flooring, hoarding
Why must MDF dust be controlled? :: The fine dust and resin (urea formaldehyde) are harmful if inhaled – use extraction and a dust mask
Why are manufactured boards often veneered or laminated? :: To give an attractive real-wood or decorative, hard-wearing surface over a cheap, stable core
What does 'marine plywood' mean? :: Plywood made with waterproof adhesive and durable veneers, so it doesn't delaminate in wet conditions

## Stock forms
What does PAR mean? :: Planed All Round – timber planed smooth on all four sides (slightly smaller than its nominal sawn size)
In what stock forms is timber supplied? :: Rough-sawn or PAR planks and boards, square and round (dowel) sections, strips, mouldings and standard sheets of manufactured board (2440 × 1220 mm)
What is the difference between nominal and actual size of planed timber? :: Nominal size is the sawn size before planing; actual size is a few millimetres smaller after planing

## Ecological and social footprint
Give environmental advantages of using timber. :: Renewable if replanted, low embodied energy, biodegradable, stores carbon, can be reused or recycled into manufactured boards or biomass fuel
Give environmental disadvantages of using timber. :: Deforestation and habitat loss if unmanaged, transport emissions for imported timber, toxic preservatives and finishes, adhesives in boards (formaldehyde)
Why are manufactured boards considered both sustainable and unsustainable? :: **Sustainable** – use waste wood, chips and fast-grown timber ;; **Unsustainable** – contain resin adhesives that release formaldehyde and make them hard to recycle or compost

## Shaping and forming timber
Name tools used to waste (cut and shape) wood by hand. :: Tenon saw, coping saw, chisels, smoothing/jack plane, surform, rasp, files, spokeshave, hand drill
Name machines used to shape wood. :: Bandsaw, scroll saw, jigsaw, pillar drill, router, planer-thicknesser, wood lathe, belt/disc sander, CNC router, laser cutter
What is wood turning? :: Shaping wood on a lathe – between centres for long items (chair legs, table legs) or on a faceplate for bowls and plates
Describe steam bending. :: Solid timber (e.g. beech, ash) is steamed (about 1 hour per 25 mm thickness) to soften the fibres, bent around a former and clamped until it dries and holds its shape
Describe laminating timber to make a curved shape. :: Thin veneers are coated in glue (e.g. PVA or urea formaldehyde), stacked and clamped over a former (or in a vacuum bag) until set – the curve keeps its shape
Give advantages of laminated curves compared with cutting a curve from solid timber. :: Stronger (no short grain), less waste, stable, can make tight curves (e.g. Eames chairs, skateboards)
What is kerfing? :: Cutting a series of saw cuts part way through the back of a board so it can be bent into a curve
What is routing? :: Using a high-speed rotating cutter to cut grooves, rebates, profiles and decorative edges

## Joining timber
Describe a butt joint and its use. :: Two pieces glued/nailed end to face – simplest but weakest joint ;; Cheap boxes and frames, often reinforced with blocks or dowels
Describe a dowel joint and its use. :: Wooden pegs glued into matching holes in both parts – neat, quick with a jig ;; Flat-pack and carcase furniture
Describe a mortise and tenon joint and its use. :: A tenon (tongue) fits into a rectangular mortise (hole) – very strong ;; Table and chair frames, doors
Describe a dovetail joint and its use. :: Interlocking wedge-shaped tails and pins – very strong, resists pulling apart, decorative ;; Drawers and quality boxes
Describe a comb (finger) joint and its use. :: Interlocking square fingers – large gluing area, strong ;; Boxes, often cut on a router or CNC
Describe a housing joint and its use. :: A groove cut across one board to take the end of another ;; Shelves in bookcases and cabinets
Describe a mitre joint and its use. :: Ends cut at 45° to form a corner – hides end grain, neat but weak unless reinforced ;; Picture frames
What is a lap joint? :: One piece overlaps a recess (rebate) cut in the other – simple, more gluing area than a butt joint ;; Drawer fronts, frames
What is a biscuit joint? :: An oval compressed beech 'biscuit' glued into slots cut in both pieces – quick alignment and strengthening of edge and corner joints
What are knock-down (KD) fittings? :: Fixings that allow furniture to be assembled and taken apart easily with simple tools – e.g. cam locks, cross dowels, corner blocks, screw blocks
Why are knock-down fittings used in flat-pack furniture? :: Allow the customer to assemble it at home, so it can be packed flat, reducing storage and transport costs; can be taken apart for moving
C: When screwing two pieces of wood together, the top piece needs a {{c1::clearance hole}} and the bottom piece needs a {{c2::pilot hole}}.
Why are screw holes countersunk? :: So countersunk screw heads sit flush with (or below) the surface
Give types of nail and uses. :: Round wire (general rough work), oval (joinery – less likely to split), panel pins (small, fine work, attaching thin panels)
Give types of hinge used on wooden products. :: Butt hinge (doors), flush hinge (no recess needed), piano/continuous hinge (long lids), concealed kitchen hinge, tee hinge (gates, sheds)
What is PVA glue used for? :: Interior woodwork – strong, safe, cheap, cleans up with water; parts must be clamped while it sets (not waterproof unless exterior grade)
What is a contact adhesive used for? :: Bonding sheet laminates to boards – applied to both surfaces, left to go tacky, then pressed together (instant bond, needs ventilation)

## Finishing timber
What does the grade number of glasspaper indicate? :: A low number is coarse (e.g. 80); a high number is fine (e.g. 240) – work from coarse to fine
What is sanding sealer? :: A thin first coat that seals the grain and raises fibres so they can be sanded smooth before a top finish
Give properties of polyurethane varnish. :: Clear, hard-wearing, waterproof, gloss/satin/matt – protects and shows the grain
What does wood stain do? :: Changes or enhances the colour of wood while showing the grain – gives little protection on its own
Why are oils (e.g. Danish, teak, linseed) used on wood? :: Penetrate the wood to give a natural, water-resistant finish that enhances grain; some are food safe (chopping boards, salad bowls)
What is a preservative (e.g. tanalising, creosote) used for? :: Protects outdoor timber from rot, fungi and insects – pressure treated into fences, sheds and decking
How is wood painted for a durable finish? :: Sand and fill, apply primer, then undercoat, then one or more top coats (gloss or satin)

## Commercial production and quality control
How is wooden furniture batch produced? :: CNC routers cut parts from sheet material using nesting software; jigs for drilling; standard KD fittings; spray finishing lines
Give quality control checks used when making a wooden product. :: Check moisture content, measure against tolerances, check squareness with a try square or by measuring diagonals, use jigs/templates for repeated holes, inspect finish
Why are equal diagonals used to check a frame? :: If the two diagonal measurements are equal, the frame is square

# 12 In depth – Metal-based materials

## Sources and extraction
How are metals obtained? :: Ores are mined or quarried from the Earth's crust, then the metal is extracted by heating (smelting) or electrolysis, then refined
C: Iron is extracted from iron ore in a {{c1::blast furnace}} using coke and limestone; aluminium is extracted from {{c2::bauxite}} by electrolysis.
How is steel made from iron? :: Excess carbon in pig iron is burned off by blowing oxygen through it (basic oxygen furnace); scrap steel is melted in an electric arc furnace
Why is extracting aluminium very energy intensive? :: It needs electrolysis, which uses huge amounts of electricity
Why is recycling aluminium so beneficial? :: It uses only about 5% of the energy needed to extract new aluminium, and it can be recycled again and again without losing quality
How can steel be separated from other waste for recycling? :: It is magnetic (ferrous), so it can be removed with large magnets
Give environmental impacts of metal production. :: Mining destroys landscapes and habitats, high energy use and CO2 emissions in extraction, toxic waste, finite ores – but metals are highly recyclable

## Ferrous metals
How much carbon does mild steel contain? :: About 0.15–0.35% carbon
Give properties and uses of high-carbon (tool) steel. :: About 0.7–1.4% carbon – very hard and can be hardened and tempered, but less ductile ;; Chisels, files, saw blades, screwdrivers, drill bits
Give properties and uses of cast iron. :: 2–4% carbon – hard, brittle, strong in compression, casts well, rusts ;; Vices, engine blocks, manhole covers, cookware, machine beds
Give properties and uses of stainless steel. :: Steel alloyed with chromium (at least about 10.5%) and nickel – hard, tough, does not rust, hygienic ;; Cutlery, sinks, surgical tools, kitchen equipment
Why does mild steel need a finish? :: It rusts (corrodes) when exposed to oxygen and water
C: Rust forms when iron reacts with {{c1::oxygen}} and {{c2::water}} to form iron oxide.

## Non-ferrous metals and alloys
Give properties and uses of copper. :: Excellent conductor of heat and electricity, ductile, malleable, corrosion resistant ;; Electrical wire, PCB tracks, water pipes, saucepan bases
Give properties and uses of zinc. :: Corrosion resistant, low melting point ;; Coating steel (galvanising), die-cast parts, roofing
Give properties and uses of tin. :: Soft, corrosion resistant, low melting point ;; Coating steel food cans, main ingredient of solder and pewter
Give properties and uses of brass. :: Alloy of copper and zinc – gold colour, corrosion resistant, casts and machines well ;; Door handles, plumbing fittings, musical instruments, ornaments
Give properties and uses of bronze. :: Alloy of copper and tin – hard, tough, corrosion resistant ;; Statues, bearings, ship propellers, medals
Give properties and uses of duralumin. :: Alloy of aluminium with copper (plus magnesium and manganese) – nearly as strong as mild steel but much lighter ;; Aircraft structures, high-performance vehicles
What is pewter? :: An alloy mainly of tin (with small amounts of antimony/copper) – low melting point (about 170–230 °C) so it can be cast in schools into MDF or silicone moulds
Why are metals often alloyed? :: To improve properties such as strength, hardness, corrosion resistance or castability, or to lower the melting point
What is the difference between a ferrous and a non-ferrous alloy? :: A ferrous alloy contains iron (e.g. stainless steel); a non-ferrous alloy does not (e.g. brass, bronze, duralumin)

## Stock forms
Why is metal supplied in standard sections such as angle, channel and I-beam? :: These shapes give high stiffness and strength for little weight and are easy to join into structures
How is the size of metal stock specified? :: By its cross-section dimensions (e.g. diameter of rod, width × thickness of flat bar, wall thickness of tube) and length; sheet by thickness (gauge or mm)

## Heat treatment and work hardening
What is work hardening? :: When metal is repeatedly bent, hammered or stretched it becomes harder and more brittle and may crack
C: {{c1::Annealing}} is heating metal to a set temperature and cooling it slowly to make it softer, more ductile and relieve work hardening.
How do you anneal aluminium in a school workshop? :: Rub soap on it and heat gently until the soap turns black (about 350 °C), then leave to cool
Describe hardening of high-carbon steel. :: Heat to cherry red (about 800 °C) and quench quickly in water or oil – it becomes very hard but brittle
Describe tempering of high-carbon steel. :: After hardening, clean to bright metal and reheat gently, watching the oxide colours (pale straw to blue); quench at the right colour – reduces brittleness and increases toughness while keeping some hardness
Which tempering colours are used for cutting tools and for springs? :: Pale straw (about 230 °C) – hard cutting tools such as scribers and lathe tools ;; Blue (about 300 °C) – tougher items such as springs and screwdrivers
What is case hardening? :: Adding carbon to the surface of mild steel by heating it in carbon-rich material, then quenching – gives a hard wear-resistant skin with a tough core
What is normalising? :: Heating steel and letting it cool in air to relieve internal stresses and refine its grain structure

## Shaping metals – wasting
Name tools for cutting metal by hand. :: Hacksaw, junior hacksaw, tin snips (sheet), bench shears/guillotine, cold chisel, files, hand drill
Compare cross filing and draw filing. :: **Cross filing** – pushing the file across the work to remove material quickly ;; **Draw filing** – sliding the file sideways along the work to give a smooth finish
What is a centre lathe used for? :: Turning round metal or plastic bar – facing off, parallel turning, taper turning, drilling, boring, knurling and cutting threads
What is knurling? :: Pressing a diamond or straight pattern into the surface of round bar on a lathe to give grip (e.g. tool handles)
What is milling? :: A rotating multi-toothed cutter removes metal to produce flat surfaces, slots and grooves (manual or CNC)
C: An internal thread is cut with a {{c1::tap}}; an external thread is cut with a {{c2::die}}.
What size hole must be drilled before tapping a thread? :: A tapping-size hole, smaller than the thread diameter (e.g. 6.8 mm for an M8 thread)
What is plasma cutting? :: An electrically ionised gas jet melts and cuts through sheet metal quickly – often CNC controlled
Why is a centre punch used before drilling metal? :: Makes a small dent so the drill bit starts in the right place and doesn't wander

## Shaping metals – deforming and reforming
Describe sand casting in more detail. :: A pattern (with draft angles) is packed in sand in a two-part box (cope and drag); it is removed, leaving a cavity; molten metal is poured down the sprue, through runners, with risers to let air out; when cool the casting is broken out and fettled
What is investment (lost wax) casting? :: A wax pattern is coated in ceramic slurry; the wax is melted out and molten metal poured into the cavity – very fine detail ;; Jewellery, turbine blades, dental parts
What is forging? :: Shaping hot metal by hammering or pressing (drop forging uses dies) – aligns the grain, making parts very strong ;; Spanners, crankshafts
What is press forming (stamping)? :: Sheet metal is pressed between matched male and female dies to form a shape ;; Car body panels, kitchen sinks, trays
What is deep drawing? :: Sheet metal is drawn into a deep shape by a punch pushing it into a die ;; Drinks cans, saucepans
What is metal spinning? :: A disc of sheet metal is spun on a lathe and pressed over a former to make round hollow shapes ;; Lampshades, bowls, cymbals
How is metal wire made? :: By drawing (pulling) rod through a series of progressively smaller dies
What is extrusion used for in metals? :: Pushing hot aluminium through a shaped die to make long constant profiles ;; Window frames, rails, heat sinks
How is bending of metal done in a workshop? :: Using a vice, folding bars, sheet metal folder/bender, bending jig or scrolling tool; thick steel may be heated first
What is fettling? :: Removing unwanted metal (sprue, runners, flash) from a casting and cleaning it up with files and saws

## Joining metals
Describe soft soldering. :: Joining with a tin-based alloy that melts at a low temperature (about 180–230 °C) – easy but weak ;; Electronics, plumbing
Describe hard (silver) soldering. :: Joining with a silver-based alloy melting at about 600–800 °C using a brazing torch – stronger than soft solder ;; Jewellery, copper and brass work
Describe brazing. :: Joining (usually mild steel) with brass spelter melting at about 850–900 °C – stronger than soldering, metals themselves do not melt
What is welding? :: Melting the edges of the parent metals together (often with a filler rod) to form a very strong permanent joint – e.g. MIG, TIG, arc, oxy-acetylene, spot welding
Why is flux used when soldering and brazing? :: It cleans the joint and prevents oxides forming when heated, so the solder flows and bonds properly
What is spot welding used for? :: Joining overlapping sheet metal using electrodes that pass a high current through a small spot – fast and automated (car bodies)
What is a pop rivet? :: A rivet set from one side only using a rivet gun – used when the back of the joint can't be reached (e.g. joining sheet metal)
Give temporary (non-permanent) methods of joining metal. :: Nuts and bolts with washers, machine screws, self-tapping screws, set screws
Why is a washer used under a nut? :: Spreads the load, protects the surface and (spring washer) helps stop the nut working loose
What is the difference between a bolt and a machine screw? :: A bolt usually has a plain shank and is used with a nut; a machine screw is threaded along its length and often screws into a tapped hole

## Finishing metals
Describe powder coating. :: Electrostatically charged polymer powder is sprayed onto the metal and then baked so it melts into a tough, even coating ;; Bikes, garden furniture, radiators
Describe plastic dip coating. :: Heated metal is dipped into fluidised polymer powder, which melts onto it to form a thick soft coating ;; Tool handles, dish racks
Describe galvanising. :: Steel is dipped in molten zinc – the zinc protects it from rusting even if scratched ;; Fences, buckets, crash barriers, nails
Describe electroplating. :: Electrolysis deposits a thin layer of another metal (chrome, nickel, silver, gold) onto the surface – decorative and corrosion resistant ;; Taps, jewellery, cutlery
Describe anodising. :: An electrolytic process that thickens the protective oxide layer on aluminium; it can be dyed in bright colours ;; Torches, carabiners, phone cases
Why is lacquer applied to polished brass or copper? :: A clear coating that stops the metal tarnishing (oxidising) while showing its colour
How should mild steel be prepared before painting? :: Remove rust and grease (degrease), then apply a primer (e.g. red oxide) before top coats
Why is oil applied to steel tools? :: Forms a barrier against moisture to prevent rusting

## Quality control and production
Give quality control methods for metal products. :: Measuring with micrometers and vernier calipers to tolerance, go/no-go gauges, feeler gauges, inspecting welds (visual, dye penetrant, X-ray), checking surface finish
How are metal parts produced on a large scale? :: Press forming, die casting, extrusion, CNC machining and robotic welding on production lines

# 13 In depth – Polymers

## Sources and structure
Describe how polymers are made from crude oil. :: Crude oil is separated by **fractional distillation** → fractions (e.g. naphtha) are **cracked** into small molecules (monomers such as ethene) → monomers are joined by **polymerisation** into long chains
C: {{c1::Polymerisation}} is the chemical process of joining many small {{c2::monomers}} into long chain molecules.
C: {{c1::Cracking}} breaks long hydrocarbon molecules from crude oil into shorter, more useful molecules.
Explain why thermoforming polymers can be reshaped but thermosetting polymers cannot. :: Thermoforming – long chains are held by weak forces that break when heated, so chains can slide and re-form ;; Thermosetting – chains are joined by strong permanent **cross-links** formed when cured, so heating cannot soften them
What is a biopolymer? :: A polymer made from renewable plant sources (e.g. PLA from corn starch, bio-PE from sugar cane) – some are biodegradable
What are polymer fillers and pigments? :: **Fillers** – cheap powders added to bulk out a polymer and reduce cost ;; **Pigments** – colourants added so the polymer is coloured throughout (no need to paint)

## Thermoforming polymers – properties and uses
Give properties and uses of HIPS (high impact polystyrene). :: Rigid, lightweight, impact resistant, cheap, easy to vacuum form ;; Vacuum-formed packaging, yoghurt pots, toys, model kits
Give properties and uses of PET. :: Clear, tough, lightweight, good barrier to gas and moisture, recyclable (code 1) ;; Drinks bottles, food trays; recycled into fleece fibre
Give properties and uses of HDPE. :: Stiff, strong, tough, chemical and water resistant (code 2) ;; Milk bottles, bins, pipes, bottle crates, chopping boards
Give properties and uses of LDPE. :: Soft, flexible, tough, chemical resistant (code 4) ;; Carrier bags, squeezy bottles, cling film, cable insulation
Give properties and uses of PVC. :: Rigid uPVC – tough, weather and chemical resistant ;; Window frames, pipes, guttering ;; Flexible PVC (with plasticiser) – cable insulation, raincoats, flooring
Give properties and uses of ABS. :: Tough, high impact strength, scratch resistant, glossy finish ;; Lego, phone and kettle casings, vacuum cleaner bodies, 3D printing filament
Give properties and uses of polycarbonate. :: Very tough, transparent, high impact resistance, heat resistant ;; Safety glasses, riot shields, phone cases, roof sheeting
Give properties and uses of nylon (polyamide) as a solid. :: Tough, hard-wearing, low friction (self-lubricating) ;; Gears, bearings, cable ties, zip teeth
Give properties and uses of expanded polystyrene (EPS). :: Very light, rigid foam, good thermal insulator, absorbs shocks ;; Protective packaging, takeaway cups, insulation, cycle helmet liners
Why is acrylic used for illuminated signs? :: Transparent with excellent light transmission, can be coloured, laser cuts with polished edges, weather resistant

## Thermosetting polymers – properties and uses
Give properties and uses of epoxy resin. :: Very strong adhesive and casting resin, good electrical insulator, chemical resistant ;; Adhesives (e.g. Araldite), matrix for carbon fibre, PCB coating
Give properties and uses of melamine formaldehyde. :: Hard, scratch and heat resistant, food safe, rigid ;; Laminate surfaces on kitchen worktops, tableware, picnic plates
Give properties and uses of polyester resin. :: Rigid, hard, brittle alone, sets with a catalyst (hardener) ;; With glass fibre to make GRP (boats, car bodies), casting/embedding
Give properties and uses of urea formaldehyde. :: Hard, rigid, heat resistant, good electrical insulator ;; Electrical plugs, sockets and switches; adhesive in MDF
Give properties and uses of phenol formaldehyde. :: Dark (brown/black), hard, heat resistant, electrical insulator ;; Pan handles, saucepan knobs, electrical fittings (Bakelite)
Why are thermosetting polymers used for electrical fittings and pan handles? :: They don't soften when hot and are good electrical and thermal insulators

## Ecological and social footprint
Give environmental problems caused by polymers. :: Made from finite crude oil; energy-intensive production; take hundreds of years to decompose in landfill; litter harms wildlife; microplastics in oceans and food chains; toxic fumes if burned
How can the environmental impact of polymers be reduced? :: Recycle thermoforming polymers (codes 1–7), use recycled or biopolymers, reduce wall thickness, design for disassembly, single-polymer products, energy recovery by incineration
Why are thermosetting polymers rarely recycled? :: Their cross-links mean they can't be melted down and reformed – at most they are ground up as filler
Why are products made from a single type of polymer easier to recycle? :: Recycling plants need polymers to be sorted by type; mixed or bonded polymers are hard to separate

## Industrial processes
Describe injection moulding in detail. :: Granules fed from a **hopper** → heated by heater bands while an **Archimedean screw** moves them forward → screw rams molten polymer into a closed **mould** under pressure → held while it cools → mould opens and **ejector pins** push the product out
Give advantages and disadvantages of injection moulding. :: **Advantages** – complex accurate 3D shapes, very fast cycle, low unit cost, good finish, little waste ;; **Disadvantages** – very expensive moulds (tooling), only economic in large quantities
How can you tell a product was injection moulded? :: Ejector pin marks, a sprue/gate mark, split (parting) lines, uniform wall thickness, ribs and bosses inside
What is a draft angle and why is it needed in moulds? :: A slight taper on the sides of a mould or former so the product can be removed (ejected) easily
What is flash on a moulded product? :: Thin excess polymer that has squeezed out at the joint (split line) of the mould – must be trimmed off
What is injection stretch blow moulding? :: An injection-moulded preform (test tube shape with screw neck) is reheated, stretched and blown into a mould – used for PET drinks bottles
Describe rotational moulding. :: Polymer powder is put into a hollow mould which is heated while rotating in two axes, coating the inside; it is cooled and opened ;; Large hollow seamless products – kayaks, traffic cones, water tanks, play equipment
Describe compression moulding. :: A measured charge of thermosetting polymer is placed in a heated mould and pressed into shape, where it cures ;; Melamine plates, electrical fittings
What is calendering of polymers? :: Passing molten polymer between heated rollers to make sheet and film of an even thickness
Which industrial process would you use to make a drinks bottle, a pipe, a kayak and a bottle cap? :: Bottle – blow moulding ;; Pipe – extrusion ;; Kayak – rotational moulding ;; Bottle cap – injection moulding

## Shaping and forming in the workshop
Give design rules for a vacuum-forming mould. :: Draft angle (about 5°) on sides, rounded corners and edges, no undercuts, vent holes in deep recesses, smooth surface, not too deep
What is webbing in vacuum forming and how is it prevented? :: Folds of plastic that form between tall or close parts of a mould ;; Space parts further apart, reduce height, use a draft angle and correct heating
What is drape forming? :: Heated thermoforming sheet is draped over a former and allowed to cool in that shape (e.g. curved acrylic display)
What is press moulding (plug and yoke)? :: A softened polymer sheet is pressed between a male plug and female yoke to form a simple 3D shape such as a dish
How are acrylic edges finished? :: Draw file, then wet-and-dry paper (silicon carbide) from coarse to fine used wet, then polish with a buffing wheel or acrylic polish
Why is acrylic supplied with protective film? :: Prevents scratching during storage, marking out and cutting – remove only when finished
How should acrylic be drilled to avoid cracking or chipping? :: Use a slow speed, a sharp bit (or one ground for plastics), backing board and no excessive pressure

## Joining polymers
What is Tensol cement? :: A solvent cement for acrylic that softens the surfaces so they fuse together – needs ventilation
Give methods of joining polymers. :: Solvent cement (Tensol), epoxy resin, hot-melt glue, nuts and bolts, self-tapping screws into moulded bosses, snap fits, rivets, plastic welding (heat or ultrasonic)
What is a snap fit? :: A moulded hook or lug that flexes and clicks into a recess, joining parts without screws or glue – quick to assemble
What is a living hinge? :: A thin flexible section of polypropylene that can bend thousands of times without breaking – e.g. flip-top lids
What is a boss in a moulded product? :: A raised cylindrical moulded feature that receives a screw or locates parts

## Finishing polymers
Why do most polymer products not need an applied finish? :: They are **self-finishing** – coloured throughout by pigments and take the smooth or textured surface of the mould
Give ways of adding decoration or information to polymer products. :: Printing (pad printing, screen printing), in-mould labels/decoration, vinyl decals, textured moulds, embossed text in the mould, flame polishing edges

## Quality control and standard components
Give quality control checks for moulded products. :: Monitor mould temperature and pressure, check dimensions and wall thickness, inspect for flash, sink marks, short shots (incomplete filling) and warping
What is a short shot? :: An incomplete moulding caused when not enough polymer fills the mould
What is a sink mark? :: A small dent on the surface of a moulding where a thick section shrank as it cooled – avoided by even wall thickness and ribs
Give standard components used with polymer products. :: Self-tapping screws, snap fits, threaded inserts, clips, hinges, nuts and bolts, battery clips

# 14 In depth – Textiles

## Sources and origins of fibres
Where does cotton come from? :: The seed pod (boll) of the cotton plant – a natural plant (cellulose) fibre
Where does linen come from? :: The stem of the flax plant – a natural plant fibre
Where does wool come from? :: The fleece of sheep (also goats, e.g. cashmere, mohair) – a natural animal (protein) fibre
Where does silk come from? :: The cocoon of the silkworm, unwound as one long filament – a natural animal fibre
Where do synthetic fibres such as polyester and nylon come from? :: Crude oil – chemicals are polymerised and the molten polymer is forced through a spinneret to form filaments
What are regenerated fibres? Give examples. :: Fibres made by chemically processing natural cellulose (usually wood pulp) ;; Viscose (rayon), lyocell (Tencel), acetate
C: {{c1::Staple}} fibres are short (e.g. cotton, wool); {{c2::filament}} fibres are very long and continuous (e.g. silk, synthetics).
Describe how fibres become fabric. :: Fibres are cleaned and combed → twisted (spun) into **yarn** → yarn is woven or knitted into fabric (or fibres are bonded directly into non-wovens)
Why is yarn twisted? :: Twisting holds the fibres together, giving the yarn strength

## Properties and uses of fibres
Give properties and uses of cotton. :: Absorbent, strong, cool and breathable, soft, washes well, but creases, shrinks and dries slowly ;; Jeans, T-shirts, towels, bedding
Give properties and uses of wool. :: Warm (fibres trap air), absorbent, elastic, crease resistant, naturally flame resistant, but can shrink and felt when washed hot ;; Jumpers, coats, suits, carpets
Give properties and uses of silk. :: Smooth, lustrous, soft, drapes well, strong, absorbent, but expensive and needs careful washing ;; Ties, scarves, lingerie, evening wear
Give properties and uses of linen. :: Very strong, cool, absorbent, smooth, but creases very easily ;; Summer clothing, tea towels, tablecloths
Give properties and uses of polyester. :: Strong, durable, crease resistant, quick drying, not absorbent, can be made from recycled PET ;; Sportswear, fleece, blended shirts, duvet filling
Give properties and uses of polyamide (nylon) fibre. :: Very strong, elastic, abrasion resistant, lightweight, quick drying, not absorbent ;; Tights, ropes, tents, outdoor and sportswear
Give properties and uses of elastane (Lycra). :: Extremely stretchy and recovers its shape; used in small amounts blended with other fibres ;; Swimwear, sportswear, skinny jeans, underwear
Give properties and uses of acrylic fibre. :: Soft, warm, lightweight, wool-like, cheap, but can pill and is not absorbent ;; Knitwear, fake fur, blankets
Give properties and uses of viscose. :: Soft, absorbent, drapes well, cool, but weak when wet and creases ;; Dresses, blouses, linings
Why are synthetic fibres often less comfortable than natural fibres in hot weather? :: They are not absorbent, so sweat isn't taken away from the skin and the fabric feels clammy

## Fabric construction
C: In a woven fabric the {{c1::warp}} threads run along the length (parallel to the selvedge) and the {{c2::weft}} threads run across the width.
What is the selvedge? :: The tightly woven edge of a fabric that runs parallel to the warp and doesn't fray
Describe plain weave. :: Each weft thread goes over one warp thread and under the next – firm, strong, hard-wearing, even surface ;; Calico, poplin, cotton shirts
Describe twill weave. :: Weft passes over two or more warps, stepping along each row to make a diagonal pattern – strong, hard-wearing, hides dirt ;; Denim, drill, chinos
Describe satin weave. :: Long floats of yarn passing over several threads – smooth, lustrous surface that drapes, but snags easily ;; Linings, evening wear, ribbons
What is the bias of a fabric? :: The direction at 45° to the warp and weft – the fabric is stretchiest along the bias and drapes well
What is grain in fabric and why does it matter when cutting? :: The direction of the warp (straight grain); pattern pieces are placed along the grain so garments hang correctly and don't twist
Compare weft-knitted and warp-knitted fabrics. :: **Weft knit** – one yarn loops across the width; very stretchy, can ladder/unravel (jersey T-shirts, jumpers) ;; **Warp knit** – loops formed vertically by machine; less stretchy, doesn't ladder or unravel (swimwear, sportswear, net)
How is felt made? :: Wool fibres are matted together using heat, moisture, pressure and agitation, so the scales on the fibres lock together
What are bonded non-woven fabrics? :: Webs of fibres held together by adhesive, heat or needle-punching ;; Disposable wipes, interfacing, medical masks, tea bags
C: In a {{c1::blend}} different fibres are mixed together before being spun into a yarn; in a {{c2::mixture}} different yarns are used together in one fabric.
Why is a small amount of elastane blended with cotton in jeans? :: Gives stretch and shape recovery for comfort and fit while keeping cotton's breathability

## Ecological and social footprint
Give environmental problems of cotton production. :: Needs huge amounts of water and pesticides/fertilisers; can drain rivers and pollute land; poor pay and conditions for workers in some countries
What is organic cotton? :: Cotton grown without synthetic pesticides and fertilisers – better for soil, water and farmers' health but lower yield and more expensive
Give environmental problems of synthetic fibres. :: Made from finite crude oil, energy-intensive, not biodegradable, shed microfibres into water when washed
Give environmental problems caused by fast fashion. :: Huge use of resources, cheap low-quality clothing worn briefly then thrown away, landfill and incineration, water pollution from dyes, exploited workers
How can textiles be recycled or reused? :: Charity shops and resale, upcycling, shredding fibres to make new yarn or insulation/stuffing, PET bottles recycled into polyester fleece
Why is lyocell (Tencel) considered a sustainable fibre? :: Made from wood pulp from managed forests in a closed-loop process that recycles almost all its solvent; biodegradable

## Patterns, lay plans and cutting
What is a pattern in textiles? :: A paper or card template of each garment piece, including seam allowances and markings
What is the standard seam allowance in the UK? :: 1.5 cm (15 mm)
Name common pattern markings. :: Grain line arrow, place-on-fold line, notches (for matching pieces), darts, buttonhole and pocket positions, cutting line, stitching line
What is a lay plan? :: An arrangement of pattern pieces on the fabric that follows the grain and minimises waste – often produced by CAD in industry
How is fabric cut in industry? :: Many layers stacked (a lay) and cut with a band knife or straight knife, die cutting (press), or CNC laser/knife cutters controlled by CAD lay plans
Why does laser cutting suit synthetic fabrics? :: It is accurate and fast and the heat seals (melts) the edges so they don't fray

## Seams, shaping and edges
Describe a plain seam. :: Two pieces placed right sides together and stitched 1.5 cm from the edge; raw edges are neatened – the most common seam
Describe a French seam and its use. :: Stitched twice so the raw edges are enclosed inside – neat with no fraying ;; Sheer/fine fabrics, lingerie, blouses
Describe a flat-fell seam and its use. :: Raw edges folded under and stitched flat with two rows of stitching – very strong and hard-wearing ;; Jeans, workwear, shirts
What does an overlocker do? :: Trims, stitches and neatens the edge of fabric in one action, using several threads – used for seams on stretch fabrics and neatening
Give methods of neatening raw edges. :: Overlocking, zigzag stitch, pinking shears, bias binding, French seam, turning and stitching (hem)
What is a dart? :: A tapered, stitched fold that takes in fabric to shape it around body curves (bust, waist)
What are pleats and gathers? :: **Pleats** – folds of fabric pressed and stitched to control fullness ;; **Gathers** – fabric drawn up evenly on a thread to give soft fullness
What is shirring? :: Several rows of stitching using elastic thread to gather fabric so it stretches (e.g. cuffs, summer tops)
What is interfacing and why is it used? :: An extra layer (often fusible – ironed on) used to stiffen, strengthen and shape areas such as collars, cuffs, waistbands and buttonholes
What is quilting? :: Stitching a layer of wadding between two layers of fabric to add warmth, padding and decoration

## Fastenings and standard components
Give types of fastening for textile products and a use for each. :: Zip (jeans, bags), buttons and buttonholes (shirts), press studs/poppers (baby clothes), hook-and-loop/Velcro (children's shoes), hooks and eyes (waistbands, bras), toggles (duffle coats), eyelets and laces, buckles
Give standard components used in textiles. :: Zips, buttons, thread, elastic, bias binding, interfacing, piping cord, ribbon, labels, poppers, Velcro
Why might hook-and-loop tape be chosen for children's or elderly users? :: Easy to open and close with little dexterity, quick, adjustable

## Dyeing and printing
Name methods of dyeing fabric. :: Immersion dyeing (yarn or fabric dipped in dye), tie-dye, batik (hot wax resist), dip dyeing, cold-water and fibre-reactive dyes
What is batik? :: A resist method: hot wax is applied to fabric in a pattern; dye doesn't penetrate the waxed areas; wax is then removed
What is tie-dye? :: Fabric is tied, folded or bound tightly so dye can't reach some areas, creating patterns
What is a mordant? :: A chemical used to fix natural dyes to fibres so the colour doesn't wash out
What is rotary screen printing? :: Fabric passes under cylindrical mesh screens (one per colour) with ink inside – continuous, fast printing for mass production
What is flat-bed screen printing? :: A flat mesh screen with a stencil; ink is pushed through with a squeegee – one colour at a time; used for T-shirts and smaller runs
What is dye sublimation printing? :: Dye is printed onto paper, then heat turns it to gas which bonds into polyester fabric – vivid, permanent, full-colour designs (sportswear)
What is digital textile printing? :: Inkjet printing directly onto fabric from a computer file – ideal for short runs, prototypes and personalised designs
What does colourfast mean? :: The dye does not fade or run when washed, exposed to light or rubbed

## Finishes for textiles
What is brushing (raising)? :: A mechanical finish where wire brushes lift fibre ends to give a soft, warm, fluffy surface (e.g. brushed cotton, flannelette)
What is calendering of fabric? :: Passing fabric between heated rollers under pressure to give a smooth, flat, glossy surface
What is mercerising? :: Treating cotton with sodium hydroxide (caustic soda) – makes it stronger, more lustrous and better at taking dye
Give chemical finishes for fabrics and their effects. :: Stain resistant (e.g. Teflon coating), water repellent, flame retardant (e.g. Proban), crease resistant/easy-care, anti-static, antibacterial, shrink resistant, moth-proofing
Why must children's nightwear and furniture fabrics meet flammability standards? :: They are a fire risk; flame-retardant fabrics or finishes slow burning, giving time to escape
What does a care label show? :: Fibre content, washing temperature, and symbols for washing (tub), bleaching (triangle), tumble drying (square with circle), ironing (iron) and dry cleaning (circle)

## Technical and smart textiles
How does Gore-Tex keep the wearer dry but comfortable? :: A microporous PTFE membrane – its pores are too small for liquid water droplets to pass through but large enough for water vapour (sweat) to escape
What is Nomex? :: A flame-resistant aramid fibre – used in firefighters' and racing drivers' protective clothing
What are conductive fabrics used for? :: Fabrics containing metal fibres or coatings that conduct electricity – touchscreen gloves, heated clothing, wearable electronics
What are microfibres? :: Extremely fine synthetic fibres (finer than silk) – soft, light, absorb oil/dirt well; used in cleaning cloths and sportswear
What are geotextiles? :: Strong permeable fabrics used in civil engineering for drainage, soil stabilisation and erosion control
Give examples of medical textiles. :: Antibacterial wound dressings, bandages, surgical gowns, absorbable stitches, compression stockings

## Production and quality control
What does 'cut, make and trim' (CMT) mean? :: A manufacturer is supplied with fabric and patterns and cuts out, sews and adds the trims (buttons, labels) to garments – common in outsourcing
Give quality control checks in textile manufacture. :: Check fabric for faults and colourfastness, accurate cutting to the lay plan, consistent seam allowances and stitch length, pattern matching, fit against size specifications, metal detection for broken needles
Give examples of CAM in textiles. :: Computerised sewing and embroidery machines, CNC knitting machines (e.g. seamless knitwear), laser and knife cutters, digital printers

# 15 In depth – Electronic and mechanical systems

## Sources and footprint of electronic materials
Name materials used to make electronic products and their source. :: Copper (tracks, wires – mined ore), tin/silver solder, gold contacts, silicon chips (from sand), glass-fibre/epoxy PCB boards, polymer casings and insulation (crude oil), rare earth metals (magnets, screens)
What is e-waste? :: Discarded electrical and electronic products – a fast-growing waste stream containing toxic and valuable materials
What does the RoHS directive do? :: Restriction of Hazardous Substances – limits lead, mercury, cadmium and some other substances in electronic products (hence lead-free solder)
Why should old electronic products be recycled rather than put in landfill? :: Toxic metals (lead, mercury, cadmium) can leach into soil and water; valuable metals (gold, copper, rare earths) can be recovered and reused

## Electrical principles
C: Ohm's law: voltage (V) = {{c1::current (I)}} × {{c2::resistance (R)}}.
State the units of voltage, current and resistance. :: Voltage – volts (V) ;; Current – amps (A) or milliamps (mA) ;; Resistance – ohms (Ω)
Calculate the current through a 450 Ω resistor connected to 9 V. :: I = V ÷ R = 9 ÷ 450 = **0.02 A (20 mA)**
Calculate the voltage across a 12 Ω component carrying 0.5 A. :: V = I × R = 0.5 × 12 = **6 V**
C: Electrical power (W) = {{c1::voltage}} × {{c2::current}}.
Calculate the power of a 12 V motor drawing 2 A. :: P = V × I = 12 × 2 = **24 W**
Calculate the total resistance of 220 Ω and 330 Ω in series. :: R = R1 + R2 = 220 + 330 = **550 Ω**
Calculate the total resistance of 200 Ω and 300 Ω in parallel. :: R = (R1 × R2) ÷ (R1 + R2) = 60 000 ÷ 500 = **120 Ω** (or 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2)
How do cells in series affect the voltage? :: The voltages add up, e.g. four 1.5 V AA cells in series give 6 V
How long will a 2000 mAh battery last powering a 100 mA circuit? :: 2000 ÷ 100 = **20 hours** (approximately)
C: A {{c1::potential divider}} uses two resistors (or a sensor and a resistor) in series to give an output voltage that changes as the sensor's resistance changes.
Calculate Vout of a potential divider with Vin = 9 V, R1 = 10 kΩ (top) and R2 = 20 kΩ (bottom). :: Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2) = 9 × 20 ÷ 30 = **6 V**
Why is a variable resistor often used with an LDR or thermistor in a sensing circuit? :: To adjust the sensitivity – the light level or temperature at which the circuit switches

## Resistors and the colour code
State the resistor colour code values. :: Black 0, Brown 1, Red 2, Orange 3, Yellow 4, Green 5, Blue 6, Violet 7, Grey 8, White 9
How do you read a four-band resistor? :: Band 1 = first digit, band 2 = second digit, band 3 = multiplier (number of zeros), band 4 = tolerance (gold ±5%, silver ±10%)
What is the value of a resistor banded brown, black, red, gold? :: 1, 0, ×100 = **1000 Ω (1 kΩ)** ±5%
What is the value of a resistor banded red, red, brown, gold? :: 2, 2, ×10 = **220 Ω** ±5%
What is the value of a resistor banded yellow, violet, orange, gold? :: 4, 7, ×1000 = **47 kΩ** ±5%
What colours would a 330 Ω resistor have? :: Orange, orange, brown (then tolerance band)

## Components in detail
Why does an LED need a series resistor? :: To limit the current so the LED isn't damaged (typically about 20 mA)
Calculate the series resistor for a red LED (2 V, 20 mA) on a 9 V supply. :: R = (9 − 2) ÷ 0.02 = **350 Ω** – use the next standard value up, **390 Ω**
How do you identify the cathode of an LED? :: The shorter leg and the flat edge on the casing – it connects towards 0 V (negative)
Give advantages of LEDs over filament bulbs. :: Use much less energy, last much longer, run cool, small, robust, available in many colours
What does a diode do? :: Allows current to flow in one direction only – protects circuits from reverse polarity and from back-EMF across motors and relays
What does a transistor do in a control circuit? :: Acts as an electronic switch (and amplifier) – a small base current switches on a larger collector–emitter current to drive an output
At about what base voltage does a typical transistor switch on? :: About 0.7 V
What is a Darlington pair? :: Two transistors connected together to give a very high gain, so a tiny input current can switch a large output current
What does a relay do? :: An electromagnetically operated switch that lets a low-power circuit switch a separate high-power or mains circuit safely
What does a capacitor do? :: Stores electrical charge; used in timing circuits (with a resistor) and to smooth power supplies – measured in farads (µF)
Why must electrolytic capacitors be connected the right way round? :: They are polarised – reverse connection can damage them or make them burst
What is a 555 timer IC used for? :: Producing timed pulses: **monostable** (one pulse of set length when triggered) or **astable** (continuous on/off pulses, e.g. flashing LED)
C: The time period of a 555 monostable is T = {{c1::1.1 × R × C}}.
Calculate the time period of a 555 monostable with R = 100 kΩ and C = 100 µF. :: T = 1.1 × 100 000 × 0.0001 = **11 seconds**
What is the difference between a buzzer and a speaker? :: A buzzer makes one fixed tone when powered; a speaker can produce varied sounds/music from a changing signal
Compare DC motor, stepper motor and servo motor. :: **DC motor** – continuous rotation, speed set by voltage, reversed by swapping polarity ;; **Stepper** – rotates in precise steps (3D printers) ;; **Servo** – turns to and holds a set angle (robot arms, RC steering)
How can a microcontroller control a motor or other high-current output? :: Through a transistor, MOSFET, relay or motor-driver IC, because its output pins can only supply a small current

## Signals, logic and programming
C: A {{c1::digital}} signal has only two states (on/off, 1/0); an {{c2::analogue}} signal varies continuously.
Give an example of an analogue and a digital input sensor. :: **Analogue** – LDR, thermistor, potentiometer (varying values) ;; **Digital** – push switch, reed switch, tilt switch (on/off)
State the output of AND, OR and NOT logic gates. :: **AND** – output 1 only when both inputs are 1 ;; **OR** – output 1 when either or both inputs are 1 ;; **NOT** – output is the opposite of the input
What are NAND and NOR gates? :: NAND = AND followed by NOT (output 0 only when both inputs are 1) ;; NOR = OR followed by NOT (output 1 only when both inputs are 0)
Which logic gate is used for a machine that runs only when the guard is closed AND the start button is pressed? :: AND gate
What is a truth table? :: A table showing the output of a logic system for every possible combination of inputs
Give the main features of a microcontroller program flowchart. :: Start, input checks (decision diamonds, e.g. 'Is switch pressed?'), outputs on/off, wait/delay boxes, loops back to repeat
Give advantages of using a microcontroller instead of discrete components. :: Fewer components, smaller cheaper PCB, easily reprogrammed to change function, can handle complex tasks and timing
What is an ADC on a microcontroller? :: Analogue-to-digital converter – turns an analogue sensor voltage into a number the program can use

## Making circuits
What is a breadboard used for? :: Building and testing prototype circuits without soldering – components push into connected holes and can be reused
What is stripboard (Veroboard)? :: A board with parallel copper strips; components are soldered on and tracks are cut where needed – for small one-off circuits
Describe how a PCB is made by photo-etching. :: Design the PCB layout in CAD → print onto a transparent mask → expose a photoresist board to UV → develop → etch away unwanted copper in ferric chloride (or similar) → clean → drill holes → solder components
How are PCBs made by CNC milling? :: A CNC machine routes away the copper around the tracks and drills the holes directly from the CAD file – no chemicals
What is surface mount technology (SMT)? :: Tiny components placed directly onto pads on the PCB surface by pick-and-place robots, then soldered in a reflow oven – used in mass production
What is through-hole technology? :: Component legs pass through drilled holes and are soldered on the underside – stronger, easier by hand; used for larger components and prototypes
What is wave soldering? :: A PCB with through-hole components passes over a wave of molten solder that solders all joints at once – industrial process
Describe how to make a good soldered joint. :: Clean, tinned iron tip; heat both the pad and the component leg together for a second or two; feed solder onto the joint (not the iron); remove solder then iron; joint should be shiny and cone (volcano) shaped
What is a dry joint? :: A dull, cracked or lumpy solder joint that doesn't connect properly – caused by not heating the joint enough or moving it while cooling
Give safety precautions when soldering. :: Use fume extraction, keep the iron in its stand, wear eye protection, wash hands (solder/flux), never touch the tip, work on a heat-proof mat
What is a heat sink used for? :: Absorbs and dissipates heat – clipped onto a component leg while soldering, or attached to power components to stop them overheating
How is a circuit tested and faults found? :: Visual inspection of joints and component orientation; multimeter to measure voltage, current, resistance and continuity; test each subsystem in turn
Why are electronic products usually housed in an injection-moulded casing? :: Protects the circuit from damage, moisture and dust; insulates users from electricity; allows ergonomic, attractive shapes with bosses, battery compartments and snap fits

## Mechanical systems – calculations
Calculate the gear ratio and output speed: driver 20 teeth, driven 60 teeth, input 300 rpm. :: Gear ratio = driven ÷ driver = 60 ÷ 20 = **3:1** ;; Output speed = 300 ÷ 3 = **100 rpm**
How is the ratio of a compound gear train calculated? :: Multiply the ratios of each stage together
Calculate the output speed: compound train with stages 10T→30T and 15T→45T, input 900 rpm. :: Ratios 3:1 × 3:1 = **9:1** ;; Output = 900 ÷ 9 = **100 rpm**
Calculate the velocity ratio of pulleys: driver 50 mm diameter, driven 150 mm, input 1200 rpm. :: VR = driven ÷ driver = 150 ÷ 50 = **3:1** ;; Output = 1200 ÷ 3 = **400 rpm**
What is the ratio of a worm and wheel with a 40-tooth wheel and a single-start worm? :: **40:1** – one turn of the wheel for 40 turns of the worm (e.g. 2000 rpm motor → 50 rpm)
C: The principle of moments: effort × distance from fulcrum = {{c1::load × distance from fulcrum}}.
Calculate the effort needed to lift a 200 N load 0.2 m from the fulcrum using a 0.8 m effort arm. :: Effort × 0.8 = 200 × 0.2 ;; Effort = 40 ÷ 0.8 = **50 N** ;; MA = 200 ÷ 50 = **4**
C: Velocity ratio = distance moved by {{c1::effort}} ÷ distance moved by {{c2::load}}.
C: Efficiency (%) = ({{c1::mechanical advantage}} ÷ {{c2::velocity ratio}}) × 100.
Calculate the efficiency of a machine with MA = 3 and VR = 4. :: (3 ÷ 4) × 100 = **75%**
C: Torque (turning moment) = {{c1::force}} × {{c2::perpendicular distance from the pivot}}.
Calculate the torque when 20 N is applied 0.15 m from a pivot. :: 20 × 0.15 = **3 Nm**
How far does a rack move for each turn of a 12-tooth pinion with a 5 mm tooth pitch? :: 12 × 5 = **60 mm**
What happens to torque when gearing reduces speed? :: Torque increases in the same proportion (ignoring friction) – slower output, greater turning force

## Mechanical components
What are bearings and why are they used? :: Components (ball, roller bearings or plain bushes) that support rotating shafts and reduce friction and wear
Why are mechanisms lubricated? :: Oil or grease reduces friction, wear, heat and noise and helps prevent corrosion
Give an advantage and a disadvantage of friction in mechanical systems. :: **Advantage** – needed for grip in brakes, belt drives, tyres ;; **Disadvantage** – wastes energy as heat, causes wear and noise
Why are nylon gears often used in small products? :: Lightweight, quiet, self-lubricating, cheap to injection mould and corrosion resistant (but weaker than steel)
What is a spring used for in a mechanism? :: Storing energy (elastic potential), returning parts to position, absorbing shocks, maintaining pressure (e.g. pen, cam follower, suspension)
What is a toggle clamp? :: A linkage that locks in position at its dead centre to hold work firmly – used on jigs and fixtures
What is a cam follower spring used for? :: Keeps the follower in contact with the cam as it rotates

# 16 Maths skills for D&T

## Units, area and volume
How many mm² are in 1 m²? :: 1 000 000 mm² (1000 × 1000)
Convert 0.5 m² into cm². :: 1 m² = 10 000 cm², so 0.5 m² = **5000 cm²**
Calculate the area of a circle 100 mm in diameter. :: r = 50 mm; A = πr² = 3.142 × 2500 ≈ **7854 mm²**
Calculate the circumference of a circle 100 mm in diameter. :: C = πd = 3.142 × 100 ≈ **314 mm**
Calculate the volume of a cylinder of radius 20 mm and height 100 mm. :: V = πr²h = 3.142 × 400 × 100 ≈ **125 664 mm³**
Calculate the volume of a block 200 mm × 50 mm × 20 mm. :: 200 × 50 × 20 = **200 000 mm³** (= 200 cm³)
C: Mass = {{c1::density}} × {{c2::volume}}.
Calculate the mass of an aluminium block 10 × 5 × 2 cm (density 2.7 g/cm³). :: Volume = 100 cm³ ;; Mass = 2.7 × 100 = **270 g**
Calculate the mass of an A4 sheet of 80 gsm paper. :: Area = 0.210 × 0.297 = 0.06237 m² ;; Mass = 80 × 0.06237 ≈ **5 g**
How do you find the surface area of a box (cuboid)? :: Add the areas of all six faces: 2(lw + lh + wh)

## Materials and cost calculations
How many 50 × 80 mm pieces can be cut from a 600 × 400 mm sheet? :: 600 ÷ 50 = 12 and 400 ÷ 80 = 5 ;; 12 × 5 = **60 pieces** (ignoring cutting waste)
What is tessellation (nesting) and why is it used? :: Arranging shapes so they fit closely together on a sheet with minimal gaps – reduces waste and cost
A part uses 0.18 m² of a 0.24 m² sheet. What percentage is wasted? :: Waste = 0.06 m² ;; 0.06 ÷ 0.24 × 100 = **25%**
Material costs £8 per m². What does 0.35 m² cost? :: 8 × 0.35 = **£2.80**
A component costing £40 rises in price by 15%. What is the new price? :: 40 × 1.15 = **£46**
A product costs £12 to make and sells for £18. Calculate the profit and the profit as a percentage of the selling price. :: Profit = **£6** ;; 6 ÷ 18 × 100 = **33.3%** (as a percentage of cost it is 6 ÷ 12 = 50%)
Add 20% VAT to £50. :: 50 × 1.2 = **£60**
C: Break-even point (units) = fixed costs ÷ ({{c1::selling price per unit}} − {{c2::variable cost per unit}}).
Fixed costs are £2000, each unit costs £15 to make and sells for £25. How many must be sold to break even? :: 2000 ÷ (25 − 15) = **200 units**
A resin and hardener are mixed in the ratio 2:1. How much of each is needed for 150 ml? :: 150 ÷ 3 = 50 ;; Resin = **100 ml**, hardener = **50 ml**
How do you calculate the cost of one product made in a batch? :: Total cost of the batch (materials + labour + overheads) ÷ number of products made

## Geometry, scale and tolerance
A drawing is at a scale of 1:5. A line measures 40 mm. What is the real length? :: 40 × 5 = **200 mm**
A part 15 mm long is drawn at 2:1. How long is it on the drawing? :: 15 × 2 = **30 mm**
A dimension is 25 ± 0.2 mm. What are the upper and lower limits? :: Upper **25.2 mm**, lower **24.8 mm**
Use Pythagoras to check whether a 300 × 400 mm frame is square. :: Diagonal should be √(300² + 400²) = √250 000 = **500 mm**
What is the sum of interior angles of a polygon with n sides? :: (n − 2) × 180° ;; e.g. hexagon = 720°, each interior angle of a regular hexagon = 120°
What is the ratio of the sides of A-size paper? :: 1 : √2 (about 1 : 1.414) – so halving the sheet keeps the same proportions

## Handling data
Find the mean, median, mode and range of 3, 5, 5, 8, 9. :: Mean = 30 ÷ 5 = **6** ;; Median = **5** ;; Mode = **5** ;; Range = 9 − 3 = **6**
What does it mean if a measurement is at the 50th percentile? :: Half the population has a smaller measurement and half larger – it is the median
Which charts suit which data in D&T? :: Bar chart – comparing categories (survey answers) ;; Pie chart – proportions of a whole ;; Line graph – change over time (sales, temperature) ;; Scatter graph – relationship between two variables
Give tips for drawing an accurate graph or chart. :: Title, labelled axes with units, sensible even scale, accurate plotting, key if needed

# 17 Exam technique

## The exam
How is OCR GCSE D&T (J310) assessed? :: **Principles of Design and Technology** written paper – 2 hours, 100 marks, 50% ;; **Iterative Design Challenge** (NEA) – 100 marks, 50%
What minimum proportion of exam marks assess maths and science? :: At least **15%** maths and at least **10%** science applied to design and technology
What types of question appear on the written paper? :: Short recall questions, calculations, sketching and annotation, analysis of products/images, and extended-response 'discuss/evaluate' questions marked in levels

## Command words
What does 'State', 'Give' or 'Identify' require? :: A short, precise answer – no explanation needed
What does 'Describe' require? :: Set out the features or steps of something in detail (what happens), in a logical order
What does 'Explain' require? :: Give reasons – say what **and why**, linking each point with 'because', 'so' or 'which means'
What does 'Evaluate' require? :: Weigh up strengths **and** weaknesses (or for and against) and reach a supported judgement/conclusion
What does 'Discuss' require? :: Present several sides of an issue in detail with reasons and examples, ideally reaching a conclusion
What does 'Compare' require? :: Identify similarities and differences between two things, referring to **both** in each point
What does 'Justify' require? :: Give reasons and evidence to support a choice or decision
What does 'Analyse' require? :: Break something down into its parts and examine how they relate (e.g. how features meet user needs)
What does 'Suggest' require? :: Apply your knowledge to an unfamiliar situation and propose a reasonable answer
What does 'Sketch' require? :: A clear freehand drawing (2D or 3D), usually with annotation – it doesn't need to be artistic but must be clear and labelled

## Writing good answers
How do you write a strong 'explain' point? :: Point + reason + consequence linked to the context, e.g. 'Polypropylene is used **because** it can form a living hinge, **so** the lid won't snap off after repeated use'
Why should you name specific materials and processes in answers? :: Vague answers like 'plastic', 'metal' or 'moulding' rarely score – examiners want e.g. 'HIPS', 'aluminium', 'injection moulding'
Why should you avoid answers like 'cheap', 'easy' or 'strong' on their own? :: They are too vague – qualify them (e.g. 'cheap to mass produce because the mould cost is spread over thousands of units')
How do you use the marks to plan an answer? :: Roughly one valid point (or developed point) per mark – a 4-mark explain question needs about two developed points or four linked points
How do you answer a long 'evaluate' or 'discuss' question (6+ marks)? :: Plan briefly; cover several points in depth; give both advantages and disadvantages; use specific examples, materials and processes; link to the user/context; finish with a justified conclusion
How should you set out a calculation answer? :: Write the formula, substitute values, show each step, give the answer with correct **units** and sensible rounding – method marks are given even if the final answer is wrong
How do you gain marks on a sketching question? :: Clear sketch with enough detail; annotate materials, dimensions, joining methods and how it works; show modifications clearly; use arrows and labels
How do you answer a question that gives a product or context? :: Refer directly to that product, its user and its environment in every point rather than writing general facts
Common mistake: confusing thermoforming and thermosetting polymers. How do you avoid it? :: Thermo**forming** can be re-formed (reheated and reshaped); thermo**setting** sets permanently
Common mistake: writing 'recyclable' for all plastics. Why is this wrong? :: Only thermoforming polymers can normally be recycled; thermosets cannot be remelted, and mixed or laminated materials are hard to recycle
What should you check before moving on from a question? :: Have I answered the command word, referred to the context, used specific terms, included units, and written enough points for the marks?

## The NEA (Iterative Design Challenge)
What is the OCR NEA? :: An iterative design challenge: students choose one of the contextual challenges set by OCR, then explore, create and evaluate a solution through repeated cycles of designing, prototyping and testing
What should the NEA evidence show? :: Investigation of the context and users, a design brief and specification, development of ideas through iteration and modelling, a final prototype, and ongoing testing and evaluation against the specification with user feedback

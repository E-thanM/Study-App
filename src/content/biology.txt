# 1 Variety of living organisms
What are the eight characteristics shared by living organisms? :: Nutrition, respiration, excretion, response to surroundings, movement, control of internal conditions, reproduction, growth and development
C: Plants are multicellular, contain {{c1::chloroplasts}} and carry out {{c2::photosynthesis}}.
What are plant cell walls made of? :: Cellulose
How do plants store carbohydrate? :: As starch (and sucrose)
Give two examples of plants. :: A flowering cereal (e.g. maize) and a herbaceous legume (e.g. peas or beans)
C: Animals are multicellular, do not contain {{c1::chloroplasts}} so cannot photosynthesise, and usually have {{c2::nervous coordination}} and can move.
How do animals store carbohydrate? :: As glycogen
Give two examples of animals. :: A mammal (e.g. humans) and an insect (e.g. housefly or mosquito)
Describe the structure of fungi. :: Usually a mycelium of thread-like hyphae containing many nuclei; some (e.g. yeast) are single-celled
What are fungal cell walls made of? :: Chitin
How do fungi feed? :: Saprotrophic nutrition – they secrete extracellular digestive enzymes onto food and absorb the soluble products
How do fungi store carbohydrate? :: As glycogen
Give two examples of fungi. :: Mucor (multicellular, hyphae) and yeast (single-celled)
What are protoctists? :: Microscopic single-celled organisms; some have chloroplasts (like plants), others feed like animals
Give two examples of protoctists. :: Amoeba (animal-like) and Chlorella (plant-like)
Name a protoctist that is a pathogen and the disease it causes. :: Plasmodium – causes malaria
Describe the structure of bacteria. :: Single-celled; cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, plasmids; no nucleus – a circular chromosome of DNA
How do bacteria feed? :: Some photosynthesise; most feed off other organisms (living or dead)
Give two examples of bacteria. :: Lactobacillus bulgaricus (rod-shaped, makes yoghurt) and Pneumococcus (spherical, causes pneumonia)
Describe the structure of a virus. :: Not living cells; a protein coat surrounding DNA or RNA; much smaller than bacteria
How do viruses reproduce? :: Only inside living cells – they are parasites that can infect every type of organism
Give two examples of viruses. :: Tobacco mosaic virus (discolours leaves) and influenza virus; also HIV (causes AIDS)
What is a pathogen? :: A microorganism that causes disease
Name the four types of pathogen. :: Fungi, bacteria, protoctists and viruses
## Extra detail
Which two kingdoms store carbohydrate as glycogen? :: Animals and fungi
Do fungi photosynthesise? :: No – they have no chloroplasts
What are hyphae? :: Thread-like structures that make up the body (mycelium) of a fungus; contain many nuclei
Name the bacterium used to make yoghurt and its shape. :: Lactobacillus bulgaricus – rod-shaped
Name the bacterium that causes pneumonia and its shape. :: Pneumococcus – spherical
Which kingdom has members with no nucleus? :: Bacteria (prokaryotes) – DNA is a circular chromosome plus plasmids
What is a plasmid? :: A small circular ring of DNA found in bacteria (separate from the main chromosome)
What does Chlorella have that Amoeba does not? :: Chloroplasts – Chlorella photosynthesises (plant-like); Amoeba feeds on other organisms (animal-like)
What does tobacco mosaic virus do to plants? :: Causes discolouration (mosaic pattern) of leaves by preventing chloroplast formation, reducing photosynthesis
What disease does HIV cause? :: AIDS
Why are viruses not considered living? :: They are not cells and can only reproduce inside living host cells (no metabolism of their own)

# 2a–b Cells and organisation
List the levels of organisation in an organism, smallest first. :: Organelles → cells → tissues → organs → organ systems
What is a tissue? :: A group of similar cells working together to perform a particular function
What is an organ? :: A group of different tissues working together to perform a particular function
What is the function of the nucleus? :: Contains genetic material (DNA) that controls the activities of the cell
What is the function of the cytoplasm? :: Where most chemical reactions take place; contains enzymes
What is the function of the cell membrane? :: Controls what enters and leaves the cell
What is the function of the cell wall? :: Made of cellulose; strengthens and supports the cell
What is the function of chloroplasts? :: Absorb light energy for photosynthesis (contain chlorophyll)
What is the function of mitochondria? :: Site of aerobic respiration – releases energy
What is the function of ribosomes? :: Site of protein synthesis
What is the function of the vacuole? :: Contains cell sap; keeps the cell turgid (rigid)
Which structures are found in plant cells but not animal cells? :: Cell wall, chloroplasts and a large permanent vacuole
What is cell differentiation? :: The process by which cells become specialised for a particular function
What are stem cells? :: Undifferentiated cells that can divide and differentiate into many different types of cell
Where are stem cells found in animals and plants? :: Animals: embryos (embryonic stem cells) and some adult tissues e.g. bone marrow ;; Plants: meristems (tips of roots and shoots)
Give two potential uses of stem cells in medicine. :: Replacing damaged cells/tissues (e.g. treating diabetes, paralysis, Parkinson's) and growing organs for transplant
Give two issues with using embryonic stem cells. :: Ethical objections (embryos destroyed); risk of rejection; risk of cells becoming cancerous; risk of viral infection
## Extra detail
What is an organ system? :: A group of organs working together to perform a particular function
Give an example of an organ system and its organs. :: Digestive system – e.g. stomach, small intestine, liver, pancreas
Give an example of an organ in a plant. :: Leaf (also root, stem, flower)
Give an example of a tissue in a plant and in an animal. :: Plant: xylem, phloem or palisade mesophyll ;; Animal: muscle or epithelium
What is an organelle? :: A specialised structure inside a cell with a particular function (e.g. nucleus, mitochondrion)
Which structures are found in both plant and animal cells? :: Nucleus, cytoplasm, cell membrane, mitochondria and ribosomes
What is the cell membrane described as? :: Partially (selectively) permeable – lets some substances through but not others
Why do root hair cells have no chloroplasts? :: They are underground and receive no light, so cannot photosynthesise
Why do muscle cells contain many mitochondria? :: To carry out lots of aerobic respiration, releasing energy for contraction
Why does a large permanent vacuole help support a plant? :: Cell sap pushes the cytoplasm against the cell wall, making the cell turgid and supporting the plant
How do you calculate magnification? :: Magnification = image size ÷ actual size (use the same units)
How do you calculate the actual size of a cell from a micrograph? :: Actual size = image size ÷ magnification
How many micrometres (µm) are in 1 mm? :: 1000
Why do multicellular organisms need differentiated cells? :: Different cells are specialised to carry out different functions efficiently (division of labour)
What are embryonic stem cells able to do? :: Differentiate into any type of cell in the body
How do adult stem cells differ from embryonic stem cells? :: Adult stem cells (e.g. bone marrow) can only differentiate into a limited range of cell types
Give one reason why stem cells taken from a patient's own body reduce problems. :: They are genetically identical to the patient, so they are not rejected by the immune system

# 2c Biological molecules
Which elements are in carbohydrates? :: Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen
Which elements are in proteins? :: Carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen (sometimes sulfur)
Which elements are in lipids? :: Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen
What are the building blocks of starch and glycogen? :: Simple sugars – glucose
What are the building blocks of proteins? :: Amino acids
What are the building blocks of lipids? :: Fatty acids and glycerol
How do you test for glucose? :: Add Benedict's solution and heat in a water bath – blue turns green/yellow/orange/brick-red if a reducing sugar is present
How do you test for starch? :: Add iodine solution – orange-brown turns blue-black
How do you test for protein? :: Add biuret solution – blue turns purple/lilac
How do you test for lipids (fat)? :: Ethanol emulsion test – shake with ethanol, add water; a cloudy white emulsion forms
What is an enzyme? :: A biological catalyst – a protein that speeds up reactions without being used up
Explain why enzymes are specific. :: The active site has a specific shape that only fits a complementary substrate (lock and key)
Explain the effect of increasing temperature on enzyme activity. :: Rate rises as molecules gain kinetic energy and collide more; above the optimum the active site changes shape (denatures) so the substrate no longer fits and rate falls
Explain how pH affects enzymes. :: Each enzyme has an optimum pH; pH far from the optimum changes the shape of the active site (denaturing it), so the substrate can't bind
Describe an investigation into the effect of temperature on enzyme activity. :: E.g. amylase + starch in water baths at different temperatures; take samples every 30 s onto spotting tile with iodine; time until no blue-black; keep pH, concentrations and volumes constant
## Molecules – extra detail
C: A lipid (fat or oil) molecule is made of {{c1::glycerol}} joined to three {{c2::fatty acids}}.
Which element is found in proteins but not in carbohydrates or lipids? :: Nitrogen
## Food tests practical
What is the colour of Benedict's solution before testing? :: Blue
What is the positive result for the Benedict's test? :: Blue → green → yellow → orange → brick-red precipitate (more sugar = further along)
Why must the Benedict's test be heated? :: The reaction only happens when heated – use a water bath at about 80 °C (or boiling) for a few minutes
How do you prepare a solid food sample for food tests? :: Grind it with a pestle and mortar, add distilled water, mix and filter (or let it settle)
What is the negative result for the iodine test? :: Stays orange-brown
What is the negative result for the biuret test? :: Stays blue
What is the negative result for the ethanol emulsion test? :: Stays clear (no cloudy white layer)
What safety precautions apply to food tests? :: Wear eye protection; ethanol is flammable – keep away from flames; use a water bath not a direct flame; biuret contains sodium hydroxide (irritant/corrosive)
Why is a water bath used rather than a Bunsen burner to heat Benedict's? :: It is safer (heats evenly, no boiling over) and ethanol may be nearby; keeps a constant temperature
Name a control for the food tests. :: Test distilled water with each reagent – result should be negative
Is the Benedict's test quantitative or qualitative? :: Semi-quantitative – the colour gives a rough idea of how much reducing sugar is present
## Enzymes – extra detail
What is a catalyst? :: A substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without being changed or used up
Why are enzymes needed in cells? :: To speed up metabolic reactions so they happen fast enough at body temperature
What is the active site? :: The region of an enzyme where the substrate binds
What is the substrate? :: The molecule that an enzyme acts on
What is an enzyme–substrate complex? :: Formed when the substrate binds to the complementary active site of the enzyme
What does denatured mean? :: The shape of the enzyme's active site has permanently changed so the substrate no longer fits – the enzyme stops working
What is the optimum temperature of most human enzymes? :: About 37 °C
What is the optimum pH of pepsin? :: About pH 2 (acidic, in the stomach)
What is the optimum pH of most enzymes in the small intestine? :: Slightly alkaline – about pH 7–8 (e.g. pancreatic amylase, trypsin, lipase)
Does a low temperature denature enzymes? :: No – low temperatures slow enzymes down (fewer collisions) but the active site is not damaged; activity returns when warmed
Sketch the graph of enzyme activity against temperature. :: Rises gradually to a peak (optimum) then falls steeply to zero as the enzyme denatures
## Enzyme practicals
In the amylase–starch temperature practical, what is the independent variable? :: Temperature
In the amylase–starch practical, what is the dependent variable? :: Time taken for the starch to disappear (iodine no longer turns blue-black)
In the amylase–starch practical, name the control variables. :: Volume and concentration of amylase and starch; pH (use a buffer); time interval between samples
How do you calculate the rate of an enzyme reaction from time taken? :: Rate = 1 ÷ time (e.g. s⁻¹)
Why are the amylase and starch put in the water bath separately before mixing? :: So both reach the required temperature before the reaction starts
Why is a buffer solution used in enzyme investigations? :: To keep the pH constant (or set a particular pH)
How is the end point detected in the amylase practical? :: Drops of mixture are added to iodine on a spotting tile every 30 s; the end point is when the iodine stays orange-brown (no starch left)
Describe how to investigate the effect of pH on enzyme activity. :: Mix amylase with buffers of different pH; add starch; keep at constant temperature in a water bath; sample every 30 s into iodine on a spotting tile; time until starch gone; repeat; calculate rate = 1/time
In the pH enzyme practical, what is the independent variable? :: pH (using different buffer solutions)
In the pH enzyme practical, what must be kept constant? :: Temperature (water bath), volume and concentration of enzyme and starch
How can you improve the reliability of an enzyme investigation? :: Repeat each test at least three times, discard anomalies and calculate a mean
What is a safety precaution in the enzyme practicals? :: Wear eye protection – iodine is an irritant/stains; take care with hot water baths
Describe the expected results of the pH enzyme practical. :: Shortest time (fastest rate) at the optimum pH; slower either side; no reaction at extreme pH (denatured)

# 2d Movement of substances
Define diffusion. :: The net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, down a concentration gradient (passive)
Define osmosis. :: The net movement of water molecules from a dilute solution to a more concentrated solution across a partially permeable membrane
Define active transport. :: Movement of substances against a concentration gradient (low to high) using energy from respiration and carrier proteins
Give an example of active transport in plants and in humans. :: Plants: root hair cells absorbing mineral ions from soil ;; Humans: absorbing glucose in the small intestine (and kidney tubules)
List the factors that affect the rate of movement of substances into and out of cells. :: Surface area to volume ratio, distance (diffusion path), temperature and concentration gradient
Why do larger organisms need exchange surfaces and transport systems? :: They have a smaller surface area to volume ratio, so diffusion across the body surface is too slow over long distances
Describe the osmosis practical with potato. :: Cut equal potato cylinders, measure mass, place in different sucrose concentrations for a set time, blot dry, reweigh; calculate % change in mass; where the line crosses 0% is the concentration of the cell sap
What happens to an animal cell in pure water? :: Water enters by osmosis; it swells and may burst (lysis)
What happens to a plant cell in pure water? :: Water enters; the cell becomes turgid but the cell wall stops it bursting
What happens to a plant cell in a concentrated solution? :: Water leaves; it becomes flaccid and then plasmolysed (membrane pulls away from wall)
## Extra detail
Is osmosis active or passive? :: Passive – no energy from respiration needed
Why do root hair cells contain many mitochondria? :: To release energy by respiration for active transport of mineral ions from the soil
What is a partially permeable membrane? :: A membrane that allows small molecules (e.g. water) through but not larger ones (e.g. sucrose)
How does increasing temperature affect the rate of diffusion? :: Increases it – particles have more kinetic energy and move faster
How does a steeper concentration gradient affect diffusion? :: Increases the rate of diffusion
How does a greater distance affect diffusion? :: Slower diffusion – particles take longer to travel further, so a short diffusion distance is better
How does surface area to volume ratio affect diffusion? :: A larger SA:V ratio means faster diffusion into/out of the whole cell or organism
How do you calculate the surface area to volume ratio of a cube? :: Surface area = 6 × side² ;; Volume = side³ ;; Ratio = SA ÷ volume (written as x : 1)
Calculate the SA:V ratio of a 2 cm cube. :: SA = 6 × 4 = 24 cm² ;; Volume = 8 cm³ ;; Ratio = 24 : 8 = 3 : 1
Why do single-celled organisms not need transport systems? :: They have a large surface area to volume ratio and short diffusion distances, so diffusion is fast enough
## Diffusion and osmosis practicals
Describe a practical to investigate diffusion using agar cubes. :: Cut cubes of different sizes from agar containing an indicator (e.g. phenolphthalein, pink); place in acid; time how long until each turns colourless – smaller cubes (larger SA:V) change fastest
Describe a simple practical to show diffusion in a liquid. :: Drop a crystal of potassium manganate(VII) into still water and watch the purple colour spread out (faster in warm water)
Describe how Visking tubing can model osmosis. :: Fill Visking tubing with sucrose solution, attach a capillary tube, place in water; water enters by osmosis and the liquid rises up the capillary tube
What does Visking tubing model? :: A partially permeable membrane (e.g. a cell membrane or gut wall)
How do you calculate percentage change in mass? :: (final mass − initial mass) ÷ initial mass × 100
Why is percentage change in mass used rather than change in mass in the potato osmosis practical? :: The cylinders may have different starting masses, so percentage change allows a fair comparison
Why are potato cylinders blotted dry before reweighing? :: To remove surface water, which would add to the mass and give inaccurate results
In the potato osmosis practical, what is the independent variable? :: Concentration of the sucrose (or salt) solution
In the potato osmosis practical, what is the dependent variable? :: Percentage change in mass (or length)
In the potato osmosis practical, name the control variables. :: Size/surface area of potato pieces, same potato, volume of solution, time, temperature
Explain why potato cylinders gain mass in distilled water. :: Distilled water is more dilute than the cell sap, so water moves into the cells by osmosis across the partially permeable membrane
Explain why potato cylinders lose mass in concentrated sucrose solution. :: The solution is more concentrated than the cell sap, so water moves out of the cells by osmosis
What does it mean if a potato cylinder shows no change in mass? :: The solution has the same concentration as the cell contents – no net movement of water
What safety precaution is needed when cutting potato cylinders? :: Use a cork borer/scalpel carefully, cutting away from the body on a tile

# 2e Plant nutrition
Write the word equation for photosynthesis. :: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (in the presence of light and chlorophyll)
Write the balanced symbol equation for photosynthesis. :: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
C: Photosynthesis converts {{c1::light}} energy into {{c2::chemical}} energy stored in glucose.
Name three limiting factors of photosynthesis. :: Light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature
Explain what a limiting factor is. :: The factor in shortest supply that stops the rate of photosynthesis increasing further
How is a leaf adapted for photosynthesis? :: Large surface area; thin (short diffusion distance); many chloroplasts in palisade layer; stomata for gas exchange; air spaces in spongy mesophyll; veins (xylem/phloem) to transport water and sugars
Why do plants need magnesium ions? :: To make chlorophyll – deficiency causes yellowing of leaves (chlorosis)
Why do plants need nitrate ions? :: To make amino acids/proteins – deficiency causes stunted growth
How do you test a leaf for starch? :: Boil in water (kill cells), boil in ethanol in a water bath (remove chlorophyll), rinse in water (soften), add iodine – blue-black shows starch
How can you show that chlorophyll is needed for photosynthesis? :: Test a variegated leaf for starch – only the green parts turn blue-black
How can you show that light is needed for photosynthesis? :: Destarch a plant, cover part of a leaf with foil, leave in light, test for starch – covered part stays orange-brown
How can you show that CO₂ is needed for photosynthesis? :: Destarch plant, enclose a leaf in a bag with soda lime (absorbs CO₂), leave in light, test for starch – no starch
Describe an investigation into the effect of light intensity on photosynthesis. :: Pondweed in water with sodium hydrogencarbonate; lamp at different distances; count bubbles or measure gas volume per minute; keep temperature constant (heat shield/water bath)
## Leaf structure
List the layers of a leaf from top to bottom. :: Waxy cuticle → upper epidermis → palisade mesophyll → spongy mesophyll (with air spaces, veins) → lower epidermis with stomata and guard cells
What is the function of the waxy cuticle? :: Reduces water loss by evaporation; it is transparent so light passes through
Why is the upper epidermis transparent? :: Lets light pass through to the palisade cells
Why is the palisade mesophyll near the top of the leaf? :: To absorb the most light; its cells are packed with chloroplasts
What is the function of the spongy mesophyll? :: Has air spaces that allow gases (CO₂, O₂, water vapour) to diffuse through the leaf
What is the function of stomata? :: Pores (mainly on the lower surface) that let CO₂ diffuse in and O₂ and water vapour diffuse out
What controls the opening and closing of stomata? :: Guard cells
Why are leaves broad and flat? :: Large surface area to absorb more light and CO₂
Why are leaves thin? :: Short diffusion distance for gases
What is the role of veins (vascular bundles) in a leaf? :: Xylem brings water and minerals; phloem carries away sugars (sucrose)
## Photosynthesis – extra detail
Where in the cell does photosynthesis happen? :: In the chloroplasts
Where does a plant get the carbon dioxide and water for photosynthesis? :: CO₂ diffuses in from the air through stomata; water is absorbed from soil by root hairs and carried in xylem
Give five uses of the glucose made in photosynthesis. :: Respiration; converted to starch for storage; cellulose for cell walls; sucrose for transport; combined with nitrates to make amino acids/proteins; lipids for storage in seeds
Why is glucose stored as starch? :: Starch is insoluble so it doesn't affect osmosis (water movement) in cells, and it is compact
Explain the effect of increasing light intensity on the rate of photosynthesis. :: Rate increases as light intensity increases (light is limiting) until it levels off, when another factor (CO₂ or temperature) becomes limiting
Explain the effect of increasing CO₂ concentration on rate of photosynthesis. :: Rate increases until it plateaus, when light intensity or temperature becomes the limiting factor
Explain the effect of temperature on the rate of photosynthesis. :: Rate increases up to an optimum as enzymes work faster; above the optimum the enzymes denature and the rate falls
On a graph of rate against light intensity, what is limiting on the rising part? :: Light intensity
On a graph of rate against light intensity, what is limiting on the flat (plateau) part? :: Not light – another factor such as CO₂ concentration or temperature
How would you show on a graph that CO₂ is limiting? :: A higher CO₂ concentration gives a higher plateau than a lower CO₂ concentration at the same light intensity
## Photosynthesis practicals
Why must a plant be destarched before a starch test experiment? :: To remove any starch already present, so any starch found must have been made during the experiment
How is a plant destarched? :: Keep it in the dark for 24–48 hours so it uses up its starch stores
Why is a leaf boiled in water at the start of the starch test? :: To kill the cells and break down the membranes so iodine can get in (stops reactions)
Why is the leaf boiled in ethanol during the starch test? :: To remove the chlorophyll (decolourise the leaf) so the colour change is visible
Why is ethanol heated in a water bath rather than over a flame? :: Ethanol is flammable – turn off the Bunsen burner before heating it
Why is the leaf rinsed in water after boiling in ethanol? :: Ethanol makes the leaf brittle; water softens it so it can be spread flat
What does soda lime (or sodium hydroxide) do in the CO₂ experiment? :: Absorbs carbon dioxide from the air around the leaf
What is the control in the CO₂-needed experiment? :: A leaf in a bag with sodium hydrogencarbonate (or nothing) instead of soda lime – it should contain starch
What is the variegated leaf experiment's control? :: The green parts of the same leaf, which do contain starch
Why is sodium hydrogencarbonate added to the water in the pondweed experiment? :: To supply carbon dioxide, so CO₂ is not limiting
In the pondweed light-intensity practical, what is the independent variable? :: Light intensity (distance of the lamp from the pondweed)
In the pondweed light-intensity practical, what is the dependent variable? :: Rate of photosynthesis – number of bubbles or volume of oxygen collected per minute
In the pondweed light-intensity practical, what are the control variables? :: Temperature, CO₂ concentration (NaHCO₃), same piece/species of pondweed, time, room light (dark room)
Why is a beaker of water placed between the lamp and pondweed? :: Acts as a heat shield so temperature stays constant (lamp gives off heat)
Why is counting bubbles less accurate than using a gas syringe? :: Bubbles can be different sizes and are hard to count; a gas syringe/measuring cylinder measures the actual volume
Why should the pondweed be left to acclimatise before recording? :: So the rate of photosynthesis stabilises at the new light intensity
What gas is given off by the pondweed and how could you test it? :: Oxygen – relights a glowing splint
How do you calculate relative light intensity from distance? :: Light intensity = 1 ÷ distance² (e.g. at 10 cm = 1/100 = 0.01)
If the lamp distance is halved, what happens to light intensity? :: It increases four times (inverse square law)

# 2e Human nutrition
What makes up a balanced diet? :: Carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, vitamins, minerals, water and dietary fibre in the right proportions
Function and source of carbohydrates? :: Energy; bread, pasta, rice, potatoes
Function and source of protein? :: Growth and repair; meat, fish, eggs, beans
Function and source of lipids? :: Energy store and insulation; butter, oil, nuts
Function and source of vitamin A? :: Healthy skin and vision (night vision); liver, carrots
Function and source of vitamin C? :: Prevents scurvy (needed for connective tissue); citrus fruits
Function and source of vitamin D? :: Absorption of calcium for bones and teeth (prevents rickets); oily fish, sunlight on skin
Function and source of calcium? :: Strong bones and teeth, blood clotting; milk, cheese
Function and source of iron? :: Making haemoglobin (prevents anaemia); red meat, spinach
Why do we need dietary fibre? :: Gives bulk to food so it can be pushed through the gut by peristalsis – prevents constipation
Why do energy requirements vary? :: With activity level, age and pregnancy (also sex and body size)
Name the organs of the alimentary canal in order. :: Mouth → oesophagus → stomach → small intestine (duodenum, ileum) → large intestine (colon, rectum) → anus
Which organs produce digestive juices but food does not pass through? :: Salivary glands, pancreas and liver (gall bladder stores bile)
Define ingestion, digestion, absorption, assimilation and egestion. :: Ingestion – taking food in ;; Digestion – breaking large insoluble molecules into small soluble ones ;; Absorption – into the blood ;; Assimilation – used by cells ;; Egestion – removing undigested food as faeces
What is peristalsis? :: Waves of contraction of circular and longitudinal muscles in the gut wall that push food along
What does amylase do and where is it made? :: Starch → maltose; made in salivary glands and pancreas
What does maltase do? :: Maltose → glucose (in the small intestine)
What do proteases do? Give an example. :: Proteins → amino acids; e.g. pepsin in the stomach, trypsin from the pancreas
What does lipase do and where is it made? :: Lipids → fatty acids + glycerol; made in the pancreas
Where is bile made and stored? :: Made in the liver, stored in the gall bladder
What are the two functions of bile? :: Neutralises stomach acid (alkaline) – giving the optimum pH for enzymes in the small intestine ;; Emulsifies fats into small droplets – larger surface area for lipase
Why does the stomach contain hydrochloric acid? :: Kills bacteria and provides the acidic optimum pH for pepsin
How is the small intestine (ileum) adapted for absorption? :: Very long; villi and microvilli give a large surface area; walls one cell thick; good blood supply (capillaries) maintains concentration gradient; lacteals absorb fatty acids and glycerol
What is absorbed in the large intestine (colon)? :: Water
Describe how to measure the energy in a food sample. :: Burn a known mass of food under a boiling tube containing a known volume of water; measure temperature rise ;; energy (J) = mass of water (g) × 4.2 × temperature change
## Diet – extra detail
Why is water needed in the diet? :: For chemical reactions in cells, as a solvent, for transport in blood and to replace water lost in sweat, urine and breath
Why does a pregnant woman need more energy and nutrients? :: To support the growth of the fetus (and extra iron and calcium for its blood and bones)
Why does an active person need more energy than an inactive person? :: Muscles respire more to release energy for contraction
Why do teenagers need more energy than young children or the elderly? :: They are growing rapidly and are usually more active
## Digestion – extra detail
Why does food need to be digested? :: Large insoluble molecules must be broken into small soluble molecules that can be absorbed into the blood
What happens to food in the mouth? :: Teeth chew it (mechanical digestion) increasing surface area; saliva contains amylase to start digesting starch; food is formed into a bolus
What is the role of the oesophagus? :: Carries food from the mouth to the stomach by peristalsis
What happens in the stomach? :: Muscular walls churn food; pepsin (protease) digests protein; hydrochloric acid kills bacteria and gives optimum pH
What is the role of the pancreas in digestion? :: Secretes pancreatic juice containing amylase, protease (trypsin) and lipase into the duodenum
What is the role of the duodenum? :: First part of the small intestine – receives bile and pancreatic juice; digestion continues
What is the role of the ileum? :: Last part of small intestine – digestion completed and digested food absorbed into the blood via villi
What is the role of the rectum? :: Stores faeces before egestion
What is emulsification? :: Breaking large fat droplets into many small droplets (by bile), giving a larger surface area for lipase to act on
Does bile contain enzymes? :: No – it emulsifies fats and neutralises acid but does not digest chemically
What is a villus? :: A finger-like projection of the small intestine wall that increases surface area for absorption
What is a lacteal? :: A lymph vessel in the centre of a villus that absorbs fatty acids and glycerol
Why does a villus have a good blood supply? :: Capillaries carry absorbed food away, keeping a steep concentration gradient for diffusion
Why is the wall of a villus only one cell thick? :: Short diffusion distance so absorption is fast
Why do villus cells have microvilli and many mitochondria? :: Microvilli increase surface area further; mitochondria release energy for active transport of glucose and amino acids
Where do absorbed glucose and amino acids go after absorption? :: Into the capillaries, then the hepatic portal vein to the liver
What is assimilation? :: When absorbed food molecules are used by body cells to build new molecules (e.g. amino acids → proteins)
How are circular and longitudinal muscles involved in peristalsis? :: Circular muscle contracts behind the food (squeezing it), longitudinal muscle contracts in front (widening the gut), pushing food along
## Energy in food practical
In the food-burning practical, what is the independent variable? :: Type of food
In the food-burning practical, what is the dependent variable? :: Temperature rise of the water (used to calculate energy per gram)
In the food-burning practical, name the control variables. :: Volume of water, mass of food, distance of food from the tube, starting temperature of water
Worked example: 1 g of food raises 20 g of water by 15 °C. Energy per gram? :: Energy = 20 × 4.2 × 15 = 1260 J ;; ÷ 1 g = 1260 J/g (1.26 kJ/g)
What does 4.2 represent in the food energy equation? :: The specific heat capacity of water – 4.2 J are needed to raise 1 g of water by 1 °C
Why is 1 cm³ of water treated as 1 g? :: The density of water is 1 g/cm³
Why is the energy measured lower than the true value? :: Heat lost to the air and the boiling tube; incomplete burning of the food; heat lost when moving food from the flame to the tube
How could you reduce heat loss in the food practical? :: Use a calorimeter or insulate the tube; keep the burning food close to the tube; shield from draughts
Why must the food be relit if it goes out? :: To make sure all the food burns completely so all its energy is released
Give safety precautions for the food-burning practical. :: Wear eye protection; tie hair back; check for nut allergies; clamp the boiling tube; use a heatproof mat

# 2f Respiration
What is respiration? :: A chemical process in all living cells that releases energy from glucose (to make ATP)
Write the word equation for aerobic respiration. :: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water (+ energy)
Write the balanced equation for aerobic respiration. :: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O
Write the word equation for anaerobic respiration in animals. :: glucose → lactic acid
Write the word equation for anaerobic respiration in plants and yeast. :: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide
Compare aerobic and anaerobic respiration. :: Aerobic needs oxygen and releases much more energy; anaerobic needs no oxygen, releases much less energy and makes lactic acid (animals) or ethanol + CO₂ (plants/yeast)
What is ATP? :: The molecule that stores and provides energy for cells
Why do muscles respire anaerobically during vigorous exercise? :: Not enough oxygen can be supplied, so extra energy is released without oxygen – lactic acid builds up (oxygen debt)
Describe an investigation into heat production by germinating seeds. :: Two vacuum flasks: germinating seeds and boiled (dead) seeds, both rinsed in disinfectant; thermometers sealed with cotton wool; record temperature over days – germinating seeds get warmer
How can hydrogencarbonate indicator show respiration? :: It turns from red to yellow as CO₂ concentration increases
## Extra detail
Where in the cell does aerobic respiration take place? :: Mitochondria
Where in the cell does anaerobic respiration take place? :: Cytoplasm
Is respiration the same as breathing? :: No – respiration is a chemical reaction in cells releasing energy; breathing (ventilation) moves air in and out of the lungs
Give uses of the energy released by respiration. :: Muscle contraction (movement), active transport, making large molecules (e.g. proteins), cell division, keeping warm (mammals and birds), nerve impulses
Is respiration exothermic or endothermic? :: Exothermic – it releases energy, some as heat
Why does anaerobic respiration release less energy than aerobic? :: Glucose is only partly broken down – energy remains in the lactic acid (or ethanol)
What is oxygen debt? :: The extra oxygen needed after exercise to break down the lactic acid produced by anaerobic respiration
Why do you keep breathing heavily after exercise stops? :: To repay the oxygen debt – extra oxygen is used to break down lactic acid in the liver
What effect does lactic acid have on muscles? :: Causes muscle fatigue and cramp
Do plants respire? :: Yes – all the time, in all living cells, day and night
## Respiration practicals
What does limewater show in a respiration experiment? :: Turns milky/cloudy when carbon dioxide is present
What colour is hydrogencarbonate indicator at atmospheric CO₂ levels? :: Red (orange-red)
What colour is hydrogencarbonate indicator when CO₂ is high? :: Yellow
What colour is hydrogencarbonate indicator when CO₂ is low? :: Purple
Describe an experiment to show CO₂ is produced by respiring organisms. :: Place living organisms (e.g. germinating seeds or maggots) on a gauze platform in a sealed tube above hydrogencarbonate indicator; indicator turns from red to yellow; a control with no/dead organisms stays red
What is the control in the respiring seeds CO₂ experiment? :: A tube with boiled (dead) seeds or glass beads of the same volume – shows the colour change is due to living organisms
Why are seeds rinsed in disinfectant in the heat-from-respiration experiment? :: To kill microorganisms on the seeds, which would respire and produce heat themselves
Why are vacuum flasks used in the germinating seeds experiment? :: To insulate and reduce heat loss so any temperature rise can be measured
Why are the flasks in the respiration experiment placed upside down and plugged with cotton wool? :: Heat rises so less is lost through the opening; cotton wool lets gases (O₂ in, CO₂ out) pass while insulating
Why are boiled seeds used as a control in the heat experiment? :: Boiling kills the seeds (denatures enzymes) so they cannot respire – any difference is due to respiration
What is the expected result of the heat-from-respiration experiment? :: Flask with germinating seeds gets warmer; flask with boiled seeds stays at the same temperature
Give a safety/ethical point when using small animals such as maggots or woodlice in respiration experiments. :: Handle carefully, keep them away from the indicator (gauze) and return them to their habitat; wash hands afterwards

# 2g Gas exchange
How are leaves adapted for gas exchange? :: Stomata; air spaces in spongy mesophyll; thin leaf; large surface area
Explain gas exchange in leaves in light and dark. :: Dark – only respiration: O₂ in, CO₂ out ;; Light – photosynthesis faster than respiration: net CO₂ in, O₂ out
How do guard cells open stomata? :: Guard cells take in water, become turgid and bend apart, opening the pore
Describe the effect of light intensity on gas exchange using hydrogencarbonate indicator. :: Bright light: purple (CO₂ used up) ;; Dim (compensation point): red ;; Dark: yellow (CO₂ produced)
Name the structures of the human thorax. :: Ribs, intercostal muscles, diaphragm, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli, pleural membranes
Describe what happens during inhalation. :: External intercostal muscles contract, ribs move up and out; diaphragm contracts and flattens; thorax volume increases, pressure decreases, air rushes in
Describe what happens during exhalation. :: Intercostal muscles relax, ribs move down and in; diaphragm relaxes and domes up; volume decreases, pressure increases, air is forced out
How are alveoli adapted for gas exchange? :: Huge surface area; walls one cell thick (short diffusion distance); moist; surrounded by many capillaries (good blood supply keeps a steep concentration gradient); ventilation
Give the effects of smoking on the lungs. :: Tar damages cilia and coats alveoli; carbon monoxide reduces oxygen carried by haemoglobin; causes COPD (emphysema, bronchitis), lung cancer and coronary heart disease
What does carbon monoxide in cigarette smoke do? :: Binds irreversibly to haemoglobin, reducing the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood
How does exercise affect breathing? :: Breathing rate and depth increase to supply more oxygen for respiration and remove CO₂
## Plants – extra detail
By what process do gases enter and leave a leaf? :: Diffusion (down concentration gradients through stomata and air spaces)
Explain why there is no net gas exchange at dawn and dusk. :: Rate of photosynthesis equals rate of respiration (compensation point) – CO₂ produced is used up and O₂ produced is used
Why do guard cells close stomata at night? :: No light for photosynthesis so CO₂ is not needed; closing reduces water loss
Why do stomata closing in hot, dry conditions reduce photosynthesis? :: Less CO₂ can diffuse into the leaf
Describe how to investigate the effect of light on net gas exchange in a leaf. :: Place leaves in tubes of hydrogencarbonate indicator (red); cover tubes with different layers (none, gauze, foil); include a tube with no leaf as a control; leave in light for a set time and record colours
In the hydrogencarbonate leaf experiment, why is a tube with no leaf included? :: As a control – shows the colour change is caused by the leaf, not by light or the indicator
In the hydrogencarbonate leaf experiment, why is the tube wrapped in foil yellow? :: In the dark the leaf only respires, so CO₂ concentration rises
In the hydrogencarbonate leaf experiment, why is the uncovered tube purple? :: In bright light photosynthesis is faster than respiration, so CO₂ is taken up and concentration falls
## Human thorax – extra detail
What is the function of the trachea? :: Carries air to the bronchi; held open by C-shaped rings of cartilage
Why does the trachea have rings of cartilage? :: To stop it collapsing when pressure falls during inhalation
What is the role of cilia and mucus in the airways? :: Mucus (from goblet cells) traps dust and pathogens; cilia beat to sweep the mucus up to the throat to be swallowed
What are the bronchi? :: Two tubes branching from the trachea, one to each lung
What are bronchioles? :: Small branching tubes in the lungs that end in alveoli
What is the role of the pleural membranes? :: Line the thorax and cover the lungs; pleural fluid between them reduces friction during breathing
What is the role of the intercostal muscles? :: Move the ribcage up and out (inhalation) or down and in (exhalation)
What is the role of the diaphragm? :: A sheet of muscle below the lungs; contracts and flattens to increase thorax volume during inhalation
Explain why air enters the lungs during inhalation. :: Thorax volume increases, so pressure inside the lungs falls below atmospheric pressure; air moves in down the pressure gradient
Why is a moist alveolar lining important? :: Gases dissolve in the moisture before diffusing across
Why does ventilation help gas exchange in the alveoli? :: It replaces air, keeping O₂ high and CO₂ low in the alveoli – maintains a steep concentration gradient
Which gas diffuses from the alveoli into the blood? :: Oxygen
Which gas diffuses from the blood into the alveoli? :: Carbon dioxide
How does inhaled air differ from exhaled air? :: Exhaled air has less oxygen (~16% vs 21%), more CO₂ (~4% vs 0.04%), more water vapour and is warmer
## Smoking
What does tar in cigarette smoke do? :: Damages/paralyses cilia; is carcinogenic (causes cancer); coats alveoli
What is emphysema? :: Breakdown of alveolar walls – smaller surface area for gas exchange, causing breathlessness
Why do smokers develop a 'smoker's cough'? :: Cilia are damaged so mucus builds up in the airways; coughing is the only way to remove it (bronchitis)
What does nicotine do? :: Addictive drug; increases heart rate and narrows blood vessels (raises blood pressure)
How does smoking increase the risk of coronary heart disease? :: Nicotine raises heart rate and blood pressure; carbon monoxide reduces oxygen supply; chemicals damage artery walls leading to fatty deposits and clots
What is a carcinogen? :: A substance that causes cancer (e.g. tar)
## Exercise and breathing practical
Describe how to investigate the effect of exercise on breathing rate. :: Count breaths per minute at rest; exercise (e.g. step-ups) for a set time; count breaths per minute immediately after and each minute until back to resting rate; repeat and calculate means
In the exercise and breathing practical, what is the independent variable? :: Intensity or duration of exercise
In the exercise and breathing practical, what is the dependent variable? :: Breathing rate (breaths per minute) – or depth
Give control variables in the exercise and breathing practical. :: Same person (or similar age, fitness, sex), same type and duration of exercise, same time intervals
Explain why breathing rate and depth increase during exercise. :: Muscles respire faster, needing more oxygen and producing more CO₂; faster deeper breathing increases gas exchange to supply O₂ and remove CO₂
What is recovery time? :: The time taken for breathing rate (or heart rate) to return to the resting rate after exercise – shorter in fitter people
Give a safety precaution in the exercise practical. :: Check the person is healthy (e.g. no asthma); stop if they feel unwell; exercise on a non-slip surface

# 2h Transport
Why do multicellular organisms need transport systems? :: Small surface area to volume ratio – diffusion alone is too slow to supply all cells
What does xylem transport? :: Water and mineral ions from roots to the leaves (one direction)
What does phloem transport? :: Sucrose and amino acids (translocation) – up and down the plant
How are root hair cells adapted? :: Long extension gives a large surface area for absorbing water (osmosis) and mineral ions (active transport)
What is transpiration? :: Evaporation of water from the surface of leaves (mesophyll cells) and diffusion out of the stomata
Name four factors that increase the rate of transpiration. :: Higher temperature, higher light intensity (stomata open), lower humidity, more wind/air movement
How do you measure the rate of transpiration? :: Potometer – measure the distance moved by an air bubble in a capillary tube in a given time (measures water uptake)
Name the components of blood and their functions. :: Red blood cells – carry oxygen ;; White blood cells – defend against pathogens ;; Platelets – clotting ;; Plasma – transports CO₂, digested food, urea, hormones and heat
How are red blood cells adapted? :: Biconcave shape (large surface area); no nucleus (more room for haemoglobin); contain haemoglobin which binds oxygen
How do phagocytes defend the body? :: They engulf and digest pathogens (phagocytosis)
How do lymphocytes defend the body? :: They produce antibodies that bind to specific antigens on pathogens, helping to destroy them; also memory cells
How does vaccination work? :: A dead/weakened pathogen (antigens) is injected; lymphocytes make antibodies and memory cells; on later infection memory cells produce antibodies rapidly in large numbers, so you don't get ill
Why does blood clot? :: Platelets release chemicals that make fibrinogen turn into insoluble fibrin, forming a mesh that traps red blood cells – stops blood loss and pathogens entering
Describe the route of blood through the heart. :: Vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta
Why is the left ventricle wall thicker than the right? :: It pumps blood at higher pressure around the whole body, not just to the lungs
What is the function of valves in the heart? :: Prevent backflow of blood
How does the heart rate change during exercise and why? :: Increases (via adrenaline and nervous control) to deliver more oxygen and glucose to muscles and remove more CO₂
Give risk factors for coronary heart disease. :: Diet high in saturated fat/salt, smoking, stress, lack of exercise, genetic factors (inheritance)
Compare arteries, veins and capillaries. :: Arteries – thick muscular elastic walls, high pressure, away from heart ;; Veins – thin walls, large lumen, valves, low pressure, to heart ;; Capillaries – walls one cell thick for exchange
Name the blood vessels to and from the liver. :: Hepatic artery (to), hepatic vein (from), hepatic portal vein (from gut to liver)
Name the blood vessels to and from the kidneys. :: Renal artery (to), renal vein (from)
What is the double circulatory system? :: Blood passes through the heart twice per circuit: heart→lungs→heart (pulmonary) and heart→body→heart (systemic)
## Plant transport – extra detail
Describe the structure of xylem vessels. :: Long hollow tubes of dead cells with no end walls; walls thickened with lignin (strong, waterproof)
Describe the structure of phloem. :: Living cells (sieve tubes) with perforated end walls (sieve plates) that transport dissolved sugars
What is translocation? :: The movement of sucrose and amino acids through the phloem from where they are made (leaves) to where they are needed or stored
Where are xylem and phloem found together? :: In vascular bundles (veins) in roots, stems and leaves
How does water enter root hair cells? :: By osmosis – soil water is more dilute than the cell sap
How do mineral ions enter root hair cells? :: By active transport (against the concentration gradient), using energy from respiration
What is the transpiration stream? :: The continuous movement of water from the roots, up the xylem, to the leaves, where it evaporates and diffuses out through the stomata
Explain how water moves up a plant. :: Water evaporates from mesophyll cells and diffuses out of stomata; this pulls water up the xylem in a continuous column (transpiration pull) from the roots
Give three reasons why transpiration is useful to a plant. :: Transports water and mineral ions to the leaves; cools the leaf by evaporation; supplies water for photosynthesis and keeps cells turgid
Explain why temperature increases the rate of transpiration. :: Water molecules have more kinetic energy, so evaporation and diffusion out of stomata are faster
Explain why humidity decreases the rate of transpiration. :: More water vapour in the air reduces the concentration gradient between the leaf air spaces and the air outside
Explain why wind increases the rate of transpiration. :: Moving air removes water vapour from around the leaf, keeping a steep concentration gradient
Explain why light intensity increases the rate of transpiration. :: Stomata open wider in light (for photosynthesis), letting more water vapour diffuse out
What does a potometer actually measure? :: The rate of water uptake (which is approximately equal to the rate of transpiration)
Why is a potometer's shoot cut under water? :: To stop air bubbles entering the xylem, which would block water movement
Why must a potometer be airtight? :: Leaks would let air in or water out, giving inaccurate readings – seal joints with petroleum jelly
How do you reset the air bubble in a potometer? :: Open the tap of the reservoir to let water push the bubble back to the start
How do you calculate rate of water uptake from a potometer? :: Distance moved by bubble ÷ time ;; or volume = πr² × distance, then ÷ time
How can you change each condition when investigating transpiration with a potometer? :: Temperature – room/heater ;; Humidity – plastic bag over shoot ;; Wind – fan at different distances/speeds ;; Light – lamp at different distances
In the potometer practical, name control variables. :: Same shoot (or same number/size of leaves), all other conditions (temperature, light, humidity, air flow), same time
Why is water uptake not exactly equal to transpiration? :: Some water is used in photosynthesis and to keep cells turgid
## Blood and immunity – extra detail
What is haemoglobin? :: A red protein in red blood cells that combines with oxygen to form oxyhaemoglobin in the lungs and releases it in tissues
Why are red blood cells biconcave? :: Large surface area to volume ratio for faster diffusion of oxygen; flexible to fit through capillaries
What is an antigen? :: A molecule (usually protein) on the surface of a cell or pathogen that triggers an immune response
What is an antibody? :: A protein made by lymphocytes that binds to a specific antigen, helping to destroy the pathogen
Why is an antibody specific? :: Its shape is complementary to only one particular antigen
What are memory cells? :: Lymphocytes that remain in the blood after an infection or vaccination and can rapidly produce antibodies if the same antigen is met again
Describe the secondary immune response. :: Faster, produces many more antibodies, lasts longer than the primary response – pathogen destroyed before symptoms appear
Explain why a person rarely catches the same disease twice. :: Memory cells recognise the antigen and quickly make large numbers of antibodies before the pathogen can cause illness
What is in a vaccine? :: Dead or weakened (attenuated) pathogens, or their antigens
How does gaining immunity by vaccination differ from catching the disease? :: Vaccination gives immunity without having the disease; the body still makes its own antibodies and memory cells
## Heart and circulation – extra detail
What is the function of the atria? :: Receive blood from veins and pump it into the ventricles
What is the function of the ventricles? :: Pump blood out of the heart into arteries (right to lungs, left to body)
What is the septum? :: The wall separating the left and right sides of the heart – stops oxygenated and deoxygenated blood mixing
Which side of the heart carries deoxygenated blood? :: The right side
Which blood vessel carries oxygenated blood from the lungs to the heart? :: Pulmonary vein
Which blood vessel carries deoxygenated blood from the heart to the lungs? :: Pulmonary artery
What is the aorta? :: The main artery carrying oxygenated blood from the left ventricle to the body
What is the vena cava? :: The main vein bringing deoxygenated blood from the body to the right atrium
What do the coronary arteries do? :: Supply the heart muscle itself with oxygenated blood (glucose and oxygen)
Why are the atrial walls thinner than the ventricle walls? :: Atria only pump blood a short distance into the ventricles
Name the valves at the base of the aorta and pulmonary artery. :: Semilunar valves – prevent backflow into the ventricles
What is coronary heart disease? :: Fatty deposits (cholesterol) build up in the coronary arteries, narrowing them and reducing blood flow (and oxygen) to the heart muscle – can cause a heart attack
Explain how CHD can cause a heart attack. :: Narrowed coronary arteries or a blood clot block blood flow; heart muscle gets no oxygen, cannot respire and dies
How does adrenaline affect heart rate? :: Increases heart rate (and stroke volume) to supply more oxygen and glucose to muscles
Why do arteries have thick muscular and elastic walls? :: To withstand and maintain high blood pressure; elastic fibres stretch and recoil to even out the pressure
Why do veins have valves? :: To prevent backflow of blood, because blood pressure in veins is low
Why do veins have a large lumen? :: Reduces resistance to the flow of low-pressure blood
How are capillaries adapted for exchange? :: Walls one cell thick (short diffusion distance); narrow, so close to cells; large network gives a big surface area
Why is a double circulation an advantage? :: Blood returns to the heart after the lungs so it can be pumped at high pressure to the body – faster delivery of oxygen

# 2i Excretion
What are the excretory organs in humans? :: Lungs (CO₂), kidneys (urea, water, salts) and skin (sweat)
What are the excretory products of plants? :: Oxygen (from photosynthesis) and carbon dioxide (from respiration), through the leaves
Name the parts of the urinary system. :: Kidneys, ureters, bladder, urethra
What are the two main functions of the kidney? :: Excretion (removing urea) and osmoregulation (controlling water content of blood)
Describe ultrafiltration. :: High pressure in the glomerulus forces small molecules (water, glucose, salts, urea) out of the blood into Bowman's capsule; blood cells and proteins stay in the blood
What is selective reabsorption? :: All glucose is reabsorbed back into the blood in the proximal convoluted tubule (by active transport)
Where is water reabsorbed in the nephron? :: In the collecting duct (and loop of Henle)
What is ADH and where is it released from? :: Antidiuretic hormone, released by the pituitary gland
How does ADH control the water content of the blood? :: If blood is too concentrated, more ADH is released; it makes the collecting duct more permeable so more water is reabsorbed – small volume of concentrated urine
What does urine contain? :: Water, urea and salts
## Extra detail
What is excretion? :: Removal of waste products of metabolism (e.g. CO₂, urea) from the body
Where is urea made? :: In the liver, from the breakdown of excess amino acids
What does the skin excrete? :: Sweat – water, salts and a little urea
What is the function of the ureters? :: Carry urine from the kidneys to the bladder
What is the function of the bladder? :: Stores urine
What is the function of the urethra? :: Carries urine from the bladder out of the body
Name the three main regions of a kidney. :: Cortex (outer), medulla (inner), pelvis (where urine collects before the ureter)
What is a nephron? :: A kidney tubule – the functional unit of the kidney that filters blood and produces urine
List the parts of a nephron in order. :: Bowman's capsule (around glomerulus) → proximal convoluted tubule → loop of Henle → distal convoluted tubule → collecting duct
What is the glomerulus? :: A knot of capillaries inside the Bowman's capsule where ultrafiltration happens
Which substances are not filtered into the Bowman's capsule? :: Blood cells and large proteins – too big to pass through
What is the glomerular filtrate made of? :: Water, glucose, salts (ions) and urea
Why is there no glucose in the urine of a healthy person? :: All glucose is selectively reabsorbed into the blood in the proximal convoluted tubule
What is the role of the loop of Henle? :: Creates a high salt concentration in the medulla, so water can be reabsorbed from the collecting duct by osmosis
What happens to ADH if the blood is too dilute (too much water)? :: Less ADH released; collecting duct less permeable; less water reabsorbed – large volume of dilute urine
What detects the water content of the blood? :: The hypothalamus in the brain (signals the pituitary gland)
Why might urine be more concentrated on a hot day? :: More water lost in sweat, so blood becomes more concentrated; more ADH released and more water reabsorbed
What is osmoregulation? :: Controlling the water content (and salt concentration) of the blood
Why does the renal vein contain less urea than the renal artery? :: Urea is filtered out of the blood by the kidney and excreted in urine
Why might protein be found in urine? :: The glomerulus is damaged, letting proteins through (kidney disease)

# 2j Coordination and response
What is homeostasis? :: Maintaining a constant internal environment (e.g. body temperature, blood glucose, water content)
What is a stimulus? :: A change in the environment that is detected by a receptor
What is the sequence of a coordinated response? :: Stimulus → receptor → coordinator (CNS) → effector → response
What is phototropism? :: Plant growth response towards light (positive phototropism in shoots)
What is geotropism (gravitropism)? :: Growth response to gravity – roots grow down (positive), shoots grow up (negative)
Explain how auxin causes phototropism. :: Auxin moves to the shaded side of the shoot tip and causes cells there to elongate more, so the shoot bends towards the light
Compare nervous and hormonal control. :: Nervous – electrical impulses, very fast, short-lived, precise ;; Hormonal – chemicals in blood, slower, longer-lasting, widespread
What makes up the central nervous system? :: The brain and spinal cord
What is a reflex arc? :: Stimulus → receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone (spinal cord) → motor neurone → effector → response; rapid and automatic
What is a synapse? :: A gap between neurones; an impulse causes release of neurotransmitter which diffuses across and triggers an impulse in the next neurone
State the function of the cornea, iris, lens, retina and optic nerve. :: Cornea – refracts light ;; Iris – controls pupil size ;; Lens – focuses light onto retina ;; Retina – contains light-sensitive receptor cells (rods and cones) ;; Optic nerve – carries impulses to brain
How does the eye respond to bright light? :: Circular muscles of the iris contract, radial muscles relax – pupil constricts
How does the eye focus on a near object? :: Ciliary muscles contract, suspensory ligaments slacken, lens becomes fatter (more convex) – refracts light more
How does the eye focus on a distant object? :: Ciliary muscles relax, suspensory ligaments tighten, lens is pulled thin
How does the body respond to being too hot? :: Sweating (evaporation cools skin); vasodilation – arterioles near skin widen so more heat lost by radiation; hairs lie flat
How does the body respond to being too cold? :: Shivering (respiration releases heat); vasoconstriction – less blood to skin; hairs stand up trapping insulating air; no sweating
Source and effect of adrenaline? :: Adrenal glands; increases heart rate (and breathing rate), raises blood glucose – prepares for 'fight or flight'
Source and effect of insulin? :: Pancreas; lowers blood glucose by making liver and muscles convert glucose to glycogen
Source and effect of testosterone? :: Testes; male secondary sexual characteristics
Source and effect of progesterone? :: Ovaries; maintains the lining of the uterus during the menstrual cycle and pregnancy
Source and effect of oestrogen? :: Ovaries; female secondary sexual characteristics, repairs/thickens uterus lining, controls menstrual cycle
Source and effect of ADH? :: Pituitary gland; controls water content of the blood (more water reabsorbed in kidney)
Source and effect of FSH? :: Pituitary gland; causes an egg to mature in the ovary and stimulates oestrogen production
Source and effect of LH? :: Pituitary gland; stimulates ovulation (release of an egg)
Source and effect of glucagon? :: Pancreas; raises blood glucose by converting glycogen to glucose in the liver
What is Type 1 diabetes and how is it treated? :: The pancreas does not produce enough insulin; treated with insulin injections and controlled diet
## Homeostasis and responses – extra detail
Why is homeostasis important? :: Keeps conditions (temperature, water, glucose) at optimum levels so enzymes and cells work properly
What is negative feedback? :: When a change from the normal level is detected and a response is triggered that brings the level back to normal
What is a receptor? :: A cell or organ that detects a stimulus (e.g. light receptors in the retina)
What is an effector? :: A muscle or gland that brings about a response
Why do organisms respond to changes in their environment? :: To increase their chances of survival (e.g. find food, avoid danger, keep internal conditions constant)
## Plant responses
Why is positive phototropism useful to a shoot? :: Leaves receive more light for photosynthesis
Why is positive geotropism useful to a root? :: Roots grow down into the soil to anchor the plant and absorb water and minerals
What is auxin? :: A plant hormone made in shoot and root tips that controls growth by cell elongation
Explain how auxin causes a horizontal shoot to grow upwards. :: Auxin collects on the lower side due to gravity; in shoots auxin stimulates cell elongation, so the lower side grows faster and the shoot bends up
Explain how auxin causes a horizontal root to grow downwards. :: Auxin collects on the lower side; in roots auxin inhibits cell elongation, so the upper side grows faster and the root bends down
What happens to a shoot if its tip is removed? :: It no longer responds to light (no phototropism) because the tip makes the auxin
What happens to a shoot if light comes from all sides (or a clinostat is used)? :: Auxin is evenly distributed, so the shoot grows straight
## Nervous system – extra detail
What are the three types of neurone? :: Sensory, relay and motor
What does a sensory neurone do? :: Carries impulses from receptors to the CNS
What does a relay neurone do? :: Carries impulses within the CNS – from sensory to motor neurones
What does a motor neurone do? :: Carries impulses from the CNS to effectors (muscles or glands)
How do nerve impulses travel? :: As electrical impulses along neurones
Why are reflexes useful? :: They are rapid and automatic (don't involve the conscious brain), protecting the body from harm
Give an example of a reflex action. :: Withdrawing the hand from a hot object; blinking; the pupil reflex
Describe what happens at a synapse. :: Impulse arrives at the end of the neurone → neurotransmitter is released → diffuses across the gap → binds to receptors on the next neurone → new impulse triggered
Why do synapses ensure impulses travel in one direction? :: Neurotransmitter is only released from one side and receptors are only on the other side
## Eye – extra detail
What is the function of the sclera? :: Tough white outer layer that protects the eye
What is the function of the pupil? :: The hole in the iris through which light enters the eye
What is the fovea? :: The part of the retina with the most cones – gives the sharpest, most detailed (colour) vision
What is the blind spot? :: Where the optic nerve leaves the retina – no light receptors, so no image detected
What is the difference between rods and cones? :: Rods – sensitive to dim light, black and white ;; Cones – work in bright light, detect colour
How does the eye respond to dim light? :: Radial muscles of the iris contract, circular muscles relax – pupil dilates to let more light in
Why is the pupil reflex important? :: Bright light could damage the retina; dim light – letting in more light improves vision
What is accommodation? :: Changing the shape of the lens to focus on near or distant objects
## Thermoregulation – extra detail
What is normal human core body temperature? :: About 37 °C
Which part of the brain monitors body temperature? :: The hypothalamus (thermoregulatory centre)
Where are temperature receptors found? :: In the skin and in the hypothalamus (monitoring blood temperature)
How does sweating cool the body? :: Water in sweat evaporates from the skin surface, using heat energy from the body
Explain how vasodilation helps cool the body. :: Arterioles supplying skin capillaries widen, so more blood flows near the surface and more heat is lost by radiation
Explain how vasoconstriction helps keep the body warm. :: Arterioles supplying skin capillaries narrow, so less blood flows near the surface and less heat is lost
How does shivering warm the body? :: Rapid muscle contractions increase respiration, which releases heat
Why does hair standing up help keep humans/animals warm? :: Erector muscles contract, raising hairs that trap a layer of insulating air
Do blood vessels move up and down in the skin during thermoregulation? :: No – the arterioles widen or narrow (vasodilation/vasoconstriction); vessels don't move
## Hormones – extra detail
What is a hormone? :: A chemical messenger secreted by an endocrine gland into the blood, which carries it to target organs
What is a target organ? :: An organ affected by a particular hormone
How are hormones transported? :: In the blood plasma
Explain how blood glucose concentration is lowered after a meal. :: Pancreas detects high glucose and secretes insulin; insulin makes liver and muscle cells take up glucose and convert it to glycogen; blood glucose falls
Explain how blood glucose concentration is raised when it is too low. :: Pancreas secretes glucagon; glucagon makes the liver convert glycogen into glucose, released into the blood
Where is glycogen stored? :: In the liver and muscles
What causes Type 1 diabetes? :: The pancreas cells that make insulin are destroyed (by the immune system) so little or no insulin is produced
What happens to blood glucose in an untreated person with Type 1 diabetes? :: It rises too high after meals because glucose is not taken up and stored – glucose may appear in urine
Why must people with Type 1 diabetes monitor their diet and blood glucose? :: To match insulin doses to carbohydrate intake and activity, avoiding blood glucose going too high or too low
Why is insulin injected rather than taken by mouth? :: It is a protein and would be digested in the gut
What are the effects of adrenaline on the body? :: Increases heart rate and breathing rate, increases blood glucose (glycogen → glucose), dilates pupils, diverts blood to muscles

# 3a Reproduction
Compare sexual and asexual reproduction. :: Sexual – two parents, gametes fuse, offspring genetically varied ;; Asexual – one parent, no gametes, offspring genetically identical (clones)
What is fertilisation? :: The fusion of a male and female gamete nucleus to form a zygote
Describe the structure of an insect-pollinated flower. :: Large, brightly coloured petals, scent and nectar; anthers and stigma inside the flower; sticky/spiky pollen
Describe the structure of a wind-pollinated flower. :: Small dull petals, no nectar; anthers and feathery stigma hang outside; large amounts of light smooth pollen
What is pollination? :: Transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma
Describe fertilisation in flowering plants. :: Pollen tube grows down the style to the ovary; the male nucleus travels down and fuses with the ovule (egg) nucleus
After fertilisation, what do the ovule and ovary become? :: Ovule → seed; ovary → fruit
What are the conditions needed for germination? :: Water, oxygen and a suitable temperature
How do germinating seeds get energy before they can photosynthesise? :: From food stores in the seed (cotyledons/endosperm)
Give examples of natural asexual reproduction in plants. :: Runners (strawberry plants), tubers (potatoes), bulbs
How do gardeners use cuttings? :: Cut a stem with leaves, dip in rooting powder (auxin), plant in moist compost – produces clones quickly
What are the roles of oestrogen and progesterone in the menstrual cycle? :: Oestrogen – repairs and thickens the uterus lining (and stimulates LH) ;; Progesterone – maintains the lining; falling progesterone causes menstruation
What happens on approximately day 14 of the menstrual cycle? :: Ovulation – an egg is released from the ovary (triggered by LH)
What is the role of the placenta? :: Exchange of materials between mother and fetus: oxygen and nutrients into fetal blood, CO₂ and urea out; acts as a barrier to some toxins and pathogens
What is the role of amniotic fluid? :: Protects the developing embryo from bumps
What are the secondary sexual characteristics in males? :: Voice deepens, growth of facial and body hair, muscles develop, penis and testes grow (caused by testosterone); sperm production starts at puberty but is not itself a secondary sexual characteristic
What are the secondary sexual characteristics in females? :: Breasts develop, hips widen, menstruation begins, pubic and underarm hair (oestrogen)
## Plant reproduction – extra detail
What is a gamete? :: A sex cell (e.g. sperm, egg, pollen nucleus, ovule) – haploid
What is a zygote? :: The fertilised egg cell formed when two gamete nuclei fuse – diploid
Give one advantage of sexual reproduction. :: Produces genetic variation, so a population is more likely to survive changes in the environment or disease
Give one advantage of asexual reproduction. :: Only one parent needed; fast; offspring identical to a successful parent
Give one disadvantage of asexual reproduction. :: No genetic variation, so all offspring may be killed by a disease or environmental change
What is the function of the sepals? :: Protect the flower when it is a bud
What is the function of petals? :: Attract insects (in insect-pollinated flowers)
What is the stamen and what does it do? :: The male part: anther (makes pollen) on a filament (holds the anther up)
What is the carpel? :: The female part: stigma, style and ovary (containing ovules)
What is the function of the stigma? :: Receives pollen during pollination
What is the function of the ovary in a flower? :: Contains the ovules; becomes the fruit after fertilisation
What is the function of nectar? :: A sugary liquid that attracts insects
Why do wind-pollinated flowers produce huge amounts of pollen? :: Most pollen is lost because wind transfer is random – increases chance of reaching a stigma
Why is wind-pollinated pollen light and smooth? :: So it can be carried easily by the wind
Why do wind-pollinated flowers have feathery stigmas? :: Large surface area to catch pollen from the air
What is the difference between self- and cross-pollination? :: Self – pollen to stigma of the same plant ;; Cross – pollen to stigma of a different plant of the same species (more variation)
After fertilisation, what does the zygote become? :: The embryo of the seed
Why do seeds need water to germinate? :: To activate enzymes and allow the seed to swell and the testa to split; for transport of food stores
Why do seeds need oxygen to germinate? :: For aerobic respiration to release energy for growth
Why do seeds need a suitable temperature to germinate? :: For enzymes to work (too cold – slow; too hot – denatured)
Describe how food stores are used during germination. :: Enzymes digest starch to sugars and proteins to amino acids; these are used for respiration and growth until leaves can photosynthesise
## Germination practical
Describe an experiment to investigate the conditions needed for germination. :: Set up tubes of seeds on cotton wool: one with water, oxygen and warmth (control); one dry (no water); one in the fridge (cold); one with boiled cooled water and oil layer (no oxygen); leave several days and count seeds germinated
Why is boiled water covered with oil used in the germination practical? :: Boiling removes dissolved oxygen; the oil layer stops oxygen dissolving back in
In the germination practical, what is the dependent variable? :: Number (or percentage) of seeds that germinate
In the germination practical, name control variables. :: Same type, number and age of seeds; same time; all conditions the same except the one being tested
How do you calculate percentage germination? :: Number of seeds germinated ÷ total number of seeds × 100
Why use many seeds in each tube in the germination practical? :: Some seeds may not be viable; a larger sample makes results more reliable
## Asexual reproduction in plants
What is a runner? :: A sideways stem growing along the ground that forms new plants (e.g. strawberry)
Why do gardeners take cuttings? :: Quick and cheap way to produce many genetically identical plants with desirable characteristics
Why are cuttings covered with a plastic bag? :: Keeps humidity high to reduce water loss by transpiration (they have no roots yet)
## Human reproduction – extra detail
What are the functions of the testes? :: Produce sperm and the hormone testosterone
What is the function of the sperm duct? :: Carries sperm from the testes to the urethra
What do the prostate gland and seminal vesicles do? :: Add fluid containing nutrients to sperm to make semen
What is the function of the scrotum? :: Holds testes outside the body to keep them slightly cooler for sperm production
What is the function of the penis? :: Transfers semen into the vagina; contains the urethra
What are the functions of the ovaries? :: Produce eggs and the hormones oestrogen and progesterone
What is the function of the oviduct (fallopian tube)? :: Carries the egg from the ovary to the uterus; site of fertilisation
What is the function of the uterus? :: Where the embryo implants and the fetus develops
What is the function of the cervix? :: Ring of muscle at the entrance to the uterus; holds the baby in during pregnancy
What is the function of the vagina? :: Receives sperm during intercourse; birth canal
How long is a typical menstrual cycle? :: About 28 days
What happens in days 1–5 of the menstrual cycle? :: Menstruation – the uterus lining breaks down and is lost
What is the role of FSH in the menstrual cycle? :: Made by pituitary; causes an egg (follicle) to mature in the ovary and stimulates the ovary to produce oestrogen
What is the role of LH in the menstrual cycle? :: Made by pituitary; triggers ovulation (release of the egg) around day 14
What happens if the egg is not fertilised? :: Progesterone levels fall, so the uterus lining breaks down (menstruation)
What happens to progesterone if pregnancy occurs? :: Stays high, maintaining the uterus lining so menstruation does not happen
What is implantation? :: When the embryo (ball of cells) embeds in the uterus lining
Which hormone stimulates the release of LH? :: Oestrogen
How is the placenta adapted for exchange? :: Large surface area (villi); thin walls (short diffusion distance); good blood supply from mother and fetus maintains concentration gradients
Why does the mother's blood not mix with the fetus's blood? :: Prevents blood pressure damage, mixing of different blood groups and transfer of some pathogens
What is the umbilical cord? :: Connects the fetus to the placenta; contains blood vessels (artery and vein)

# 3b Inheritance
What is a gene? :: A section of DNA that codes for a specific protein
What is an allele? :: A different version of the same gene
Describe the structure of DNA. :: Two strands coiled to form a double helix, linked by complementary base pairs: A–T and C–G
What is a chromosome? :: A long strand of DNA in the nucleus carrying many genes
How many chromosomes are in a human body cell? :: 46 (23 pairs) – diploid
How many chromosomes are in a human gamete? :: 23 – haploid
Define dominant and recessive. :: Dominant – allele expressed if present (one copy needed) ;; Recessive – only expressed when two copies are present
Define homozygous and heterozygous. :: Homozygous – two identical alleles (AA or aa) ;; Heterozygous – two different alleles (Aa)
Define genotype and phenotype. :: Genotype – the alleles an organism has ;; Phenotype – its physical characteristics
What is codominance? :: Both alleles are expressed in the phenotype of a heterozygote (e.g. blood group AB)
How many alleles control human ABO blood groups? :: Three: Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant; Iᴼ is recessive
What is the expected ratio from a monohybrid cross Aa × Aa? :: 3 : 1 (dominant : recessive phenotype); genotypes 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa
What is a test cross? :: Cross an organism showing the dominant phenotype with a homozygous recessive – if any offspring show the recessive trait, the parent was heterozygous
How is sex determined in humans? :: Females XX, males XY; the father's sperm (X or Y) decides – 50% chance of each
What is mitosis and what is it used for? :: Cell division producing two genetically identical diploid cells – used for growth, repair and asexual reproduction
What is meiosis and where does it happen? :: Cell division producing four genetically different haploid gametes – happens in the testes and ovaries
Why does meiosis produce variation? :: Chromosomes are shuffled (independent assortment) so each gamete gets a different combination; plus random fertilisation
What are the two causes of variation? :: Genetic (alleles inherited) and environmental (e.g. diet, climate) – often both
What is a mutation? :: A rare, random change in the base sequence of DNA; most have no effect, some are harmful, a few are beneficial
What increases the rate of mutation? :: Ionising radiation (gamma rays, X-rays), UV light and some chemicals (mutagens, e.g. in tobacco smoke)
Describe evolution by natural selection. :: Variation exists in a population; some individuals have alleles that make them better adapted; they survive and reproduce more; pass on the advantageous alleles; over many generations the allele frequency increases
Explain how antibiotic resistance develops in bacteria. :: A random mutation makes a bacterium resistant; antibiotics kill non-resistant bacteria; resistant ones survive and reproduce rapidly, passing on the resistance allele
What does a pedigree diagram show? :: How a characteristic (often a genetic disorder) is inherited through generations of a family
## DNA and protein synthesis
What is the genome? :: The entire DNA of an organism
Name the four bases in DNA. :: Adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C) and guanine (G)
How does a gene code for a protein? :: The sequence of bases in the gene determines the order of amino acids in the protein
How many bases code for one amino acid? :: Three – a triplet (codon)
What is RNA? :: A single-stranded molecule similar to DNA; contains uracil (U) instead of thymine
What happens during transcription? :: In the nucleus, the DNA of a gene unzips and a complementary mRNA copy is made of one strand
What happens during translation? :: At a ribosome in the cytoplasm, the mRNA codons are read; tRNA brings specific amino acids, which join in the correct order to make a protein
What is the role of tRNA? :: Carries a specific amino acid to the ribosome; its anticodon pairs with the complementary mRNA codon
Why does mRNA leave the nucleus but DNA does not? :: DNA is too large and must be protected; mRNA is a small copy that can pass through nuclear pores to the ribosomes
Which base in RNA pairs with adenine? :: Uracil (U)
How can a mutation in a gene change a protein? :: Changes the base sequence, which can change the amino acid sequence, so the protein folds into a different shape and may not work (e.g. enzyme active site changes)
Why do most mutations have no effect? :: They may occur in non-coding DNA, or the changed triplet may still code for the same amino acid
## Genetics – extra detail
What is monohybrid inheritance? :: Inheritance of a single characteristic controlled by one gene
How do you draw a genetic diagram? :: Parent phenotypes → parent genotypes → gametes (circled) → Punnett square of offspring genotypes → offspring phenotypes and ratio
What is the expected ratio of offspring from Aa × aa? :: 1 : 1 (heterozygous dominant : homozygous recessive)
What offspring result from AA × aa? :: All Aa – all show the dominant phenotype
Two heterozygous parents: what is the probability a child is homozygous recessive? :: 1 in 4 (25%, 0.25)
Two heterozygous parents: what proportion of offspring with the dominant phenotype are heterozygous? :: 2 out of 3
Why might an actual ratio differ from the expected ratio? :: Fertilisation is random, so small numbers of offspring may not match the predicted ratio
What is the probability of having a boy (or girl) at each birth? :: 50% (1 in 2) – independent of previous births
How can you tell from a pedigree that a trait is recessive? :: Two unaffected parents have an affected child – both parents must be heterozygous carriers
What is a carrier? :: A heterozygous person who has one recessive allele for a disorder but does not show it
What are the possible genotypes for blood group A? :: IᴬIᴬ or IᴬIᴼ
What are the possible genotypes for blood group B? :: IᴮIᴮ or IᴮIᴼ
What is the genotype for blood group O? :: IᴼIᴼ
What is the genotype for blood group AB? :: IᴬIᴮ – both alleles expressed (codominance)
Can parents with blood groups A and B have a child with blood group O? :: Yes – if they are IᴬIᴼ and IᴮIᴼ (1 in 4 chance)
What is a diploid cell? :: A cell with two sets of chromosomes (46 in humans) – body cells
What is a haploid cell? :: A cell with one set of chromosomes (23 in humans) – gametes
Why must gametes be haploid? :: So when they fuse at fertilisation the zygote has the normal diploid number
## Cell division – extra detail
What happens to chromosomes before mitosis? :: Each chromosome is copied (DNA replicates) to form two identical chromatids
Describe what happens during mitosis. :: Chromosomes are copied, line up in the middle of the cell, the copies are pulled to opposite ends, and the cell divides into two identical cells
Give the differences between mitosis and meiosis. :: Mitosis – 1 division, 2 cells, diploid, identical, growth/repair ;; Meiosis – 2 divisions, 4 cells, haploid, genetically different, gametes
Why does random fertilisation increase variation? :: Any male gamete can fuse with any female gamete, giving huge numbers of possible combinations of alleles
## Variation and evolution – extra detail
Give an example of variation caused only by genes. :: Blood group (or eye colour, inherited disorders)
Give an example of variation caused by the environment. :: Scars, language spoken, tattoos
Give an example of variation caused by genes and environment together. :: Height or body mass (also skin colour)
What is continuous variation? :: A characteristic with a range of values (e.g. height) – often affected by genes and environment; shown by a histogram/line graph
What is discontinuous variation? :: A characteristic with distinct categories (e.g. blood group) – usually genetic; shown by a bar chart
What is the source of new alleles? :: Mutation
Can a mutation be beneficial? :: Rarely – it may give an advantage (e.g. antibiotic resistance in bacteria), increasing survival
What is an adaptation? :: A feature that helps an organism survive and reproduce in its environment
Who proposed the theory of evolution by natural selection? :: Charles Darwin (and Alfred Russel Wallace)
What does 'survival of the fittest' mean? :: Individuals best adapted to the environment are more likely to survive and reproduce
Why does antibiotic resistance spread quickly? :: Bacteria reproduce very rapidly (and can transfer plasmids), so resistant bacteria soon form large populations
How can the development of antibiotic resistance be reduced? :: Only prescribe antibiotics when needed (not for viruses); complete the full course; avoid overuse in farming
Why don't antibiotics cure viral infections? :: Viruses live inside cells and have no cell structures for antibiotics to act on

# 4 Ecology and environment
Define population, community, habitat and ecosystem. :: Population – all organisms of one species in an area ;; Community – all populations in an area ;; Habitat – where an organism lives ;; Ecosystem – a community and its physical environment interacting
How do you use a quadrat to estimate population size? :: Place quadrats randomly (random coordinates); count organisms in each; mean per quadrat × (total area ÷ quadrat area)
How do you use a quadrat to investigate distribution along a gradient? :: Place quadrats at regular intervals along a transect (tape measure) and record organisms and abiotic factors at each point
Name some biotic and abiotic factors affecting populations. :: Biotic – predators, competition, disease, food ;; Abiotic – temperature, light, water, pH, soil minerals, CO₂/O₂
Define producer, primary consumer, secondary consumer, decomposer. :: Producer – makes its own food by photosynthesis ;; Primary consumer – eats producers (herbivore) ;; Secondary consumer – eats primary consumers ;; Decomposer – breaks down dead material
What does a pyramid of biomass show? :: The mass of living material at each trophic level
Why is energy lost between trophic levels? :: Not all of the organism is eaten; some is indigestible (lost in faeces); energy is lost as heat from respiration and used for movement; only ~10% passes on
Why are food chains rarely longer than five trophic levels? :: Energy is lost at each level, so not enough remains to support more levels
Describe the carbon cycle. :: CO₂ removed by photosynthesis → carbon compounds in plants → animals by feeding → returned by respiration of plants, animals and decomposers, and combustion of fossil fuels
Describe the nitrogen cycle. :: Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (soil/root nodules) and lightning make nitrates/ammonium ;; plants absorb nitrates → proteins → animals ;; decomposers make ammonium (putrefaction) ;; nitrifying bacteria: ammonium → nitrite → nitrate ;; denitrifying bacteria (waterlogged soil) turn nitrates into N₂
Name the gases that cause the enhanced greenhouse effect. :: Carbon dioxide, methane, water vapour, nitrous oxide, CFCs
What are the effects of global warming? :: Rising sea levels, flooding, extreme weather, changes in species distribution, loss of habitats, crop failures
What are the effects of sulfur dioxide and how is it produced? :: Produced by burning fossil fuels; dissolves to form acid rain – kills trees and aquatic organisms, damages buildings
How does carbon monoxide pollution form and what does it do? :: Incomplete combustion of fuels; binds to haemoglobin reducing oxygen transport
Describe eutrophication. :: Fertiliser/sewage runs into water → algae grow rapidly (algal bloom) → block light, plants die → decomposers break down dead matter and use up oxygen → fish and other organisms die
What are the effects of deforestation? :: Loss of habitat and biodiversity; soil erosion; less CO₂ absorbed and more released (burning) – increases greenhouse effect; disturbs water cycle; leaching of nutrients
## Sampling practicals – extra detail
Why must quadrats be placed randomly? :: To avoid bias, so the sample is representative of the whole area
How can quadrats be placed randomly? :: Lay two tape measures at right angles and use a random number generator to pick coordinates
Worked example: mean 6 daisies per 0.25 m² quadrat, field 400 m². Population? :: 6 ÷ 0.25 = 24 per m² ;; 24 × 400 = 9600 daisies
How can you improve the accuracy of a quadrat population estimate? :: Use more quadrats (larger sample) and calculate a mean
What is percentage cover? :: The percentage of a quadrat's area covered by a species – used when individual plants are hard to count (e.g. grass)
What is a belt transect? :: Placing quadrats continuously or at regular intervals along a line to study how distribution changes across a habitat
Give abiotic factors that might be measured along a transect. :: Light intensity (light meter), soil moisture, soil pH, temperature
Why is a transect used instead of random sampling? :: There is a gradient (change in conditions) across the area, e.g. from shade to open ground or from sea to land
Give safety precautions in fieldwork. :: Wear gloves/wash hands (soil, plants, animal waste); be aware of water/tides; avoid hazardous plants and animals; work in groups
What is a limitation of using quadrats for animals? :: Most animals move away, so quadrats are best for plants and slow-moving animals
## Feeding relationships – extra detail
What does an arrow in a food chain show? :: The direction of energy transfer (from the organism eaten to the eater)
What is a trophic level? :: A feeding level (position) in a food chain
What is a tertiary consumer? :: An animal that eats secondary consumers
What is a food web? :: A network of interconnected food chains in an ecosystem
Why can a change in one population affect others in a food web? :: Organisms are interdependent – e.g. losing prey reduces food for predators, and the predator's other prey may then be eaten more
What does a pyramid of numbers show? :: The number of organisms at each trophic level
Why can a pyramid of numbers be an odd shape? :: Organisms vary in size – e.g. one large tree supports many caterpillars (inverted base)
What does a pyramid of energy show? :: The energy transferred at each trophic level (per unit area per year) – always pyramid-shaped
Why is a pyramid of energy always pyramid-shaped? :: Energy is lost at every trophic level, so each level has less than the one below
What is the original source of energy for almost all food chains? :: The Sun (light energy captured by producers in photosynthesis)
How do you calculate efficiency of energy transfer? :: Energy transferred to next level ÷ energy at previous level × 100
Worked example: grass 10 000 kJ, rabbit 1000 kJ. Efficiency? :: 1000 ÷ 10 000 × 100 = 10%
What is the role of decomposers in ecosystems? :: Break down dead organisms and waste, releasing CO₂ and recycling mineral ions (e.g. nitrates) back into the soil
Name two types of decomposer. :: Bacteria and fungi
## Carbon cycle – extra detail
Which processes return CO₂ to the atmosphere? :: Respiration (plants, animals, decomposers) and combustion (burning fossil fuels and wood)
How are fossil fuels formed? :: Dead organisms that did not decay were compressed over millions of years, locking up carbon as coal, oil and gas
## Nitrogen cycle – extra detail
Why do plants and animals need nitrogen? :: To make proteins (amino acids) and DNA
Why can't plants use nitrogen gas from the air? :: It is very unreactive; plants can only absorb nitrogen as nitrate (or ammonium) ions
What do nitrogen-fixing bacteria do? :: Convert nitrogen gas (N₂) into ammonium/nitrogen compounds – free-living in soil or in root nodules of legumes (peas, beans, clover)
What do nitrifying bacteria do? :: Convert ammonium ions into nitrites and then nitrates (need oxygen)
What do denitrifying bacteria do? :: Convert nitrates back into nitrogen gas – in waterlogged (anaerobic) soils
What do decomposers do in the nitrogen cycle? :: Break down proteins and urea in dead matter and waste into ammonium ions (ammonification/putrefaction)
How does lightning contribute to the nitrogen cycle? :: Its energy makes nitrogen react with oxygen, forming nitrogen oxides that dissolve in rain to make nitrates
Why do farmers plough or drain soil? :: Adds oxygen – helps nitrifying bacteria and reduces denitrifying bacteria (which are favoured by waterlogged soil)
Why do farmers grow legumes in crop rotation? :: Nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root nodules increase the nitrate content of the soil
## Human impact – extra detail
Explain the greenhouse effect. :: Short-wave radiation from the Sun passes through the atmosphere; Earth emits longer-wave infrared radiation; greenhouse gases absorb and re-radiate it, warming the Earth
What is the enhanced greenhouse effect? :: Human activities increase greenhouse gas levels, trapping more heat and causing global warming
Give human sources of carbon dioxide. :: Burning fossil fuels, deforestation (burning trees)
Give human sources of methane. :: Cattle (digestion), rice paddy fields, landfill (decomposition)
Give a human source of nitrous oxide. :: Fertilisers (bacteria in soil) and vehicle engines
Give a source of CFCs. :: Old aerosols, refrigerators and air conditioning
How does acid rain affect lakes? :: Lowers pH of water, killing fish and other aquatic organisms (also releases toxic aluminium)
How can sulfur dioxide pollution be reduced? :: Remove sulfur from fuels; use scrubbers in power-station chimneys; use renewable energy
Explain why fish die during eutrophication. :: Dead plants and algae are broken down by aerobic bacteria (decomposers), which use up dissolved oxygen; fish cannot respire
How does untreated sewage cause water pollution? :: Adds nutrients (eutrophication) and organic matter for bacteria to decompose (uses up oxygen); also contains pathogens
How can eutrophication be reduced? :: Use less fertiliser; don't apply before rain; leave buffer strips by rivers; treat sewage
How does deforestation increase atmospheric CO₂? :: Fewer trees to remove CO₂ by photosynthesis; burning or decay of trees releases CO₂
How does deforestation cause soil erosion? :: Without tree roots to hold soil and leaves to shelter it, rain washes the soil away
How does deforestation lead to leaching? :: Without tree roots taking up minerals, rain washes nutrients out of the soil, making it infertile
How does deforestation affect the water cycle? :: Less transpiration, so less water vapour and rainfall; more surface run-off and flooding
How does deforestation reduce biodiversity? :: Habitats are destroyed, so many species lose food and shelter and may become extinct

# 5 Use of biological resources
How do glasshouses increase crop yield? :: Control temperature, light and CO₂ (e.g. paraffin heaters raise CO₂ and temperature) – removes limiting factors
Why do farmers use fertilisers? :: Replace minerals (e.g. nitrates) used by crops so they grow better – increases yield
Compare pesticides and biological control. :: Pesticides – chemicals that kill pests; quick, but can harm other organisms and bioaccumulate; pests become resistant ;; Biological control – a natural predator is introduced; long-term, no chemicals, but may become a pest itself
What is the role of yeast in making bread? :: Yeast respires anaerobically, producing CO₂ that makes the dough rise
What is the role of bacteria in making yoghurt? :: Lactobacillus converts lactose into lactic acid, which lowers the pH and thickens/sours the milk
Describe how yoghurt is made. :: Pasteurise milk (kill bacteria), cool, add Lactobacillus, incubate ~40 °C, lactic acid produced, cool and package
What happens in an industrial fermenter? :: Microorganisms grow in controlled conditions: aseptic precautions, nutrients, optimum temperature (water jacket) and pH, oxygen supply (aeration) and stirring – to make e.g. penicillin, insulin
Why is it important to use aseptic precautions in fermenters? :: To prevent contamination by other microorganisms that would compete or produce unwanted products
How are fish farmed to increase yield? :: Controlled water quality; nets to protect from predators; intraspecific predation prevented by separating sizes; disease controlled; high-protein food; selective breeding
What is selective breeding? :: Humans choose plants or animals with desired characteristics and breed them together over many generations
Give examples of selective breeding. :: High-yield cereal crops, disease-resistant plants, cows with high milk yield, animals with more meat
What are restriction enzymes? :: Enzymes that cut DNA at specific base sequences, leaving sticky ends
What are ligase enzymes? :: Enzymes that join pieces of DNA together
What is a vector in genetic modification? :: Something that carries the gene into a cell – e.g. a plasmid or virus
Describe how human insulin is made using bacteria. :: Insulin gene cut out with restriction enzyme; plasmid cut with same enzyme; gene inserted with ligase (recombinant plasmid); plasmid put into bacteria; bacteria grown in fermenter and produce insulin
What is a transgenic organism? :: An organism that contains genes from a different species
Give an example of a GM crop and its advantage. :: Insect-resistant crops (Bt gene) – higher yield, less pesticide; or crops resistant to herbicide
Describe how Dolly the sheep was cloned. :: Nucleus removed from an egg cell; nucleus from a body (udder) cell of the donor inserted; electric shock to make it divide; embryo implanted into a surrogate mother
Give uses of cloned transgenic animals. :: Producing human proteins/antibodies in their milk; organs for transplantation
## Food production – extra detail
What are polythene tunnels used for? :: Like glasshouses – cheaply raise temperature and protect crops, allowing longer growing seasons and higher yields
Explain why burning paraffin in a glasshouse increases yield. :: Releases CO₂ and heat – both increase the rate of photosynthesis if they are limiting
Why is there no point increasing CO₂ in a glasshouse if light is limiting? :: The rate of photosynthesis is limited by light, so extra CO₂ will not increase it further (wasted money)
Why do growers use artificial lighting in glasshouses? :: To increase light intensity and length of 'day', so photosynthesis continues for longer
Compare manure and artificial fertilisers. :: Manure – cheap, adds organic matter and improves soil structure, releases nutrients slowly, exact composition unknown ;; Artificial – exact amounts of minerals (NPK), quick acting, more likely to leach and cause eutrophication
Which three main elements are in NPK fertilisers? :: Nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium
What is a pest? :: An organism that damages crops or livestock (e.g. insects, weeds, fungi)
Give the advantages of chemical pesticides. :: Fast acting, easy to apply, kill most of the pests
Give the disadvantages of chemical pesticides. :: May kill harmless/beneficial species; can bioaccumulate in food chains; pests can become resistant; must be reapplied
Give the advantages of biological control. :: Specific to the pest; no chemicals; long-lasting (predator reproduces); pests don't become resistant
Give the disadvantages of biological control. :: Slow; doesn't eradicate the pest completely; the introduced organism may become a pest or eat other species
What is bioaccumulation? :: When a substance (e.g. pesticide) builds up in concentration along a food chain, reaching toxic levels in top predators
## Microorganisms – extra detail
What happens to the ethanol produced by yeast during baking? :: It evaporates during baking
Why is bread dough left in a warm place to rise? :: Yeast enzymes work best at warm temperatures, so respiration and CO₂ production are faster
Why does baking stop the bread rising? :: High temperature kills the yeast (denatures enzymes)
Describe how to investigate the effect of temperature on anaerobic respiration in yeast. :: Mix yeast with glucose (sugar) solution; cover with a layer of oil (anaerobic); place in water baths at different temperatures; measure the volume of CO₂ (gas syringe/bubbles counted) in a set time
In the yeast respiration practical, what is the independent variable? :: Temperature (or sugar concentration / type of sugar)
In the yeast respiration practical, what is the dependent variable? :: Volume of CO₂ produced per minute (or number of bubbles, or height of dough rise)
In the yeast respiration practical, what are the control variables? :: Mass/concentration of yeast, concentration and volume of sugar solution, time, pH
Why is a layer of oil put on the yeast mixture? :: To stop oxygen entering, so respiration is anaerobic
Why should the yeast mixture be left to acclimatise before measuring? :: So it reaches the water bath temperature and the rate becomes steady
Describe the expected results of the yeast temperature practical. :: CO₂ production increases up to an optimum (about 35–40 °C) then falls as enzymes denature
What does pasteurising milk do in yoghurt making? :: Heats it (about 85–95 °C) to kill harmful bacteria that would compete or cause disease
Why is milk cooled to about 40 °C before adding the bacteria? :: It's the optimum temperature for Lactobacillus enzymes; hotter would kill them
Why does lactic acid help preserve yoghurt? :: The low pH stops the growth of most harmful microorganisms
Why does milk clot (thicken) when making yoghurt? :: The lactic acid lowers the pH, causing milk proteins to coagulate
## Fermenters
What is the function of the water jacket in a fermenter? :: Removes heat produced by respiration of microorganisms, keeping the temperature at the optimum
Why is air pumped into a fermenter? :: Supplies oxygen for aerobic respiration of the microorganisms
What is the function of the stirrer (paddles) in a fermenter? :: Mixes nutrients, oxygen and microorganisms evenly and spreads heat
Why are pH probes used in fermenters? :: To monitor pH and keep it at the optimum for the microorganism's enzymes (acid/alkali added)
How are aseptic conditions kept in a fermenter? :: Sterilise the vessel with steam before use; filter incoming air; sterilise nutrients
What nutrients are supplied in a fermenter? :: A carbon source such as glucose/sugar and a nitrogen source such as amino acids or ammonium salts
Give examples of products made in fermenters. :: Penicillin (antibiotic), insulin (by GM bacteria), mycoprotein
## Fish farming – extra detail
Why are fish kept in cages or tanks in fish farming? :: Restricts movement so less energy is wasted (more goes into growth) and protects them from predators
Why is water quality monitored in fish farms? :: Oxygen levels, temperature and waste must be controlled so fish stay healthy and grow
Why is disease a problem in fish farms and how is it controlled? :: Fish are crowded, so disease spreads quickly; controlled by antibiotics, pesticides or biological control (e.g. wrasse eating sea lice)
Why are fish of different sizes or ages kept separately? :: To stop larger fish eating smaller ones (intraspecific predation)
Why are farmed fish fed a high-protein diet? :: For fast growth – protein is used to make new tissue
Give a disadvantage of fish farming. :: Waste and uneaten food cause water pollution (eutrophication); disease and parasites spread to wild fish; antibiotic overuse
## Selective breeding – extra detail
Describe the steps in selective breeding. :: Choose parents with the desired characteristic; breed them; select the best offspring; breed these together; repeat over many generations
Give a disadvantage of selective breeding. :: Reduces genetic variation (inbreeding), so organisms are more vulnerable to disease and inherited disorders
## Genetic modification – extra detail
What is genetic modification (genetic engineering)? :: Transferring a gene from one organism into another (often a different species)
What are sticky ends? :: Short single-stranded overhanging sections of DNA left after cutting with restriction enzymes, which allow DNA pieces to join by complementary base pairing
Why must the same restriction enzyme be used to cut the gene and the plasmid? :: So they have complementary sticky ends that can pair up
What is recombinant DNA? :: DNA formed by joining DNA from two different sources (e.g. human gene + bacterial plasmid)
Why are bacteria used to make human insulin? :: They reproduce rapidly, so large amounts of insulin are made quickly and cheaply; the insulin is identical to human insulin
Give advantages of GM crops. :: Higher yields; resistance to pests, herbicides or drought; improved nutrition (e.g. Golden Rice with vitamin A)
Give concerns about GM crops. :: Genes may spread to wild plants (e.g. herbicide-resistant weeds); effects on insects and food chains; possible long-term health effects; seed costs for farmers
## Cloning – extra detail
What is a clone? :: A genetically identical copy of an organism
Describe the steps of micropropagation. :: Take small pieces (explants) from the tips of shoots; sterilise; place on sterile agar with nutrients and plant hormones (auxin); grow into plantlets; transfer to compost
Why are aseptic conditions needed in micropropagation? :: Microorganisms would grow on the nutrient agar and kill or compete with the explants
Give advantages of micropropagation. :: Many identical plants quickly, all year round, in a small space; keeps desirable characteristics; disease-free plants
Give a disadvantage of micropropagation. :: No genetic variation – all could be wiped out by one disease; expensive; skilled work
Why is Dolly genetically identical to the udder-cell donor and not the egg donor? :: The nucleus (containing the DNA) came from the udder cell; the egg's own nucleus was removed
Why was an electric shock used in cloning Dolly? :: To stimulate the egg cell (with its new nucleus) to divide and form an embryo
Give concerns about cloning animals. :: Low success rate; cloned animals may have health problems and shorter lives; ethical concerns; reduced genetic variation

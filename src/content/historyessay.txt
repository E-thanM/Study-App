# Medicine c1250–present
## Medieval: why was there so little progress? (Church, ideas, government)
When did the Black Death reach England, and where? :: **June 1348**, at **Melcombe** (Weymouth), Dorset ;; → a sudden crisis that doctors could not explain or treat
Roughly what share of England's population did the Black Death kill (1348–50)? :: About **a third to a half** ;; → shows how helpless medieval medicine was against epidemic disease
Who were the two ancient doctors whose ideas still dominated medicine c1250–1500, and their theories? :: **Hippocrates** (Four Humours) and **Galen** (Theory of Opposites, c.AD 129–216) ;; → medicine relied on books over 1,000 years old, not observation
Why did the Church support Galen's ideas? :: Galen believed the body was designed by **one creator** with a soul – fitted Christian teaching ;; → the Church protected Galen, so his mistakes went unchallenged = **Church held back progress**
How were physicians trained in medieval universities? :: Up to **10 years** reading ancient texts (Galen, Hippocrates, Avicenna) – little practical work; universities run by the Church ;; → training reinforced old ideas instead of testing them
Why was dissection rare in the Middle Ages, and what happened when it was done? :: Church taught the body must stay whole for Judgement Day; dissections that did happen were used to **show Galen was right**, not test him ;; → no new anatomy knowledge
What supernatural explanation did the Church give for the Black Death, and what was the response? :: **Punishment from God for sin** – people prayed, went on pilgrimages and joined **flagellants** (whipping themselves) ;; → religion shaped treatment, so it was ineffective
Which Islamic doctor's book was used alongside Galen in medieval universities? :: **Avicenna (Ibn Sina)** – *The Canon of Medicine* ;; → even new texts repeated Galen, so ideas stayed the same
What did the king do about filth in London in 1349? :: **Edward III** wrote to the Lord Mayor ordering the streets to be cleaned of filth ;; → government DID try to improve public health – a limit to 'no progress'
What was the 1388 Statute of Cambridge? :: First national public health law: **banned throwing waste into ditches and rivers** ;; → government knew dirt linked to disease (miasma) but enforcement was weak
How many hospitals were there in England by about 1500, and who ran most of them? :: About **1,100**, mostly run by the **Church** – care (rest, food, prayer) not cure; most refused infectious patients ;; → the Church HELPED by caring for the sick
How did monasteries show good public health? :: Piped fresh water, separate latrines, washing rules – e.g. **Christ Church Priory, Canterbury** (water system c.1160s) ;; → the Church could improve health where it had money and organisation
Judgement: was the Church the main reason for little progress c1250–1500? :: Strong case – it **controlled training and books** and protected Galen ;; Counter: lack of science/technology (no microscope) and weak government mattered too ;; → the Church was the most important because it shaped what doctors were ALLOWED to think

## Renaissance c1500–1700: how much changed? (individuals, technology, continuity)
When did Vesalius publish *The Fabric of the Human Body*, and what did he show? :: **1543** – about **300 mistakes** in Galen, e.g. the human **jawbone is one bone**, not two ;; → dissection + observation challenged ancient authority
How did printing help Vesalius's ideas spread? :: Printing press (Gutenberg c.1450; **Caxton in England 1476**) – accurate detailed drawings copied exactly ;; → technology spread new ideas fast across Europe
When did Harvey publish his work on the circulation of the blood? :: **1628**, *De Motu Cordis* – blood **circulates**, pumped by the **heart** (Galen said it was made in the liver and used up) ;; → huge discovery in understanding
Why did Harvey's discovery have little impact on treatment? :: Doctors still **bled patients**; blood transfusions failed (no blood groups until **1901**); Harvey said his practice 'fell mightily' after publishing ;; → understanding improved but treatment did not = **continuity**
What did Thomas Sydenham do, and what was he called? :: Observed patients closely and classified diseases by symptoms, e.g. separated **scarlet fever from measles** (*Observationes Medicae*, **1676**); 'the **English Hippocrates**' ;; → new emphasis on observation, but still no cause of disease
When was the Royal Society founded, and why does it matter? :: **1660** (royal charter **1662**); journal *Philosophical Transactions* from **1665** ;; → scientists shared experiments and challenged old ideas = science as a factor
Who described micro-organisms through a microscope in 1676? :: **Antonie van Leeuwenhoek** ('animalcules'); Robert **Hooke**'s *Micrographia* **1665** ;; → saw microbes but nobody linked them to disease for 200 years
How many people died in the Great Plague of London (1665)? :: Bills of Mortality recorded **68,596**; real total about **100,000** (roughly a quarter of London) ;; → still no idea of the real cause
What did authorities do during the 1665 Plague? :: Shut infected houses for **40 days**, red cross and 'Lord have mercy upon us' on doors, **searchers** checked bodies, plague pits, theatres closed, dogs and cats killed ;; → more organised government action than 1348 – a change
How did the Great Plague show continuity with the Black Death? :: Both blamed on **miasma** and God; both treated with prayer, herbs, fleeing; neither knew about fleas/bacteria ;; → ideas about cause barely changed in 300 years
How many people did Charles II 'touch' for scrofula (the King's Evil)? :: About **92,000** (1660–82) ;; → supernatural beliefs about cure continued into the 1680s
What happened to hospitals when Henry VIII closed the monasteries (1536–41)? :: Most monastery hospitals closed; **St Bartholomew's** refounded by **Henry VIII** in **1546**; hospitals started to treat more than just care ;; → Church's role in medicine shrank
Judgement: how far did the Renaissance change medicine? :: **Knowledge** changed (Vesalius 1543, Harvey 1628) but **causes, treatment and prevention** barely did (bleeding, miasma, 1665 shut-ins) ;; → big change in ideas, little change for ordinary patients

## 1700–1900: why did prevention improve? (Jenner, government, public health)
When did Edward Jenner test vaccination, and on whom? :: **1796**, **James Phipps** (aged 8) – cowpox, then smallpox; he didn't catch it ;; → scientific method (experiment) by an individual
When did Jenner publish, and how did the government respond? :: Published **1798**; Parliament gave him **£10,000 in 1802** and **£20,000 in 1807** ;; → government backing for an individual's discovery
How did the government enforce smallpox vaccination? :: **1840** free vaccination for infants (inoculation banned); **1853 compulsory**; **1871** enforced with fines ;; → government action turned a discovery into prevention for everyone
Why did some oppose Jenner? :: He **couldn't explain how** it worked (no germ theory); Church objected to using animal material; inoculators lost income; cartoons (Gillray **1802**) showed people growing cow heads ;; → lack of scientific understanding slowed acceptance
When was smallpox declared wiped out worldwide? :: **1980** (World Health Organization) ;; → long-term significance of Jenner
What did Chadwick's 1842 Report show about life expectancy in Liverpool? :: Gentry **35**, tradesmen **22**, labourers **15** ;; → poor living conditions killed people; pushed government towards public health laws
What was the weakness of the 1848 Public Health Act? :: It was **voluntary** – towns could set up a local Board of Health but most didn't ;; → laissez-faire attitudes held government back
How many died in the 1848–49 cholera epidemic in England and Wales? :: About **50,000** ;; → cholera created pressure for action, but not enough on its own
What did John Snow do in 1854, and what did it prove? :: Mapped cholera deaths around the **Broad Street pump** (over **500 deaths in 10 days**), had the **handle removed** – deaths stopped ;; → cholera spread through **water**, not miasma
Why didn't Snow's discovery change things immediately? :: He couldn't identify the cause (germ theory not until **1861**); many still believed miasma; government ignored him at first ;; → individuals need science AND government to make change
What was the Great Stink, and what did it lead to? :: **Summer 1858** – sewage smell from the Thames closed Parliament; **Joseph Bazalgette** built London's sewers (about **82 miles** of main sewers, **1,100 miles** of street sewers, finished **1865–75**) ;; → government acted when MPs were affected – chance + government
How did the vote change government attitudes to public health? :: **1867 Second Reform Act** gave the vote to working-class men in towns ;; → politicians had to win their support, so they improved living conditions
What did the 1875 Public Health Act do? :: **Compulsory**: councils had to provide clean water, sewers, drainage, rubbish collection and a **Medical Officer of Health** ;; → government became the main factor in prevention
Which other 1875 laws improved health? :: **Artisans' Dwellings Act** (clear slums) and **Sale of Food and Drugs Act** (stop adulteration) ;; → government tackled the causes of poor health, not just disease
Judgement: was government the main reason public health improved in the 1800s? :: Yes – only **1875 compulsory** laws worked across the country ;; But science (Snow 1854, Pasteur 1861) gave the reason, and cholera/Great Stink gave the push ;; → government was decisive, but needed science first

## 1700–1900: why did understanding and surgery improve? (germ theory, Lister, Simpson)
When did Louis Pasteur publish germ theory? :: **1861** – microbes in the air cause decay (swan-necked flask experiment) ;; → first time the real cause of disease was understood
What did Robert Koch identify, and when? :: Bacteria for **anthrax (1876)**, **TB (1882)** and **cholera (1883)**; used dyes to stain bacteria and photographed them ;; → proved specific germs cause specific diseases
How did rivalry between France and Germany help? :: After the **Franco-Prussian War (1870–71)** Pasteur and Koch competed, with government funding and research teams ;; → war/nationalism sped up science
How did Pasteur discover a vaccine for chicken cholera? :: **1879** – an assistant (**Chamberland**) left a culture over the summer; the weakened germs protected the chickens ;; → chance + science
What was the opposition to germ theory in Britain? :: Many doctors kept believing **spontaneous generation** and miasma – e.g. **Henry Bastian** argued against it in the **1870s** ;; → new ideas took decades to accept
What were the three problems of surgery before the 1840s? :: **Pain, infection, bleeding** ;; → operations had to be fast (Robert Liston amputated in under **3 minutes**)
When did James Simpson discover chloroform? :: **November 1847** ;; → solved pain, so surgeons could do longer operations
Who was Hannah Greener, and why does she matter? :: A 15-year-old who **died under chloroform in 1848** ;; → anaesthetics were risky, so some opposed them
How did Queen Victoria help anaesthetics? :: Used chloroform giving birth to **Prince Leopold in 1853** ;; → royal approval made anaesthetics acceptable
Why did death rates sometimes RISE after anaesthetics? :: Longer, deeper operations without germ theory = **more infection** – the 'black period' of surgery ;; → solving one problem made another worse
When did Joseph Lister first use carbolic acid? :: **August 1865** (James Greenlees, 11, compound fracture); published in *The Lancet* **1867** ;; → applied Pasteur's germ theory to surgery
How much did Lister cut the death rate for amputations? :: From about **46% to 15%** (Glasgow, 1864–70) ;; → antiseptics worked – strong evidence for the role of an individual
Why did some surgeons oppose Lister? :: Carbolic **irritated skin** and lungs, **slowed operations**, and some did not accept germ theory ;; → attitudes slowed change
What replaced antiseptic with aseptic surgery? :: Germs kept out completely: **steam sterilisation (1886)**, rubber gloves (**1890**), gowns, masks ;; → building on Lister's work
What did Florence Nightingale achieve at Scutari (1854–56)? :: Death rate fell from about **42% to 2%** after cleaning, ventilation and better food ;; → hygiene in hospitals saves lives
What did Nightingale do after the Crimean War? :: *Notes on Nursing* (**1859**); Nightingale School of Nursing at **St Thomas'** (**1860**); pavilion-style hospital design ;; → nursing became a trained profession
Judgement: was Pasteur's germ theory the most important development 1700–1900? :: It explained **why** Snow, Lister and public health worked and led to Koch and vaccines ;; Counter: Jenner and Simpson succeeded without it; government made prevention reach everyone ;; → most important because later breakthroughs depended on it

## 1900–present: why did treatment improve? (penicillin, science, war, chance)
What was Ehrlich's 'magic bullet'? :: **Salvarsan 606** (**1909**, with **Hata**) – first chemical cure, for **syphilis** ;; → science and teamwork, building on Koch
What was Prontosil? :: First sulphonamide drug, **Gerhard Domagk** (**1932**) – cured his daughter's blood poisoning; British **M&B 693** (**1938**) treated Churchill's pneumonia in **1943** ;; → drug treatments before penicillin
When did Alexander Fleming discover penicillin? :: **September 1928** – mould killed bacteria in a dish left while he was on holiday; published **1929** ;; → chance, but he could not produce it
Who developed penicillin into a usable drug, and when? :: **Howard Florey and Ernst Chain**, Oxford, **1938–41** ;; → teamwork and science turned discovery into treatment
Who was the first patient treated with purified penicillin? :: **Albert Alexander**, a policeman, **February 1941** – recovered, then died when the penicillin **ran out** ;; → it worked but could not be mass-produced in wartime Britain
Why did the USA mass-produce penicillin? :: After **Pearl Harbor (Dec 1941)** the US government paid drug companies to mass-produce it for soldiers ;; → war and government were essential
How many doses of penicillin were ready for D-Day (June 1944)? :: **2.3 million** ;; → war turned penicillin into a mass medicine
Who won the Nobel Prize for penicillin, and when? :: **Fleming, Florey and Chain**, **1945** ;; → shows it was a team achievement, not just Fleming
When was the structure of DNA discovered? :: **1953**, **Watson and Crick** using **Rosalind Franklin**'s X-ray photographs ;; → led to the Human Genome Project
When was the Human Genome Project completed? :: **2003** ;; → helps diagnosis of genetic conditions and targeted treatment
When were X-rays and CT scans developed? :: X-rays **Röntgen 1895**; first CT scan **Godfrey Hounsfield 1971** ;; → technology improved diagnosis
When were lung cancer and smoking first linked? :: **Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill, 1950** ;; → science identified lifestyle causes of disease
Judgement: was chance the main reason penicillin was developed? :: Chance only started it (**1928**); it needed **science** (Florey and Chain), **war** and **US government money** (2.3 million doses by 1944) ;; → war and government were more important than chance

## 1900–present: why did government do more? (Liberal reforms, NHS, lifestyle)
What did the Liberal reforms do for health (1906–11)? :: **Free school meals 1906**, school medical inspections **1907**, old age pensions **1908**, **National Insurance Act 1911** ;; → government took responsibility for health
What did the 1911 National Insurance Act offer workers? :: Sick pay and free GP care; worker paid **4d**, employer **3d**, state **2d** ('9d for 4d') ;; → but only for workers, not wives or children
Why did the Boer War push government to act? :: About **40%** of volunteers were unfit to fight (1899–1902) ;; → war revealed poor health
What were the Beveridge Report's 'five giants' (1942)? :: **Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor, Idleness** – sold over **600,000** copies ;; → public demand for a welfare state
When did the NHS launch, and who led it? :: **5 July 1948**, **Aneurin Bevan** (Labour) – free at the point of use ;; → biggest government action on health ever
How did Bevan win over doctors who opposed the NHS? :: Let consultants keep private patients – 'I **stuffed their mouths with gold**' ;; → government compromise made the NHS possible
When were prescription charges introduced? :: **1952** (1 shilling) – the NHS cost far more than planned ;; → limits of government provision
What was the Great Smog and the government's response? :: **December 1952**, London – about **4,000** deaths at the time (later estimates **12,000**) ;; → **Clean Air Act 1956**
How did government act on smoking? :: TV cigarette adverts banned **1965**; smoking banned in enclosed public places in England **July 2007** ;; → government prevention of lifestyle disease
Which recent lifestyle campaigns has government run? :: **Change4Life (2009)**; sugar tax (Soft Drinks Industry Levy) **April 2018** ;; → government still the key factor in prevention
When was a national diphtheria immunisation campaign started? :: **1942** ;; → government vaccination campaigns (also polio **1956**, MMR **1988**)
Judgement: was government the most important factor in 20th-century medicine? :: Yes for prevention and access (**NHS 1948**, 1956, 2007) ;; But science/technology made the cures (penicillin, DNA) and war sped them up ;; → government most important for ordinary people because it made care free and universal

# Elizabethan England 1558–88
## Why did Elizabeth face problems in 1558? (legitimacy, gender, money, foreign threat)
When did Elizabeth become queen, and how old was she? :: **17 November 1558**, aged **25** ;; → young, unmarried woman in a male-dominated world
Why did Catholics see Elizabeth as illegitimate? :: The Pope never accepted Henry VIII's **divorce** from Catherine of Aragon, so Elizabeth (Anne Boleyn's daughter) was illegitimate ;; → Mary Queen of Scots had a rival claim
How much debt did Elizabeth inherit? :: About **£300,000** – roughly a year's Crown income ;; → couldn't afford war, needed Parliament for taxes
What did England lose in 1558 that showed its weakness? :: **Calais** (to France, January 1558) – England's last land in France ;; → weak, expensive war; France a threat
What was the 'Auld Alliance', and why was it a threat in 1558? :: Alliance of **France and Scotland**; Mary Queen of Scots married the French heir **Francis** in **1558** ;; → threat of invasion from the north
How did Elizabeth reduce the size of the Privy Council? :: To about **19** members (Mary I's had around 50) ;; → stronger control over government
When did William Cecil become Secretary of State? :: **1558**; became **Lord Burghley 1571** and Lord Treasurer **1572** ;; → loyal, skilled adviser who helped her survive
Why did Elizabeth not marry? :: A **foreign** husband could drag England into wars; an **English** one would cause rivalry at court; risk of **death in childbirth** ;; → unsettled succession was a long-term problem
Judgement: what was Elizabeth's biggest problem in 1558? :: **Religion** – a divided country (Catholic north, Protestant exiles returning) plus a Catholic rival claim (**Mary Queen of Scots**) ;; Others: **£300,000** debt, **Calais** lost, being a **woman** ;; → religion was the biggest because it linked to her legitimacy AND to France and Spain

## Why was the religious settlement of 1559 successful? (and its challenges)
What did the 1559 Act of Supremacy do? :: Made Elizabeth **Supreme Governor** (not 'Head') of the Church; clergy had to swear an oath ;; → 'Governor' calmed Catholics and those against a woman leading the Church
What did the 1559 Act of Uniformity do? :: Set churches' appearance and services; **Book of Common Prayer**; church attendance compulsory – fine of **1 shilling a week** for recusants ;; → a 'middle way'
How close was the vote on the Act of Uniformity in the House of Lords? :: Passed by only **3 votes (21–18)** ;; → showed strong Catholic opposition among bishops/nobles
How many parish clergy refused the oath in 1559? :: Only about **250** of around **9,000** ;; → most clergy accepted the settlement = success
How many of Mary I's bishops accepted the settlement? :: All but **one** refused – Elizabeth appointed new Protestant bishops ;; → the Church leadership changed completely
What were the 1559 Royal Injunctions? :: Instructions to clergy, e.g. a Bible in English in every church, clergy to wear **vestments** ;; → compromise satisfied many Catholics, annoyed Puritans
What was the 1566 vestment controversy? :: Archbishop **Parker's Book of Advertisements** enforced clerical dress; **37 London clergy** refused and lost their posts ;; → Puritans challenged the settlement but Elizabeth won
Judgement: why was the settlement successful by 1569? :: Most clergy conformed (only ~250 refused), fines were low, it was a **middle way** ;; But the Catholic north and Puritans resisted ;; → successful in the short term because it was flexible

## Why did Mary Queen of Scots pose such a threat? (1568–87)
When did Mary Queen of Scots flee to England? :: **May 1568**, after defeat at **Langside** ;; → Elizabeth faced a Catholic rival inside England
Why had Mary lost the Scottish throne? :: Husband **Darnley** murdered (**Feb 1567**); she married **Bothwell** (May 1567), suspected killer; forced to abdicate for son **James VI** (July 1567) ;; → scandal, but still a queen
What were the Casket Letters, and what was the result of the 1568–69 inquiry? :: Letters suggesting Mary helped murder Darnley; inquiry found **nothing proven** ;; → Elizabeth kept her prisoner without condemning her
For how long was Mary imprisoned in England? :: About **19 years** (1568–87) ;; → long-lasting focus for Catholic plots
When and where was Mary executed? :: **8 February 1587**, **Fotheringhay Castle** ;; → removed the Catholic alternative but provoked Spain
What evidence condemned Mary in the Babington Plot (1586)? :: Her letter of **July 1586** approving the plan, sent in **beer barrels**, decoded by **Thomas Phelippes** for **Walsingham** ;; → Walsingham's spy network trapped her
Judgement: why was Mary Queen of Scots such a threat? :: Strong **claim to the throne** (Henry VII's great-granddaughter) and **Catholic** – a focus for every plot (**1569, 1571, 1583, 1586**) and for France and Spain ;; → she was a threat because others could use her, which is why plots only ended with her execution in **1587**

## Why were there plots and rebellion against Elizabeth? (1569–86)
When was the Revolt of the Northern Earls, and who led it? :: **November 1569**, Earls of **Northumberland** (Percy) and **Westmorland** (Neville) ;; → Catholic nobles lost influence at court
What did the rebels do at Durham Cathedral? :: Held a **Catholic Mass** (14 Nov 1569) ;; → religion was a key cause
What was the plan of the Revolt of the Northern Earls? :: Free Mary and **marry her to the Duke of Norfolk**; make her heir or queen ;; → Mary was the focus for rebellion
How big was the revolt, and how did it end? :: About **4,600** rebels; collapsed when a royal army of about **14,000** came north; about **450** executed ;; → showed Elizabeth's strength
What did the Pope do in February 1570? :: Issued the Papal Bull ***Regnans in Excelsis*** – **excommunicated** Elizabeth, freed Catholics from obeying her ;; → Catholics became seen as potential traitors
What was the Ridolfi Plot (1571)? :: Banker **Roberto Ridolfi**: Spanish invasion from the Netherlands, Mary to marry Norfolk; **Norfolk executed 1572** ;; → foreign Catholic support for plots
What was the Throckmorton Plot (1583)? :: **Francis Throckmorton**: French invasion led by the **Duke of Guise**, Spanish-funded; Spanish ambassador **Mendoza expelled (1584)** ;; → international Catholic threat
What laws did Parliament pass after the Throckmorton Plot? :: **Bond of Association 1584**; **Act for the Preservation of the Queen's Safety 1585** – anyone plotting on behalf of a claimant would be executed ;; → laid legal ground for Mary's execution
Judgement: was religion the main reason for plots against Elizabeth? :: Religion: Papal Bull **1570**, Mass in Durham ;; But: power/ambition (Norfolk), **Mary's claim**, foreign support (Spain, Guise) ;; → religion linked all of them, but Mary made them possible

## How did Elizabeth deal with the Catholic threat? (Jesuits, Walsingham, laws)
When was the seminary at Douai founded, and by whom? :: **1568**, **William Allen** – trained English Catholic priests ;; → missionary threat from abroad
When did the first Jesuits arrive, and what happened to Edmund Campion? :: **1580**; **Campion executed December 1581** ;; → Elizabeth treated priests as traitors
Who was the first seminary priest executed? :: **Cuthbert Mayne**, **1577** ;; → harsher policy after 1570
How did the recusancy fine change in 1581? :: Raised from 1 shilling a week to **£20 a month** ;; → financial pressure on Catholics
What did the 1585 Act against Jesuits and seminary priests do? :: Made it **treason** to be a Catholic priest in England ;; → priests could be executed
About how many Catholics were executed under Elizabeth? :: About **180**, around **120 of them priests** ;; → harsh, but most Catholics were left alone if loyal
When did Walsingham become Secretary of State? :: **1573** ;; → ran spies, agents provocateurs, codebreakers – uncovered Throckmorton (1583) and Babington (1586)
How were the Babington plotters punished? :: **14** executed (September 1586), Babington hanged, drawn and quartered ;; → plots crushed by surveillance
Judgement: was Walsingham's spy network the main reason Elizabeth defeated the Catholic threat? :: Walsingham uncovered **Throckmorton (1583)** and **Babington (1586)** ;; But **laws** (£20 fine 1581, treason 1585) and most Catholics staying **loyal** mattered too ;; → spies stopped the plots, but loyalty of ordinary Catholics meant there was never a mass rising after **1569**

## Why did England go to war with Spain? (1568–88)
What happened with the Spanish treasure ships in 1568? :: Elizabeth seized Genoese gold (about **£85,000**) meant to pay Spain's army in the Netherlands ;; → trade embargo between England and Spain until 1573
Why did the Duke of Alba's arrival in the Netherlands worry Elizabeth? :: **1567**, about **10,000** troops; Council of Troubles ('**Council of Blood**') executed over **1,000** ;; → a Catholic army across the Channel
What was the attack at San Juan de Ulúa (1568)? :: Spanish attacked **Hawkins and Drake** in Mexico – most English ships lost ;; → personal grudge and push for privateering
How much treasure did Drake bring back from his circumnavigation (1577–80)? :: About **£400,000** – more than the Crown's annual income; knighted on the **Golden Hind** in **April 1581** ;; → Elizabeth rewarded attacks on Spain
What was the Treaty of Nonsuch (August 1585)? :: Elizabeth sent about **7,400 troops** under **Leicester** to help the Dutch rebels ;; → open war with Spain
What did Drake do at Cadiz in April 1587? :: Destroyed about **30 ships** and barrel-making supplies – '**singeing the King of Spain's beard**' ;; → delayed the Armada by a year
Judgement: what was the main reason for war with Spain? :: Religion (Catholic Spain v Protestant England), trade/**privateers** (£400,000), the **Netherlands** (Nonsuch 1585), Mary's execution **1587** ;; → Netherlands was decisive: Nonsuch made war official

## Why was the Spanish Armada defeated? (1588)
How big was the Armada? :: About **130 ships**, about **30,000 men** (around **18,000 soldiers**) ;; → huge invasion force
Who led the Armada, and why was he a weakness? :: **Duke of Medina Sidonia** – little naval experience, got seasick ;; → Spanish leadership was poor
What was the Armada's plan with the Duke of Parma? :: Meet Parma's army of about **27,000** in the Netherlands and escort it across ;; → no deep-water port and no way to communicate quickly = flaw in plan
What happened on the night of 28 July 1588? :: **8 English fireships** sent into the Armada at **Calais** – the Spanish cut anchors and scattered ;; → broke the crescent formation
When was the Battle of Gravelines, and what happened? :: **29 July 1588** (English dates) – English ships fought at close range; Spain lost around **5 ships** ;; → Armada blown into the North Sea
How did English ships and guns give them an advantage? :: Faster **race-built galleons** (Hawkins' design); long-range **culverins**; Spanish shot often badly cast ;; → English tactics and technology
How many Armada ships returned to Spain? :: About **65** of 130 – storms wrecked many off **Scotland and Ireland** ;; → weather (the 'Protestant wind') mattered as much as battle
When was Elizabeth's Tilbury speech? :: **August 1588** – 'I have the **heart and stomach of a king**' ;; → propaganda, boosted her image
Judgement: was English naval skill the main reason the Armada failed? :: English: **fireships**, **Gravelines**, **culverins** ;; Spanish mistakes: **Medina Sidonia**, **Parma** plan ;; Weather: about half the ships lost on the way home ;; → Spanish planning was the root cause – the plan to meet Parma could never work

## Why did poverty and vagabondage increase? (and how did government respond?)
How much did England's population grow under Elizabeth? :: From about **3 million (1550s)** to about **4 million (1600)** ;; → more demand, higher prices, lower wages
Why did prices and real wages make poverty worse? :: Prices rose faster than wages – **real wages fell** sharply; **bad harvests** (e.g. **1586–87**) ;; → even working people slipped into poverty
How did enclosure cause poverty? :: Landowners fenced off land for **sheep**, which needed fewer workers ;; → rural unemployment, people moved to towns
How did the decline of the cloth trade cause poverty? :: Trade disrupted with **Antwerp** (1560s–70s, Spanish war) ;; → unemployed cloth workers
Why had the dissolution of the monasteries made poverty worse? :: Monasteries had given charity to the poor; closed **1536–41** ;; → no safety net
What did the 1572 Vagabonds Act do? :: Beggars **whipped and bored through the right ear**; but each parish had to raise a compulsory **poor rate** for the deserving poor ;; → harsh on idle poor, help for 'impotent' poor
What did the 1576 Poor Relief Act do? :: Towns had to provide **raw materials** (wool, flax) so the able-bodied poor could work; **houses of correction** for those who refused ;; → recognised that some poor wanted work
Which towns took action before national laws? :: **Norwich** – census of the poor (**1570**), begging banned, poor given work; London's **Bridewell** house of correction ;; → local government led the way
Judgement: why did government attitudes to the poor change? :: Fear of **disorder**; growing numbers; town schemes worked (**Norwich 1570**); **Puritan** ideas of charity ;; → fear of rebellion was the strongest reason

## Why did the Age of Exploration happen, and why did Roanoke fail?
What technology helped English exploration? :: Galleons (larger, better armed); navigation: **astrolabe**, **Mercator's map (1569)** ;; → longer voyages possible
When did John Hawkins begin the English slave trade? :: **1562** – 'triangular trade' between England, West Africa and the Americas ;; → profit drove exploration
How many of Drake's 5 ships completed the voyage around the world (1577–80)? :: Only **1 – the Golden Hind** ;; → dangerous, but hugely profitable
When was Virginia named, and after whom? :: **1584**, after Elizabeth, the '**Virgin Queen**' ;; → exploration linked to national pride
What happened to the first Roanoke colony (1585)? :: About **108** men under **Ralph Lane**; poor relations with natives (Chief **Wingina** killed **1586**); Drake took them home **1586** ;; → soldiers not farmers, short of supplies
What happened to the second Roanoke colony (1587)? :: About **117** settlers under **John White** (granddaughter **Virginia Dare**); found abandoned in **1590** – 'CROATOAN' carved on a post ;; → the 'Lost Colony'
Why did the Armada contribute to Roanoke's failure? :: Ships were kept in England in **1588** to fight Spain – no supplies reached the colony ;; → war at home ended the colony
Judgement: what was the main reason Roanoke failed? :: Poor relations with **Native Americans**; lack of **supplies**; **Armada (1588)**; bad planning (arrived too late to plant) ;; → lack of supplies was key – every other problem made it worse

# Weimar and Nazi Germany 1918–39
## Why was the Weimar Republic weak 1918–23? (Versailles, constitution, uprisings)
When did the Kaiser abdicate, and when was the armistice? :: **9 November 1918**; armistice **11 November 1918** ;; → new republic linked to defeat ('November criminals')
When was the Treaty of Versailles signed? :: **28 June 1919** – a '**Diktat**' (dictated peace) ;; → republic blamed for the treaty
What did Article 231 say? :: **War guilt** – Germany accepted blame for the war ;; → humiliation, justified reparations
How much were reparations, set in 1921? :: **£6.6 billion** (132 billion gold marks) ;; → crippled the economy, led to the Ruhr crisis
What were Versailles' military terms? :: Army **100,000**, no tanks, no air force, **6 battleships**, no submarines, **Rhineland demilitarised** ;; → Germany felt defenceless
How much land and resources did Germany lose? :: **13% of land**, about **12% of population**, **48% of iron** and **16% of coal** production, all **colonies** ;; → economic damage + resentment
What was the 'stab in the back' myth? :: Claim the army was **not defeated** but **betrayed by politicians** who signed the armistice ('**November criminals**') ;; → the right attacked the republic
How did proportional representation weaken Weimar? :: One seat per **60,000 votes**; many small parties; about **20 coalition governments 1919–33** ;; → weak, short-lived governments
What was Article 48? :: President could rule by **emergency decree** without the Reichstag ;; → later used by Brüning and Hindenburg, undermining democracy
What was the Spartacist Revolt? :: **January 1919**, communists in Berlin; crushed by the **Freikorps**; **Rosa Luxemburg** and **Karl Liebknecht** killed ;; → threat from the left; republic relied on right-wing ex-soldiers
What was the Kapp Putsch? :: **March 1920**, **Wolfgang Kapp** and about **5,000 Freikorps** took Berlin; army refused to fire; defeated by a **general strike** ;; → army couldn't be trusted
How many political murders were there 1919–22, and who committed them? :: **354 by the right**, **22 by the left**; right-wing killers got light sentences ;; → judges were hostile to the republic
Which ministers were assassinated? :: **Erzberger (1921)**, who signed the armistice; **Rathenau (June 1922)**, foreign minister ;; → right-wing violence against the republic
When did France and Belgium occupy the Ruhr? :: **January 1923**, after Germany missed reparations payments; workers used **passive resistance** ;; → government printed money to pay strikers
How bad did hyperinflation get by November 1923? :: A loaf of bread cost **250 marks (Jan 1923)** → **200,000 million marks (Nov 1923)**; **$1 = 4.2 trillion marks** ;; → middle-class savings wiped out
Who gained from hyperinflation? :: People with **debts or mortgages**, businessmen (e.g. **Hugo Stinnes**) ;; → losers (savers, pensioners) blamed the republic
Judgement: was Versailles the main reason Weimar was weak 1919–23? :: Versailles caused **reparations → Ruhr → hyperinflation** and the 'November criminals' label ;; But the **constitution** (PR, Article 48) and violence from left and right ;; → Versailles most important because it led to the other crises

## How far did Weimar recover 1924–29? (Stresemann – 'golden age'?)
When was Stresemann chancellor, and then what? :: Chancellor **August–November 1923**; foreign minister **1923–29** (died **3 October 1929**) ;; → stability came from one man
What did Stresemann do about passive resistance and the currency? :: Ended passive resistance (**Sept 1923**); **Rentenmark** (**Nov 1923**), then the **Reichsmark (1924)** ;; → ended hyperinflation
What was the Dawes Plan (1924)? :: US loan of **800 million marks**; reparations reduced to **£50 million a year** for 5 years; French left the Ruhr (1925) ;; → recovery, but on **borrowed American money**
What was the Young Plan (1929)? :: Reparations cut from £6.6 billion to about **£2 billion**, paid over **59 years** ;; → good for Germany, but attacked by Hitler and the right
What was the Locarno Pact (1925)? :: Germany accepted its **western borders** with France and Belgium ;; → treated as an equal again
When did Germany join the League of Nations? :: **1926** (permanent member of the Council) ;; → back as a great power
What was the Kellogg–Briand Pact (1928)? :: Germany and about **60** other countries agreed not to use war to settle disputes ;; → improved international standing
Which prize did Stresemann win? :: **Nobel Peace Prize, 1926** (shared with Briand of France) ;; → respected abroad
By 1928, how did industrial production compare with 1913? :: It was **higher than before the war** ;; → real economic recovery
How did Weimar improve housing and welfare? :: Over **2 million** new homes (**1924–31**); **unemployment insurance (1927)** for about **17 million** workers ;; → better living standards
What new rights did women gain in Weimar? :: **Vote at 20** (1919); in **1919** about **10%** of Reichstag members were women ;; → more equality
Give three examples of Weimar culture. :: **Bauhaus** (Gropius, **1919**); film ***Metropolis*** (**1927**); artist **Otto Dix** ;; → creative freedom – but conservatives saw it as immoral
What % of the vote did the Nazis get in May 1928? :: Only **2.6%** (12 seats) ;; → extremists weak when the economy was good
What evidence shows the recovery was fragile? :: Depended on **short-term US loans**; farmers in difficulty from **1927**; unemployment still over **1 million**; coalitions still fell ;; → 'dancing on a volcano' (Stresemann)
Judgement: was 1924–29 a 'golden age'? :: Yes: stable currency, Dawes, Locarno, 2 million homes, Nazis 2.6% ;; No: built on **US loans**, farmers struggling, political system unchanged ;; → recovery was real but not secure – it collapsed in 1929

## Why did Hitler become chancellor in January 1933?
When did Hitler join the German Workers' Party (DAP)? :: **September 1919** ;; → became leader **July 1921**
When was the Munich Putsch, and what happened? :: **8–9 November 1923**, Bürgerbräukeller; **16 Nazis** killed; Hitler sentenced to **5 years**, served **9 months** ;; → used his trial for publicity; wrote *Mein Kampf*
What did Hitler decide after the Munich Putsch? :: Win power **legally through elections**; Bamberg Conference **1926** confirmed his control ;; → reorganised party for the long term
How did Nazi party membership grow 1925–28? :: About **27,000** (1925) to over **100,000** (1928) ;; → organisation ready for the Depression
How high did unemployment rise in the Depression? :: About **6 million** by **1932** ;; → desperate voters turned to extremes
How did Brüning's use of Article 48 grow? :: **5 decrees in 1930**, **44 in 1931**, **66 in 1932** ;; → democracy already failing before Hitler
How many Reichstag seats did the Nazis win in each election 1928–33? :: **12 (1928)**, **107 (Sept 1930)**, **230 (July 1932)**, **196 (Nov 1932)**, **288 (March 1933)** ;; → Depression made them the largest party
What share of the vote did the Nazis win in July 1932? :: **37.3%** – the **largest party**, but never a majority ;; → couldn't take power by votes alone
How many seats did the Communists (KPD) win in November 1932? :: **100** ;; → fear of communism pushed elites and middle classes towards Hitler
What was the result of the 1932 presidential election? :: Hindenburg **53%**, Hitler **36.8%** (second round) ;; → Hitler was a national figure
How big was the SA by 1932? :: About **400,000** ;; → violence against opponents, image of order
How did Goebbels' propaganda campaign use technology in 1932? :: Hitler **flew to rallies in over 20 cities** in a week; radio, films, posters ;; → modern campaigning reached millions
What was the 'backstairs intrigue'? :: **Papen** (sacked Dec 1932) persuaded **Hindenburg** to appoint Hitler chancellor with Papen as vice-chancellor ;; → Hitler given power by elites, not elected
How many Nazis were in Hitler's first cabinet? :: Only **3 of 12** (Hitler, **Frick**, **Göring**) ;; → elites thought they could control him
What did Papen say about controlling Hitler? :: '**We've hired him**' / 'In two months we'll have pushed Hitler so far into a corner that he'll squeak' ;; → elites underestimated him
Why does the November 1932 election show Hitler was not certain to win power? :: Nazis **lost 2 million votes** and **34 seats**; party short of money ;; → without the intrigue, support may have kept falling
When did industrialists petition Hindenburg to appoint Hitler? :: **November 1932** ;; → business backed Hitler against communism
When was Hitler appointed chancellor? :: **30 January 1933** ;; → legally, by Hindenburg
Judgement: was the Depression the main reason Hitler became chancellor? :: Depression made the Nazis the largest party (**12 → 230 seats**) ;; But votes were falling (**Nov 1932**), and **Papen's intrigue** put him in power ;; → the Depression made it possible, but backstairs intrigue made it happen

## How did the Nazis remove opposition and create a dictatorship 1933–34?
When was the Reichstag fire, and who was blamed? :: **27 February 1933**; **Marinus van der Lubbe**, a Dutch communist ;; → excuse to attack communists
What did the Decree for the Protection of the People and the State do? :: **28 February 1933** – suspended civil rights; about **4,000** communists arrested ;; → legal cover for terror
What was the result of the March 1933 election? :: Nazis **43.9%**, **288 seats** – still not a majority ;; → needed the Enabling Act
What was the vote on the Enabling Act? :: **23 March 1933**, **444 to 94** – only the **SPD** voted against; KPD deputies banned ;; → Hitler could make laws without the Reichstag for 4 years
When were trade unions banned? :: **2 May 1933** – replaced by the **German Labour Front (DAF)** ;; → no workers' opposition
When were all other political parties banned? :: **14 July 1933** ;; → Germany a one-party state
When was the Night of the Long Knives, and who was killed? :: **30 June 1934** – **Ernst Röhm** and SA leaders, plus **Schleicher** and **Strasser**; about **400** killed ;; → removed rivals, won army support
How big was the SA in 1934? :: About **3 million** ;; → a threat to the army and to Hitler
What happened when Hindenburg died? :: **2 August 1934** – Hitler became **Führer**; army swore a personal **oath of loyalty** ;; → total power
Judgement: was the Reichstag fire the most important step to dictatorship? :: It led to the **Decree** and arrests ;; But the **Enabling Act** gave legal power and the **Night of the Long Knives** won the army ;; → Enabling Act most important – it made everything else legal

## How did the Nazis control Germany? (terror v propaganda, 1933–39)
When was Dachau opened? :: **March 1933** – the first concentration camp ;; → terror from the very start
Who led the SS, and how big was it by 1939? :: **Heinrich Himmler**; about **240,000** members ;; → main instrument of terror
How did the Gestapo find opponents? :: Relied heavily on **informers** and denunciations from ordinary Germans ;; → people policed each other – fear was everywhere
How many people were in 'protective custody' in 1939? :: About **162,734** ;; → huge scale of political imprisonment
How did the number of crimes punishable by death change? :: From **3 (1933)** to **46 (1943)** ;; → courts became a tool of terror
When was the People's Court set up? :: **1934** – for treason cases; judges had to be Nazi loyalists ;; → no fair trials
When was Goebbels' Ministry of Propaganda created? :: **March 1933** ;; → controlled newspapers, radio, film, art
How widespread were radios by 1939? :: About **70%** of households had a radio (cheap **People's Receiver**) ;; → Hitler's speeches reached most Germans
When was the Nazi book burning? :: **10 May 1933** – books by Jewish, communist and 'un-German' writers burned ;; → censorship
What did the 1936 Berlin Olympics show? :: Germany won the **most medals** (**33 gold**) ;; → propaganda for 'Aryan' strength
Which film glorified the 1934 Nuremberg Rally? :: ***Triumph of the Will*** (**Leni Riefenstahl**, **1935**) ;; → cult of the Führer
Judgement: was terror more important than propaganda in controlling Germany? :: Terror: Dachau 1933, **162,734** in custody, 3 → 46 capital crimes ;; Propaganda: **70%** radios, Olympics ;; → terror stopped opposition; propaganda won support – terror was the foundation

## How did the Nazis deal with the Churches and opposition?
What was the Concordat (July 1933)? :: Agreement with the **Pope**: Church kept schools, Nazis kept out of Church affairs if it kept out of politics ;; → broken by the Nazis within months
What did Pope Pius XI publish in 1937? :: ***Mit brennender Sorge*** ('With Burning Concern'), read in Catholic churches, criticising the Nazis ;; → Catholic opposition
What was the Reich Church? :: Nazi-controlled Protestant Church under **Reich Bishop Ludwig Müller** (**1933**) ;; → Nazis tried to take over religion
When was the Confessing Church formed, and by whom? :: **1934**, **Martin Niemöller** and **Dietrich Bonhoeffer** ;; → Protestant opposition
What happened to Niemöller? :: Arrested **1937**, held in concentration camps until **1945** ;; → opposition punished harshly
How many Confessing Church pastors were arrested in 1937? :: About **800** ;; → shows scale of Church opposition and persecution
What were the Edelweiss Pirates and the Swing Youth? :: Youth groups who rejected the Hitler Youth – Pirates fought HJ patrols; Swing Youth listened to **jazz** ;; → limits of Nazi control over young people
Judgement: how far was there opposition to the Nazis 1933–39? :: Some: Churches (**800** pastors, *Mit brennender Sorge*), youth groups ;; But no mass opposition – parties and unions banned **1933** ;; → opposition was small and mostly about protecting their own freedom, not overthrowing Hitler

## How successful were Nazi policies towards women?
What were the 'three Ks'? :: ***Kinder, Küche, Kirche*** – children, kitchen, church ;; → women's role was at home
What did the Law for the Encouragement of Marriage (1933) offer? :: A loan of **1,000 marks**; **25%** cancelled for each child ;; → encouraged marriage and children
What was the Mother's Cross (1938)? :: **Bronze** for 4–5 children, **silver** 6–7, **gold** 8+ ;; → rewarded large families
How did the birth rate change 1933–39? :: About **970,000** births (1933) to about **1.4 million** (1939) ;; → success, but the rate was also recovering from the Depression
What was Lebensborn (1935)? :: **SS** programme for '**racially pure**' births, including **unmarried mothers** ;; → racial aims came before traditional values
How did Nazi policy on women's work change after 1937? :: Labour shortages from **rearmament** meant **more women in work by 1939** than in 1933 ;; → policy failed / was reversed by economic needs
Judgement: were Nazi policies towards women successful? :: Success: births up (**970,000 → 1.4m**), marriage loans ;; Failure: more women in work by 1939; many women didn't change ;; → partly successful – the economy forced a U-turn

## How successful were Nazi policies towards young people?
How did Hitler Youth membership grow? :: About **2.3 million (1933)** to over **7 million (1939)** ;; → most young people involved
When did the Hitler Youth become effectively compulsory? :: Law of **December 1936**; compulsory from **1939** ;; → shows not everyone joined willingly
What was the girls' organisation? :: **League of German Maidens (BDM)** ;; → prepared girls to be wives and mothers
How much of the school timetable was PE? :: **15%** ;; → fitness for soldiers and mothers
How many teachers joined the Nazi Teachers' League by 1937? :: **97%** ;; → control of education
What were the Adolf Hitler Schools (1937)? :: Elite schools for **12–18-year-olds** training future **Nazi leaders** ;; → indoctrination of the best students
How did the Nazis change the curriculum? :: New subjects like **race studies** (eugenics); history glorified Germany; maths questions on military and racial topics ;; → indoctrination
Judgement: were Nazi policies towards young people successful? :: Success: **7 million** in HJ, **97%** teachers ;; Limits: compulsory by law (1936, 1939), Edelweiss Pirates, Swing Youth ;; → largely successful, but needed force to keep everyone involved

## How did Nazi policies affect workers and living standards?
How much did unemployment fall 1933–39? :: From about **6 million** to about **0.3 million** (1939) ;; → biggest Nazi success claim
What was the Reich Labour Service (RAD)? :: **Compulsory** 6 months' work for men aged 18–25 from **1935** ;; → cut unemployment with low-paid work
How much autobahn had been built by 1938? :: About **3,000 km** ;; → public works created jobs
What was 'invisible unemployment'? :: **Women** and **Jews** removed from statistics; **conscription (1935)** took men into the army ;; → real unemployment higher than the figures
How big was the army by 1939? :: About **1.4 million** (from 100,000 under Versailles) ;; → rearmament created jobs
How did average working hours change 1933–39? :: About **43** to **49 hours a week** ;; → workers worked longer for frozen wages
What was the Volkswagen scheme? :: Workers paid **5 marks a week**; about **336,000** saved – **no one received a car** ;; → propaganda more than benefit
What were Strength Through Joy (KdF) and Beauty of Labour (SdA)? :: **KdF**: cheap holidays, **cruises**, concerts; **SdA**: better workplaces (**canteens, showers**) ;; → won support, kept workers loyal
Judgement: did Nazi economic policy improve living standards? :: Better: jobs (**6m → 0.3m**), KdF ;; Worse: hours **43 → 49**, wages frozen, no trade unions, VW cars never delivered ;; → more jobs but not better lives

## How did persecution of minorities increase 1933–39?
When was the boycott of Jewish shops? :: **1 April 1933** ;; → persecution began immediately
What did the April 1933 Civil Service law do? :: Jews **dismissed from the civil service** (**7 April 1933**) ;; → removed from public life
How many Jews lived in Germany in 1933? :: About **500,000** – under **1%** of the population ;; → a small minority used as scapegoats
When were the Nuremberg Laws, and what did they do? :: **15 September 1935** – Jews lost **citizenship**; marriage and relationships between Jews and non-Jews **banned** ;; → legal persecution
When was Kristallnacht, and what happened? :: **9–10 November 1938** – about **100 Jews killed**, about **7,500** shops and hundreds of synagogues destroyed, about **30,000** sent to camps; Jews fined **1 billion marks** ;; → from discrimination to violence
What happened to Jewish children after Kristallnacht? :: Banned from German schools (**November 1938**) ;; → removal from society
How did the Nazis treat disabled people? :: **Sterilisation Law, July 1933** – about **400,000** sterilised by 1945; 'euthanasia' (T4) from **1939** ;; → racial ideas about 'unfit' Germans
How were Roma (Gypsies) persecuted? :: **1938** decree 'Combating the Gypsy Plague'; registered, many sent to camps ;; → racial persecution beyond Jews
Judgement: why did persecution increase in 1938? :: Nazi power secure, economy recovering, no foreign pressure (Olympics over), **Kristallnacht** shows radicalisation ;; → once Hitler was secure, he no longer needed to hide his aims

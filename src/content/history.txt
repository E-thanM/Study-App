# Paper 1 Medicine in Britain c1250–present
## c1250–1500 Medieval
What were the main supernatural ideas about the cause of disease in medieval England? :: Disease was a punishment from God for sin; astrology (alignment of the planets) also blamed
What was the Theory of the Four Humours? :: Hippocrates' idea that the body has four humours – blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile – and illness comes from an imbalance
How did Galen develop the Four Humours? :: Theory of Opposites – treat an excess humour with its opposite (e.g. a cold with something hot like pepper); his books were the basis of medical training
Why did Galen's ideas last so long? :: The Church supported them (fitted the idea of a creator); dissection was rare; physicians trained on his books; challenging them was discouraged
What was miasma? :: The belief that bad air (from rotting matter) caused disease
Give medieval treatments based on the Four Humours. :: Bloodletting, purging (vomiting/laxatives), Theory of Opposites
Give religious and supernatural treatments in medieval England. :: Prayer, pilgrimage, flagellation, buying charms, astrology-based treatment
Name three medieval prevention methods. :: Regimen sanitatis (healthy lifestyle), keeping clean/bathing, carrying posies against miasma, prayer and fasting
Who were the main medical practitioners in medieval England? :: Physicians (university-trained, expensive), apothecaries (made remedies), barber surgeons (bloodletting, minor surgery), women/wise women at home
What was the role of hospitals in medieval England? :: Run by the Church; mainly care (rest, food, prayer) for the poor/elderly rather than treatment; often refused infectious patients
When did the Black Death arrive in England and what did people think caused it? :: 1348 ;; God's punishment, miasma, alignment of planets, imbalance of humours
How did people try to treat and prevent the Black Death? :: Prayer, flagellants, bloodletting, carrying posies, cleaning streets (Edward III ordered London cleaned), avoiding the sick
What role did the Church play in medieval medicine? :: Controlled universities and training; promoted Galen; ran hospitals; encouraged prayer and religious explanations; restricted dissection
## c1500–c1700 Renaissance
How did ideas about the cause of disease change in the Renaissance? :: Less belief in religious causes; Four Humours declined among scientists; Sydenham's careful observation; miasma still believed; new ideas spread through printing
What did Vesalius do? :: Published 'The Fabric of the Human Body' (1543) based on dissection; corrected Galen's mistakes (e.g. jawbone, no holes in septum); encouraged dissection
What did William Harvey discover? :: Circulation of the blood (1628) – the heart is a pump and blood circulates (veins and arteries)
Why did Harvey's discovery have little impact on treatment at the time? :: Didn't help cure disease; doctors kept using bloodletting; took time to be accepted
What did Thomas Sydenham contribute? :: 'English Hippocrates' – observed symptoms closely, treated disease based on observation not books; classified diseases
How did the printing press help medicine? :: Ideas (e.g. Vesalius) spread quickly and accurately; harder for the Church to control knowledge
What was the Royal Society (1660)? :: A group of scientists who shared ideas and experiments; published journal (Philosophical Transactions)
How did people respond to the Great Plague of 1665? :: Prayer, posies, smoking tobacco, killing cats and dogs; council: quarantine (houses shut with red cross), bodies buried at night in pits, searchers, banning public gatherings
How did hospitals change in the Renaissance? :: After the Dissolution of the Monasteries (1536) many closed; some reopened run by councils/charities; more treatment of sick (e.g. St Bartholomew's)
## c1700–c1900 18th & 19th centuries
What was Louis Pasteur's germ theory (1861)? :: Microbes in the air cause decay (and disease) – disproved spontaneous generation
What did Robert Koch contribute? :: Identified specific bacteria causing specific diseases (anthrax 1876, TB 1882, cholera 1883); staining techniques, agar plates
How did Edward Jenner develop vaccination (1796)? :: Noticed milkmaids who caught cowpox didn't catch smallpox; infected James Phipps with cowpox then smallpox – he didn't get ill
Why was there opposition to Jenner's vaccination? :: Religious objections; people didn't understand how it worked; inoculators lost money; fear of cow material; government forcing it (compulsory 1853)
What three problems faced surgeons before c1850? :: Pain, infection, bleeding
How did James Simpson help surgery? :: Discovered chloroform as an anaesthetic (1847)
What were problems with chloroform? :: Difficult to get the dose right – could kill (Hannah Greener 1848); patients unconscious so surgeons attempted longer operations, raising infection
How did Joseph Lister help surgery? :: Used carbolic acid spray on wounds, instruments and dressings (antiseptic surgery, 1867) – reduced death rates
Why was there opposition to Lister? :: Carbolic acid irritated skin; slowed operations; some didn't accept germ theory; early results inconsistent
What did Florence Nightingale do for hospitals? :: Improved hygiene and nursing (Crimea, Scutari); 'Notes on Nursing' (1859); pavilion-style hospitals; trained nurses at St Thomas' (1860)
What did John Snow discover in 1854? :: Mapped cholera deaths in Broad Street, London; removed the pump handle – showed cholera spread through contaminated water
What was the 1875 Public Health Act? :: Made councils provide clean water, sewers, drainage, street lighting, and appoint a Medical Officer of Health – compulsory
What was the Great Stink? :: Summer 1858 – smell of sewage in the Thames was so bad Parliament acted; Joseph Bazalgette built London's sewer system
## c1900–present Modern
What causes of disease were understood in the 20th century? :: Genetic and lifestyle factors (smoking, diet, alcohol) as well as germs; improved diagnosis (blood tests, X-rays, MRI, CT scans)
Who discovered the structure of DNA and when? :: Watson and Crick (with Rosalind Franklin's X-ray images), 1953
What was the Human Genome Project? :: 1990–2003 – mapped all human genes; helps identify genetic conditions
What was the first magic bullet? :: Salvarsan 606 (Paul Ehrlich's team, Hata) – cured syphilis, 1909
How did Alexander Fleming discover penicillin? :: 1928 – noticed mould killing bacteria on a Petri dish left out; published but couldn't produce it in quantity
How did Florey and Chain develop penicillin? :: 1940s – purified it, tested on mice and a policeman (Albert Alexander); US government funded mass production during WW2
What were the key features of the NHS (1948)? :: Free at the point of use, funded by taxation, for everyone; set up by Aneurin Bevan; based on the Beveridge Report (1942)
How did the government tackle lifestyle diseases? :: Campaigns (Change4Life, anti-smoking), smoking ban in public places (2007), banning cigarette advertising, Clean Air Acts (1956, 1968), sugar tax
What new treatments developed in the 20th century? :: Antibiotics, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, transplants, keyhole surgery, dialysis, insulin, vaccines
Case study: lung cancer – government action? :: Ban on TV cigarette adverts (1965), health warnings on packets, smoking ban in public places 2007, plain packaging, higher taxes
Name key factors in the development of medicine. :: Individuals, science and technology, institutions (Church, government), war, attitudes in society, chance
## c1250–1500 Medieval
Who was Hippocrates and what did he contribute? :: Ancient Greek doctor (c460–370 BC) ;; Developed the **Four Humours** theory and stressed **clinical observation** – observing and recording symptoms
Who was Galen? :: Greek doctor working in Rome (2nd century AD) ;; Built on Hippocrates; dissected animals (pigs, apes); wrote many books that dominated medicine for over 1,000 years
Name the four humours and the organ/element/season each was linked to. :: Blood (heart, air, spring); phlegm (brain, water, winter); yellow bile (liver, fire, summer); black bile (spleen, earth, autumn)
Why was the Four Humours theory described as a rational explanation? :: It looked for a **natural** cause in the body rather than a supernatural one, based on observation (e.g. phlegm with a cold)
How did astrology affect medieval diagnosis? :: Physicians used **almanacs** and the **zodiac man** to link parts of the body and illnesses to star signs and planetary positions
How did a medieval physician diagnose illness? :: Studied the patient's **urine** (colour, smell, taste) using a urine chart; checked the pulse; consulted astrological charts (vademecum)
Why did people believe disease was a punishment from God? :: The Church taught that God controlled everything; sin brought illness; the Bible described plagues as punishment – so the cure was prayer and repentance
What was the link between miasma and medieval prevention? :: People cleaned streets, removed rubbish and carried or burned sweet-smelling herbs to **purify the air**
What was the regimen sanitatis? :: A set of instructions from a physician for a healthy lifestyle – diet, exercise, sleep, bathing – to keep the humours balanced (mainly for the rich)
What were medieval remedies made from? :: Herbs, spices, minerals and animal parts, mixed by **apothecaries** or made at home by women (e.g. theriaca, a complex mixture)
What was purging? :: Getting rid of excess humours by making the patient vomit (emetics) or empty their bowels (laxatives)
How was bloodletting carried out in medieval England? :: By barber surgeons or physicians – cutting a vein, using leeches or cupping – timing guided by astrology
How were physicians trained in medieval England? :: At university (e.g. Oxford, Cambridge) for about 7 years, reading the books of Hippocrates and Galen; little practical experience
How were barber surgeons trained and what did they do? :: Apprenticeship, not university ;; Bloodletting, pulling teeth, lancing boils, setting bones, simple amputations – cheaper than physicians
Who provided most medical care in medieval England? :: Women in the home (mothers, wise women) using herbal remedies – care was cheapest and most common at home
Which types of hospital existed in 13th-century England? :: Hospitals run by monks/nuns for the poor and elderly (care, food, rest, prayer); **lazar houses** (leper hospitals) outside towns; almshouses
Why did most medieval hospitals not admit people with infectious diseases? :: Feared spreading infection (miasma) and they focused on care and the soul, not cure
What new approach to hospital care developed in the 13th century? :: More hospitals founded by the Church and wealthy donors, some specialising (e.g. leper hospitals); emphasis on cleanliness, rest and prayer for the soul
C: The Black Death reached England in {{c1::1348}} and killed about {{c2::a third}} of the population by 1349.
What actually caused the Black Death? :: Bacteria (Yersinia pestis) spread by fleas on rats (bubonic) and by coughing (pneumonic) – unknown at the time
What were the two forms of the Black Death? :: **Bubonic** – buboes (swellings) in armpits and groin, spread by flea bites ;; **Pneumonic** – attacked the lungs, spread through the air by coughing
How did local and national government respond to the Black Death? :: Edward III ordered the Lord Mayor of London to clean the streets (1349); Gloucester tried to shut its gates against Bristol; quarantine was very limited
Who were the flagellants? :: People who whipped themselves in public to show God they were sorry for their sins and so stop the plague
Why did medieval people fail to stop the Black Death? :: They did not know about germs or fleas; treatments (prayer, bloodletting, posies) did nothing; little effective government action
Give an example of continuity between the medieval period and today: :: Herbal remedies; looking at urine/blood to diagnose; hospitals providing care; belief that a healthy lifestyle prevents illness
Explain why the Church was a barrier to medical progress in medieval England. :: Controlled universities so training was based on Galen ;; Discouraged dissection and challenges to accepted ideas ;; Taught disease was God's punishment, so prayer was the main treatment
Explain why the Church helped medicine in medieval England. :: Ran hospitals that cared for the sick and poor ;; Monks copied and preserved ancient medical texts ;; Encouraged cleanliness in monasteries (fresh water, drains)
## c1500–c1700 Renaissance
What was the Renaissance? :: A 'rebirth' of interest in learning from ancient Greece and Rome (c1500s) – encouraged questioning, observation and experiment
C: Andreas Vesalius published 'The Fabric of the Human Body' in {{c1::1543}}.
Why was Vesalius' book so influential? :: Detailed, accurate drawings (by skilled artists) from human dissection; printed so copies spread widely; showed Galen could be wrong
Why did Vesalius' work not improve treatment straight away? :: Knowing anatomy did not explain what caused disease or how to cure it; many doctors still trusted Galen
C: William Harvey published his discovery of the circulation of the blood in {{c1::1628}}.
How did Harvey prove the circulation of the blood? :: Dissected live cold-blooded animals (heart beats slowly), showed valves in veins only let blood flow one way, calculated the heart pumped far more blood than the body could make
What did Harvey's discovery disprove? :: Galen's idea that blood was made in the liver and used up by the body, and that it passed through holes in the septum
What was the long-term significance of Harvey's work? :: Made later blood transfusions and heart surgery possible; encouraged experiment rather than trusting books
How did Sydenham challenge medical authority? :: Said doctors should learn from observing patients, not old books; argued diseases were separate entities with their own symptoms; distinguished scarlet fever from measles
Why did Sydenham's ideas have limited impact on understanding cause? :: He still did not know what caused disease and he still believed in miasma and the humours
C: The Royal Society was founded in {{c1::1660}} and received a royal charter from {{c2::Charles II}} in 1662.
How did the Royal Society help the spread of ideas? :: Members discussed experiments and published 'Philosophical Transactions' (from 1665), so findings spread across Britain and Europe
When was printing introduced to England and by whom? :: 1476 by William Caxton (Gutenberg developed the printing press c1440s)
Which ideas about the cause of disease continued in the Renaissance? :: Miasma, the Four Humours (among ordinary doctors) and religious explanations all continued
What new treatments arrived in the Renaissance? :: New ingredients from the Americas (e.g. quinine/cinchona bark for fevers, tobacco); chemical remedies
What was 'touching for the King's Evil'? :: People with scrofula believed the monarch's touch would cure them – Charles II touched thousands
What was transference? :: Belief a disease could be passed to an object or animal (e.g. rubbing a wart with a cut onion)
How did medical training improve in the Renaissance? :: More dissection (barber surgeons allowed bodies of executed criminals), Vesalius' book studied, more emphasis on anatomy
What were pest houses? :: Houses (often outside towns) where plague victims were isolated
Name a hospital refounded after the Dissolution of the Monasteries. :: St Bartholomew's (1546) and St Thomas' (1551) in London – funded by charity/the city
C: The Great Plague hit London in {{c1::1665}}, killing about {{c2::100 000}} people.
What did the Lord Mayor's plague orders (1665) require? :: Infected houses shut up (with a red cross and 'Lord have mercy upon us'), watchmen guarded them, searchers recorded deaths, bodies buried at night, public gatherings banned, dogs and cats killed
What were Bills of Mortality? :: Weekly lists of deaths and causes in London parishes – used to track the plague
How were the responses to the Black Death (1348) and the Great Plague (1665) similar? :: Both blamed God and miasma; both used prayer, posies and avoiding the sick; neither understood the real cause (fleas)
How were the responses to the Black Death and the Great Plague different? :: In 1665 there was much more organised local government action – quarantine of houses, searchers, Bills of Mortality, banning gatherings, plague pits
Explain why there was little change in the treatment of disease c1500–c1700. :: Causes still unknown so treatments still based on humours (bloodletting, purging) ;; New discoveries (Vesalius, Harvey) were about anatomy, not cures ;; Doctors and patients were conservative and kept familiar methods
Explain why the printing press was important for medicine in the Renaissance. :: Accurate copies of books (e.g. Vesalius) spread quickly ;; The Church lost control of ideas so new ideas could be challenged ;; Doctors across Europe could build on each other's work

## c1700–c1900 18th & 19th centuries
What was spontaneous generation? :: The belief that microbes were created by decaying matter – Pasteur showed microbes cause decay instead
How did Pasteur prove germ theory? :: Swan-necked flask experiment: sterilised liquid stayed fresh when air-borne microbes could not reach it but decayed when exposed
Why was Pasteur working on microbes? :: He was employed to find out why wine, beer and vinegar went sour – brewing and silk industries
Which vaccines did Pasteur develop after germ theory? :: Chicken cholera (1879, partly by chance), anthrax (1881), rabies (1885)
Why was the rivalry between Pasteur and Koch important? :: National rivalry after the Franco-Prussian War (1870–71) pushed both teams to work fast; governments funded them
What techniques did Koch develop? :: Solid culture medium (agar jelly in Petri dishes) to grow bacteria; dyes to stain bacteria; photographing bacteria through microscopes
Why was germ theory slow to change medicine in Britain? :: Many doctors still believed in spontaneous generation/miasma; germs were hard to see; it did not immediately give cures
Explain the impact of germ theory on medicine in Britain. :: Showed specific microbes cause disease so led to vaccines and later magic bullets ;; Encouraged antiseptic and aseptic surgery (Lister) ;; Gave the government reasons to clean up water and sewers (1875 Act)
What was inoculation (variolation)? :: Deliberately giving a mild dose of smallpox to give immunity – introduced to Britain by Lady Mary Wortley Montagu (1721); risky
What was smallpox? :: A deadly infectious disease causing fever and pustules; killed or scarred many people in the 18th century
C: Edward Jenner tested vaccination on James Phipps in {{c1::1796}} and published his findings in {{c2::1798}}.
How did the government support Jenner's vaccination? :: Gave Jenner £10 000 (1802) and £20 000 (1807); vaccination free 1840 (inoculation banned); compulsory 1853; enforced from 1871
Why was Jenner's vaccination significant? :: First vaccine; led to compulsory vaccination; smallpox eventually eradicated worldwide (1980) ;; But Jenner could not explain how it worked
What was laissez-faire? :: The belief that the government should not interfere in people's lives – a reason public health was neglected before 1875
What was the 1848 Public Health Act? :: Set up a Board of Health; councils could improve water and sewers but it was voluntary – few did
What was the Crimean War significance for Florence Nightingale? :: 1854–56 ;; She cleaned the hospital at Scutari; death rate fell from about 42% to 2%; she became a national hero
How did Nightingale change nursing? :: Set up a nurses' training school at St Thomas' Hospital (1860); nursing became a respectable trained profession for women
How did Nightingale change hospital design? :: Promoted the pavilion plan – separate wards, good ventilation, more windows, easy to clean ('Notes on Hospitals', 1859)
Why did Nightingale's ideas about cause limit her? :: She believed in miasma, not germ theory – but her focus on cleanliness and fresh air still reduced infection
How did hospitals improve in the 19th century? :: Cleaner, trained nurses, pavilion wards, more specialist and cottage hospitals, antiseptic then aseptic surgery
Who first used nitrous oxide and ether as anaesthetics? :: Humphry Davy found nitrous oxide (laughing gas) eased pain (1799); ether first used in Britain by Robert Liston (1846)
What were the problems with ether? :: Irritated the lungs causing coughing; highly flammable; heavy to carry
C: James Simpson discovered chloroform in {{c1::1847}}; Queen Victoria used it in childbirth in {{c2::1853}}.
Why was Queen Victoria's use of chloroform important? :: Made anaesthetics respectable and widely accepted
What was the 'black period' of surgery? :: c1850–70 – anaesthetics allowed longer, deeper operations but without antiseptics infection deaths rose
How did Lister get the idea for carbolic acid? :: Read about Pasteur's germ theory; knew carbolic acid was used to treat sewage in Carlisle
What was the impact of Lister's antiseptics? :: At Glasgow Royal Infirmary, amputation death rate fell from about 46% to 15% (1864–70)
What was aseptic surgery? :: Preventing germs getting into the wound at all – sterilised instruments (steam), surgical gowns, masks, rubber gloves (from 1890s), clean theatres
Explain why surgery improved in the 19th century. :: Anaesthetics (chloroform 1847) solved pain ;; Germ theory and Lister's carbolic spray (1867) reduced infection ;; Aseptic methods in the 1890s made surgery safer still
C: Cholera epidemics hit Britain in 1831–32, 1848–49, {{c1::1853–54}} and 1866.
How was cholera spread and what were its symptoms? :: Spread by water contaminated with sewage ;; Severe diarrhoea and vomiting, dehydration, death often within days
What did people believe caused cholera before Snow? :: Miasma – bad air from rotting rubbish and sewage
C: John Snow's book 'On the Mode of Communication of Cholera' was first published in {{c1::1849}}.
What evidence did Snow use in the Broad Street outbreak of 1854? :: Mapped about 500 deaths in 10 days around the pump; brewery workers (drank beer) and workhouse residents (own well) were not affected; a woman in Hampstead who had Broad Street water sent died
What was later found at the Broad Street pump? :: A cesspit near the well was leaking – a baby's nappy had been washed into it (found by Rev. Henry Whitehead)
Why was Snow's discovery not accepted immediately? :: Most people and the Board of Health still believed miasma; he could not identify the germ (germ theory came 1861)
What was the significance of Snow's work? :: Proved cholera spread through water; encouraged clean water and sewer improvements; an example of using statistics and mapping
How many miles of sewers did Bazalgette build? :: About 1 300 miles (main sewers completed in the 1860s–70s) – cholera never returned to London after 1866
What other public health laws were passed in 1875? :: Artisans' Dwellings Act (slum clearance) and Sale of Food and Drugs Act (stopped harmful additives)
Explain why the government took more action on public health by 1875. :: Working men got the vote (1867) so politicians had to respond ;; Scientific evidence (Snow, germ theory) showed dirt caused disease ;; Events like the Great Stink (1858) and cholera epidemics forced action
## c1900–present Modern
What lifestyle factors cause disease? :: Smoking, poor diet and obesity, alcohol, lack of exercise, sunbathing (skin cancer), drug use
Name some genetic conditions identified through genetic research. :: Cystic fibrosis, Huntington's disease, sickle-cell anaemia; faulty BRCA genes increase breast cancer risk
How has the Human Genome Project helped medicine? :: Genetic screening and tests; tailored treatments; research into gene therapy for inherited diseases
How have blood tests improved diagnosis? :: Detect infections, diabetes (blood sugar), cholesterol, cancers and genetic conditions quickly and accurately
Name modern scanning technologies used in diagnosis. :: X-rays, CT scans (1970s), MRI scans, ultrasound, PET scans, endoscopes
How have monitors improved treatment? :: Continually measure heart rate, blood pressure, blood sugar and oxygen levels, alerting staff to problems
What was Paul Ehrlich's idea of a 'magic bullet'? :: A chemical that would kill a specific germ without harming the body
What was the second magic bullet? :: Prontosil – a sulphonamide discovered by Gerhard Domagk (1932), cured blood poisoning (he used it on his daughter)
What was M&B 693? :: A sulphonamide drug (1938) that treated pneumonia – used to treat Churchill in 1943
C: Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin in {{c1::1928}} at St Mary's Hospital, London.
Why could Fleming not develop penicillin himself? :: He could not purify or mass-produce it and lacked funding; he did not test it on infected animals
Who helped Florey and Chain produce enough penicillin to test? :: Norman Heatley – devised ways to grow and extract it (using bedpans, milk churns)
What happened to Albert Alexander (1941)? :: Policeman with a severe infection; improved on penicillin but died when supplies ran out
Why was the Second World War important for penicillin? :: US government and drug companies funded mass production – enough for Allied troops by D-Day (1944)
C: Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the Nobel Prize in {{c1::1945}}.
What is the problem of antibiotic resistance? :: Overuse of antibiotics lets resistant bacteria such as MRSA develop
Explain the roles of chance, individuals and war in the development of penicillin. :: Chance – mould landed on Fleming's dish (1928) ;; Individuals – Florey and Chain purified and tested it ;; War – US government funding enabled mass production for the troops
Name high-tech surgical developments since 1945. :: Transplants (first UK kidney transplant 1960), hip replacements, keyhole surgery, robotic surgery, pacemakers, microsurgery
What were the 'five giants' in the Beveridge Report (1942)? :: Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor, Idleness
C: The NHS was launched on {{c1::5 July 1948}} by Health Minister {{c2::Aneurin Bevan}}.
Why did many doctors oppose the NHS? :: Feared losing income and independence – Bevan won the BMA over by letting consultants keep private patients
What was the impact of the NHS? :: Free GPs, hospitals and treatment for all; millions treated who couldn't afford it; improved health and life expectancy ;; Costs grew – prescription charges (1952)
Give examples of government mass vaccination campaigns. :: Diphtheria (1942), polio (1950s), MMR (1988), HPV (2008), flu jabs for the elderly, COVID-19 (2020–21)
What was the Great Smog of 1952 and what did it lead to? :: Thick smog in London killed about 4 000 people (later estimates higher) ;; Led to the Clean Air Act 1956
How has science and technology improved treatment since 1900? :: Mass-produced drugs, scanners, dialysis machines, radiotherapy, keyhole surgery, robotics, genetic tests
C: Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill showed the link between smoking and lung cancer in {{c1::1950}}.
How is lung cancer diagnosed in the 21st century? :: CT and PET-CT scans, chest X-rays, bronchoscopy (camera into lungs), biopsy, genetic testing of tumours
How is lung cancer treated in the 21st century? :: Surgery (removing part or all of a lung), radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted drugs matched to tumour genes, immunotherapy, lung transplants
C: Smoking in enclosed public places was banned in England in {{c1::2007}}; plain cigarette packaging followed in {{c2::2016}}.
What other government actions have reduced smoking? :: Warnings on packets (1971), age of purchase raised to 18 (2007), display ban in shops, ban on smoking in cars with children (2015), NHS stop-smoking services
Why is lung cancer still hard to treat? :: Symptoms often appear late so it is diagnosed late; it can spread quickly
How did modern government action on public health differ from 19th-century action? :: Much more involved – NHS, vaccination, lifestyle campaigns, laws on smoking and food – instead of laissez-faire
Explain why medical treatment improved so much after 1900. :: Science and technology – magic bullets, antibiotics, scanners ;; Government – NHS (1948) made treatment free and funded research ;; War – drove development (penicillin, plastic surgery, blood transfusion)

## Factors and themes across the period
What is meant by 'continuity' and 'change' in medicine? :: Continuity – things stay the same (e.g. herbal remedies, miasma ideas) ;; Change – things become different (e.g. germ theory replaces miasma)
What is a turning point? :: A moment of significant change after which things are different – e.g. germ theory (1861), NHS (1948)
Give an example of individuals as a factor. :: Vesalius, Harvey, Jenner, Pasteur, Snow, Fleming – e.g. Snow proved cholera came from water
Give an example of the Church as a factor. :: Medieval – promoted Galen and prayer, ran hospitals ;; its influence declined after the Reformation
Give an example of government as a factor. :: 1875 Public Health Act; compulsory vaccination (1853); NHS (1948); smoking ban (2007)
Give an example of science and technology as a factor. :: Printing press, microscopes (germ theory), X-rays, scanners, mass production of penicillin
Give an example of attitudes in society as a factor. :: Opposition to Jenner and Lister; laissez-faire; public acceptance of anaesthetics after Queen Victoria
How did ideas about the cause of disease change from 1250 to the present? :: God/astrology/humours/miasma (medieval) → some questioning (Renaissance) → germ theory (1861) → genetics and lifestyle (20th century)
How did prevention change from 1250 to the present? :: Prayer, regimen sanitatis, posies → quarantine (1665) → vaccination (1796) and public health (1875) → mass vaccination and lifestyle campaigns
How did care and treatment change from 1250 to the present? :: Home and Church care, humours-based treatment → trained nurses and hospitals (19th century) → magic bullets, antibiotics, NHS, high-tech surgery

# Paper 1 The British sector of the Western Front 1914–18
What was the Ypres Salient? :: A bulge in the British line around Ypres, Belgium; surrounded on three sides – heavy fighting (1st Ypres 1914, 2nd 1915 gas, 3rd Passchendaele 1917)
Name four key battles on the Western Front in the specification. :: Ypres (1914–17), Arras (1917), the Somme (1916), Cambrai (1917)
What was significant about the tunnels at Arras? :: Old quarries extended by New Zealand tunnellers to hold 24 000 men, with a hospital (700 beds) underground
What was the Battle of Cambrai (1917) significant for? :: First large-scale use of tanks (around 450); also the first use of stored blood (blood bank) to treat casualties
Describe the trench system. :: Front-line, support and reserve trenches connected by communication trenches; zig-zag shape; no man's land; barbed wire
What conditions in the trenches caused illness? :: Mud, rats, lice, cold, standing water, poor food, unburied bodies
What was trench foot and how was it prevented? :: Feet rotting from standing in cold water; prevented by dry socks, whale oil, duckboards
What was trench fever? :: Flu-like illness spread by lice; treated by rest; prevented by delousing stations
What effect did gas attacks have and how was it dealt with? :: Chlorine (1915, Ypres), phosgene, mustard gas (1917) – choking, blisters, blindness ;; Gas masks/respirators
What was shell shock? :: Psychological trauma (now PTSD) from constant shelling; around 80 000 cases; treated with rest, sometimes at Craiglockhart
Why were head injuries common and what helped? :: Shrapnel in trenches; Brodie steel helmet introduced 1915–16
Describe the chain of evacuation. :: Stretcher bearers → Regimental Aid Post → Dressing Station (Advanced and Main) → Casualty Clearing Station → Base Hospital → home (Blighty)
What was the role of the RAMC? :: Royal Army Medical Corps – doctors, stretcher bearers and orderlies providing care
What was the role of FANY? :: First Aid Nursing Yeomanry – women who drove ambulances, ran mobile baths and canteens
What were Casualty Clearing Stations? :: Field hospitals a few miles behind the line; carried out surgery; triage of wounded
What was the Thomas splint? :: Kept a broken leg rigid and straight; reduced deaths from compound femur fractures from 80% to 20% (1916)
How did X-rays help on the Western Front? :: Located bullets and shrapnel; mobile X-ray units; Marie Curie's 'petites Curies'
How was blood transfusion developed during WW1? :: Sodium citrate prevented clotting (1915); blood stored – first blood bank at Cambrai (1917)
How was the problem of infection tackled? :: Carrel–Dakin method (sterile salt solution through tubes); debridement (cutting away infected tissue); amputation
What was plastic surgery's development in WW1? :: Harold Gillies treated facial injuries with skin grafts at Queen's Hospital, Sidcup
What sources would you use to investigate injuries on the Western Front? :: Army records, hospital records, diaries/letters, photographs, RAMC records, official histories
## Context: battles, trenches and terrain
C: The First Battle of Ypres was in {{c1::1914}}; the Second, in {{c2::1915}}, saw the first use of chlorine gas.
C: The Battle of the Somme began on {{c1::1 July 1916}}; the British suffered about {{c2::57 000}} casualties on the first day.
Why was the Battle of the Somme significant for medical services? :: Huge numbers of casualties overwhelmed the RAMC; the evacuation system and CCSs had to expand; showed the need for better organisation
C: The Third Battle of Ypres, also known as {{c1::Passchendaele}}, was fought July–November {{c2::1917}} in deep mud.
C: The Battle of Arras began in {{c1::April 1917}}; the Battle of Cambrai was in {{c2::November 1917}}.
What happened at Hill 60 near Ypres? :: British tunnellers dug under German lines and exploded mines in April 1915; Hill 60 was mined again in the Battle of Messines (June 1917)
Why were mines and tunnels used on the Western Front? :: To destroy enemy trenches from below and to move troops safely underground (e.g. Arras)
What were the problems of the terrain in Flanders (Ypres)? :: Flat, low-lying land with a high water table and clay soil – trenches flooded, mud everywhere, hard to drain
How was the terrain at the Somme and Arras different from Ypres? :: Chalk – easier to dig deep trenches and tunnels and better drained (though still muddy in rain)
Why did the British often have worse trenches than the Germans? :: The Germans had retreated to higher, drier ground and built deeper, concrete-lined defences; the British were often in low, wet ground
What is a fire step? :: A ledge in the front-line trench that soldiers stood on to fire over the parapet
What are the parapet and parados? :: Parapet – sandbagged front wall of the trench ;; Parados – raised back wall protecting from shells bursting behind
Why were trenches dug in a zig-zag pattern (traverses)? :: To stop shell blasts and shrapnel travelling along the trench and stop an enemy who got in firing down the whole line
What were dugouts and saps? :: Dugouts – shelters cut into the trench wall ;; Saps – short trenches dug forward into no man's land for listening posts
What were communication trenches used for? :: Linking front, support and reserve lines – moving men, supplies and the wounded
## Conditions, illnesses and injuries
What were the main causes of wounds on the Western Front? :: Artillery shells and shrapnel caused most wounds; also rifle and machine-gun bullets, gas, and grenades
Why did shrapnel wounds become easily infected? :: Shrapnel carried bits of muddy clothing and soil (fertilised with manure) deep into the wound
What was gas gangrene? :: Infection from bacteria in the soil that produced gas in the wound; flesh died and amputation was often needed
Why were wounds from bullets often less deadly than shell wounds? :: Bullets made smaller clean wounds; shells caused multiple, ragged wounds packed with dirt
How did gas masks develop during the war? :: Cloth pads soaked in water or urine (1915) → PH helmets (hood with chemicals) → small box respirator (1916)
Why was mustard gas especially feared? :: Odourless, worked within hours, caused internal and external blisters, blindness and could stay on the ground for days
How deadly was gas compared to other weapons? :: Relatively few gas casualties died (a small percentage), but it caused panic, long-term lung damage and blindness
What was the treatment for gas victims? :: Removed from the area, eyes and skin washed, rest; little could be done for lung damage
How was shell shock treated? :: Rest away from the front, sometimes counselling (Craiglockhart for officers) or harsher methods like electric shocks; some soldiers were executed for cowardice
## RAMC, FANY and the chain of evacuation
How much did the RAMC grow during the war? :: From about 9 000 men in 1914 to over 110 000 by 1918
What did stretcher bearers do and what made their job hard? :: Carried wounded from no man's land to the Aid Post – often under fire, in mud (up to 4–6 men per stretcher), only at night in some battles
What was the Regimental Aid Post (RAP)? :: Close to the front line (about 200 m); run by the regimental medical officer; first aid – no surgery
What was a Field Ambulance? :: A mobile RAMC unit (not a vehicle) that ran the Advanced and Main Dressing Stations
What happened at Dressing Stations? :: Advanced Dressing Station – wounds dressed, basic care ;; Main Dressing Station – more treatment before moving to a CCS
How far behind the front were Casualty Clearing Stations and why were they located there? :: About 7–12 miles; near railways or roads so the wounded could be moved on; far enough to be out of artillery range
What were the triage categories used at CCSs? :: Walking wounded; those needing urgent hospital treatment; those unlikely to survive (made comfortable)
What were Base Hospitals? :: Large hospitals near the French coast (e.g. Étaples, Boulogne, Rouen) for longer-term treatment and surgery before men were sent to Britain
How were the wounded transported along the chain of evacuation? :: Stretchers, wheeled stretchers, horse-drawn and motor ambulances, ambulance trains, canal barges, hospital ships to Britain
What were the problems of transport and communication on the Western Front? :: Mud and shell-holes, roads destroyed by shelling, shortage of motor ambulances early on, telephone lines cut so messages relied on runners
Who were the VADs and QAIMNS? :: VAD – Voluntary Aid Detachment (volunteer nurses) ;; QAIMNS – Queen Alexandra's Imperial Military Nursing Service (trained army nurses)
When was FANY founded and what was significant about its role? :: Founded 1907 ;; Initially worked for the French and Belgians; from 1916 the first women officially to drive ambulances for the British Army
## Medical developments and context
How was the understanding of infection in the early 1900s? :: Germ theory accepted; aseptic surgery normal in British hospitals – but impossible to keep conditions sterile at the front
C: X-rays were discovered by {{c1::Wilhelm Röntgen}} in {{c2::1895}}.
What were the limitations of X-ray machines on the Western Front? :: Tubes overheated after about an hour so had to be rested; couldn't show cloth or some fragments; mobile units were few
C: Blood groups were discovered by {{c1::Karl Landsteiner}} in {{c2::1901}}, making transfusions safer.
Why was blood transfusion difficult before the war? :: Blood clotted quickly so it had to go directly from donor to patient
Who developed a portable blood transfusion kit? :: Geoffrey Keynes – could be used near the front line
How did scientists extend blood storage? :: Sodium citrate stopped clotting; adding glucose (Rous and Turner, 1916) let blood be stored for about four weeks
Who set up the first blood bank at Cambrai (1917)? :: Captain Oswald Robertson (US Army) – stored blood in ice boxes before the battle
What was debridement (wound excision)? :: Cutting away damaged and infected tissue around a wound to prevent gas gangrene
What was the Carrel–Dakin method? :: Sterile salt solution (Dakin's solution) passed through tubes into the wound to kill bacteria – used widely by 1917
Why was the Thomas splint introduced? :: Broken thigh bones (femur) caused heavy bleeding and death when moved ;; The splint kept the leg still during transport
What was the tube pedicle? :: Gillies' technique (1917) – a tube of skin kept attached to the body to keep a blood supply while a graft grew over a facial wound
When did Queen's Hospital, Sidcup open and what was it known for? :: 1917 ;; Facial reconstruction and plastic surgery under Harold Gillies – over 11 000 operations
What was the significance of the Western Front for medicine? :: Huge numbers of casualties led to new techniques – blood storage, Thomas splint, X-rays, plastic surgery – many later used in civilian hospitals
Explain why the treatment of wounds improved on the Western Front. :: Huge numbers of casualties meant surgeons gained experience fast ;; New methods – debridement, Carrel–Dakin, Thomas splint, X-rays ;; Better organisation – chain of evacuation and CCSs doing surgery quickly
## Sources and following up a source
What is a national source? :: A source covering the whole country or army – e.g. army records, government reports, national newspapers, official histories
What is a local source? :: A source about a specific place or person – e.g. hospital records, local newspapers, letters, diaries, war memorials
What are the strengths and weaknesses of army records? :: Strengths – official, detailed figures on wounds, deaths, units ;; Weaknesses – many destroyed in WW2 bombing; don't show personal experience
What are the strengths and weaknesses of national newspapers from 1914–18? :: Strengths – show what the public was told ;; Weaknesses – heavily censored, used for propaganda, rarely showed the full horror
What are the strengths and weaknesses of personal accounts (letters, diaries)? :: Strengths – first-hand experience, emotions ;; Weaknesses – one person's view; letters were censored; memoirs written later may be inaccurate
What are the strengths and weaknesses of photographs from the front? :: Strengths – show conditions and treatment ;; Weaknesses – could be staged, censored or used for propaganda; only one moment
What are the strengths and weaknesses of hospital records? :: Strengths – detailed on injuries and treatments ;; Weaknesses – may be incomplete; cover only one hospital
Which medical journals published articles on wartime medicine? :: The Lancet and the British Medical Journal (BMJ) – useful for new treatments but written for experts
What is the provenance of a source? :: Its nature (type), origin (who made it, when) and purpose (why it was made)
What is a good question to ask when following up a source? :: A specific, answerable question linked to the enquiry – e.g. 'How many soldiers were treated for trench foot in 1915?'
Give an example of a source to follow up the number of men treated at a CCS. :: CCS admission records or RAMC war diaries – they give official figures

# Paper 2 Early Elizabethan England 1558–88
When did Elizabeth become queen and what problems did she face? :: 1558 ;; legitimacy (Henry VIII's marriage to Anne Boleyn), gender, marriage/succession, religion, financial weakness (£300 000 debt), threats from France, Spain, Scotland
What was Elizabeth's Privy Council? :: Her main advisers (about 19), e.g. William Cecil (Secretary of State), Francis Walsingham (spymaster), Robert Dudley
What was the role of Parliament under Elizabeth? :: Granted taxes and passed laws; only met when called; Elizabeth controlled the agenda and banned discussion of marriage/religion
What were the problems with religion in 1558? :: Divided between Catholics (north, nobles) and Protestants (south-east, London); Puritans wanted more reform
What was the Religious Settlement (1559)? :: Act of Supremacy (Elizabeth Supreme Governor), Act of Uniformity (Book of Common Prayer, church attendance, fine 1 shilling), Royal Injunctions
Why was the Settlement called the 'middle way'? :: Protestant in doctrine (English Bible, services) but kept some Catholic features (bishops, vestments, crucifixes)
What were the challenges from the Puritans? :: Objected to crucifixes and vestments (Vestment Controversy 1566); some wanted prophesyings; Archbishop Grindal suspended for supporting them
Why was Mary Queen of Scots a threat? :: Catholic; legitimate claim to the English throne (great-granddaughter of Henry VII); focus for Catholic plots; arrived in England 1568
What was the Revolt of the Northern Earls (1569)? :: Northumberland and Westmorland rebelled to make Mary Queen of Scots queen and restore Catholicism; marched south, heard mass at Durham; collapsed; about 450 executed
What was the papal bull of 1570? :: Pope Pius V excommunicated Elizabeth and told Catholics not to obey her – made Catholics seem traitors
Describe the Ridolfi Plot (1571). :: Florentine banker planned a Spanish invasion to put Mary on the throne, marry Norfolk; discovered; Norfolk executed 1572
Describe the Throckmorton Plot (1583). :: French-backed invasion (Duke of Guise) to free Mary; Throckmorton arrested and tortured by Walsingham's agents; Spanish ambassador expelled
Describe the Babington Plot (1586). :: Plan to murder Elizabeth and free Mary; letters smuggled in beer barrels intercepted by Walsingham; Mary approved the plot – executed February 1587
Why was Mary Queen of Scots executed in 1587? :: Evidence of involvement in the Babington Plot; ended Catholic hope of a Catholic successor
Name reasons for conflict with Spain. :: Religion (Philip II Catholic), trade with New World, English privateers (Drake), English support for Dutch Protestant rebels, execution of Mary
What was the Treaty of Nonsuch (1585)? :: Elizabeth agreed to send an army (led by Robert Dudley) to help the Dutch Protestants against Spain – effectively war
What was the 'singeing of the King of Spain's beard' (1587)? :: Drake attacked Cadiz, destroying around 30 ships and supplies – delayed the Armada by a year
Why did the Spanish Armada fail in 1588? :: English fireships at Calais broke the crescent formation; Battle of Gravelines; better English ships and cannon; bad weather ('Protestant wind'); poor Spanish planning (Medina Sidonia inexperienced, couldn't meet Parma's army)
Describe education in Elizabethan England. :: Petty schools, grammar schools (boys, Latin), universities (Oxford, Cambridge); rich educated at home; girls mainly at home
Describe leisure activities in Elizabethan England. :: Theatre (purpose-built playhouses such as The Theatre, 1576, and the Rose, 1587 – the Globe came later, in 1599), blood sports (bear-baiting, cockfighting), hunting, football, music, dancing
What caused poverty and vagabondage under Elizabeth? :: Population growth, rising prices (inflation), enclosure of land, bad harvests, decline of the cloth trade, dissolution of the monasteries
How did attitudes to the poor change and what laws were passed? :: Distinguished deserving and undeserving poor ;; Vagabonds Act 1572 (whipping, ear bored), Poor Relief Act 1576 (work for able-bodied), poor rate collected locally
What were the reasons for exploration under Elizabeth? :: New technology (astrolabe, galleons), trade opportunities, rivalry with Spain, wealth from privateering
Describe Drake's circumnavigation (1577–80). :: First English circumnavigation of the globe in the Golden Hind; captured Spanish treasure worth £400 000; knighted 1581
Why did Raleigh's attempt to colonise Virginia (1585) fail? :: Poor relations with Native Americans, lack of supplies, bad weather, poor planning; Roanoke 'lost colony' 1587

## Key topic 1: Queen, government and religion 1558–69
C: Elizabeth became queen on {{c1::17 November 1558}} aged {{c2::25}} and was crowned in January 1559.
Describe the structure of Elizabethan society. :: Countryside: nobility → gentry → yeomen → tenant farmers → labourers → vagrants ;; Towns: merchants → professionals → craftsmen → unskilled labourers
What was the Court? :: The nobles, advisers and servants who surrounded the monarch – a place to gain influence and patronage
What were Lord Lieutenants? :: Nobles appointed to govern each county for the Queen and raise the local militia
What were Justices of the Peace (JPs)? :: Unpaid local gentry who kept law and order, tried minor crimes and (later) ran the Poor Laws
What was the royal prerogative? :: The monarch's personal powers – e.g. declaring war, making peace, calling and dismissing Parliament, granting titles and monopolies
Who was William Cecil? :: Elizabeth's most trusted adviser – Secretary of State from 1558, Lord Burghley from 1571, Lord Treasurer from 1572
Why was Elizabeth's legitimacy a problem? :: Catholics did not recognise Henry VIII's divorce from Catherine of Aragon, so saw his marriage to Anne Boleyn as invalid and Elizabeth as illegitimate
Why was Elizabeth's gender a problem? :: People believed women were weaker and should obey men; a female ruler was expected to marry and let her husband guide her
Why was Elizabeth's marriage a problem? :: Marrying a foreign prince could make England subordinate; marrying an English noble could cause rivalry; but without an heir the succession was uncertain
Name Elizabeth's main suitors. :: Philip II of Spain, Archduke Charles of Austria, Robert Dudley, the Duke of Anjou (French, 1579–81)
Why was Elizabeth unable to marry Robert Dudley? :: Scandal after his wife Amy Robsart died in suspicious circumstances (1560); rivals at court (Cecil) opposed it
What were Elizabeth's character and strengths? :: Well educated (spoke several languages), intelligent, charismatic, decisive when needed, skilled at managing advisers and public image
What was the French threat in 1558? :: France was England's enemy; Mary Queen of Scots married the French heir (1558) and had a claim to England; French troops in Scotland (Auld Alliance)
What did England lose in 1558 and how was it confirmed? :: Calais – lost to France in 1558 under Mary I; Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis (1559) confirmed it
What was the Treaty of Edinburgh (1560)? :: French and English troops left Scotland – reduced the French threat via Scotland
How did Elizabeth deal with her financial weakness? :: Cut spending, sold Crown lands, avoided expensive wars, granted monopolies – reluctant to raise taxes
Where were Catholics and Protestants strongest in 1558? :: Catholics – north and west (e.g. Lancashire), many nobles ;; Protestants – London, the south-east, East Anglia
Why was the Religious Settlement hard to pass? :: Catholic bishops in the House of Lords opposed it – the Act of Uniformity passed by only three votes
What were the Royal Injunctions (1559)? :: Instructions to clergy: teach royal supremacy, preach regularly, an English Bible in every church, no pilgrimages, a licence needed to preach, all clergy take the Oath of Supremacy
How was the Religious Settlement enforced? :: Church visitations (1559) checked clergy; the Oath of Supremacy; fines for recusants; bishops who refused were replaced
How many clergy refused to accept the Settlement? :: Few – about 400 of around 8 000 parish clergy lost their posts, though nearly all Marian bishops refused
What is a recusant? :: A Catholic who refused to attend Church of England services
What was the role of the Church in Elizabethan society? :: Centre of village life; taught obedience to the Queen through sermons; ran baptisms, marriages and burials; parish helped the poor
What did Puritans want? :: A 'purer' church with no Catholic features – no crucifixes or vestments, more preaching, some wanted no bishops (Presbyterians)
What happened in the Vestment Controversy (1566)? :: Archbishop Parker's 'Advertisements' made clergy wear vestments; 37 London clergy refused and lost their jobs
What were prophesyings? :: Puritan meetings for clergy to study and discuss the Bible – Elizabeth feared they spread radical ideas
Who was Archbishop Grindal and why was he suspended? :: Archbishop of Canterbury who refused to ban prophesyings (1576–77); Elizabeth suspended him (house arrest)
How serious was the Puritan challenge? :: Limited – most Puritans were loyal to Elizabeth; some influential MPs and councillors (Walsingham) sympathised; Elizabeth refused further change
What was the Catholic challenge in the 1560s? :: Mostly passive – many Catholics conformed outwardly; nobles in the north resented losing influence; the Pope told Catholics not to attend church (1566)
What was the Counter-Reformation? :: The Catholic Church's campaign to reform itself and win back Protestant areas (Council of Trent) – made the Papacy more hostile to Elizabeth
Why were foreign powers part of the Catholic threat? :: Catholic Spain and France could invade or support plots; the Pope could encourage rebellion
C: Mary Queen of Scots was born in {{c1::1542}} and fled to England in {{c2::May 1568}}.
Why did Mary Queen of Scots flee to England? :: Scottish lords rebelled after her husband Darnley was murdered (1567) and she married Bothwell (suspected killer); forced to abdicate for her son James; defeated at Langside (1568)
What options did Elizabeth have with Mary in 1568? :: Help her regain the Scottish throne; hand her to the Scottish lords; let her go to France; keep her prisoner in England – she chose prison
What were the Casket Letters? :: Letters said to show Mary helped murder Darnley; the inquiry (York/Westminster 1568–69) reached no verdict, giving Elizabeth an excuse to keep her
Why did keeping Mary prisoner create problems for Elizabeth? :: Mary became a focus for Catholic plots for 19 years; executing a fellow queen risked war and set a dangerous precedent
Explain why Mary Queen of Scots was a threat to Elizabeth 1568–69. :: Strong claim to the throne – many Catholics saw her as rightful queen ;; Catholic, so attracted English Catholic nobles (planned marriage to Norfolk) ;; Links to France and Spain could bring foreign support
Explain why religion was a problem for Elizabeth in 1558. :: The country was divided between Catholics and Protestants after Mary I ;; Catholic powers (France, Spain, the Pope) could intervene ;; Puritans wanted more change than she was willing to give
## Key topic 2: Challenges at home and abroad 1569–88
Who led the Revolt of the Northern Earls? :: Thomas Percy, Earl of Northumberland, and Charles Neville, Earl of Westmorland
C: The Northern Earls heard Catholic mass in Durham Cathedral in {{c1::November 1569}}.
What were the causes of the Revolt of the Northern Earls? :: Religion (restore Catholicism); loss of power and land to 'new men' like Cecil; plan for Mary to marry the Duke of Norfolk and be named heir
Why did the Revolt of the Northern Earls fail? :: Norfolk lost his nerve and was arrested; expected Spanish help never came; Earl of Sussex's royal army advanced; rebels fled to Scotland
What was the significance of the Revolt of the Northern Earls? :: First serious rebellion of the reign; showed Mary was a danger; Elizabeth took firmer control of the north (Council of the North); led to the 1570 papal bull
What was the name of the 1570 papal bull? :: 'Regnans in Excelsis'
How did the papal bull change the Catholic threat? :: Catholics were told to disobey Elizabeth, so they were seen as potential traitors; harsher laws against Catholics followed
What were seminary priests? :: English Catholic priests trained abroad (Douai, founded by William Allen in 1568) and sent into England from 1574 to keep Catholicism alive
Who was Edmund Campion? :: A Jesuit priest who came to England in 1580; captured, tortured and executed in 1581
What did the 1581 and 1585 Acts do to Catholics? :: 1581 – converting to Catholicism became treason; recusancy fine raised to £20 a month ;; 1585 – being a Jesuit or seminary priest in England became treason
Who planned the Ridolfi Plot invasion force? :: The Duke of Alba was to invade from the Netherlands with about 10 000 Spanish troops, backed by Philip II and the Pope
How was the Ridolfi Plot uncovered? :: Cecil's agents intercepted letters (via a messenger, Charles Baillie) and questioned him
What was the significance of the Ridolfi Plot? :: Showed Spain and the Pope were willing to back plots; Norfolk executed (1572); Elizabeth still refused to execute Mary despite Parliament's pressure
What was the significance of the Throckmorton Plot? :: Showed French and Spanish involvement; led to the Bond of Association (1584) and stricter watching of Catholics
What was the Bond of Association (1584)? :: An oath by nobles and gentry to kill anyone who plotted against Elizabeth – including the person who would benefit (Mary)
Who was the double agent in the Babington Plot? :: Gilbert Gifford – carried Mary's letters in beer barrels and passed them to Walsingham
Who decoded the letters in the Babington Plot? :: Thomas Phelippes, Walsingham's codebreaker
What was the significance of the Babington Plot? :: Gave clear proof Mary approved Elizabeth's murder – led to her trial (October 1586) and execution
C: Mary Queen of Scots was executed at {{c1::Fotheringhay Castle}} on {{c2::8 February 1587}}.
Who did Elizabeth blame for Mary's execution? :: William Davison, her secretary, who sent the signed warrant – he was imprisoned
What were the consequences of Mary's execution? :: Removed the main Catholic claimant; Philip II now had a reason (and Mary's claim) to invade – the Armada; James VI of Scotland protested but accepted (hoped to inherit)
When did Walsingham become Principal Secretary? :: 1573
What methods did Walsingham use? :: Spies at home and abroad (ports, embassies, universities), agents provocateurs, intercepting letters, codebreakers, torture (e.g. the rack for Throckmorton)
What was the political rivalry between England and Spain? :: Spain was the strongest Catholic power; Philip II wanted to restore Catholicism; England supported Dutch Protestant rebels
Why was the Netherlands important to England? :: Main market for English cloth (Antwerp); Spanish troops there could invade England
What was the Dutch Revolt? :: Protestants in the Spanish Netherlands rebelled from 1566; Philip sent the Duke of Alba with 10 000 troops in 1567
What was the commercial rivalry between England and Spain? :: Spain controlled trade with the New World and its silver; English merchants wanted a share; English privateers attacked Spanish ships
What was a privateer? :: A private ship captain given permission (secretly) by the monarch to attack enemy ships and keep a share of the treasure
Who was John Hawkins? :: An English merchant who traded enslaved Africans to Spanish colonies (1560s); attacked by the Spanish at San Juan de Ulúa (1568)
Why was San Juan de Ulúa (1568) important? :: The Spanish attacked Hawkins' and Drake's ships – Drake developed a lasting hatred of Spain
What happened in 1568 with the Genoese loan? :: Spanish ships carrying money to pay Alba's army sheltered in English ports; Elizabeth seized the money – relations worsened
What was the Nombre de Dios raid (1572–73)? :: Drake attacked Spanish silver shipments in Panama and returned with a large treasure
What was the significance of Drake's activities for relations with Spain? :: Made Elizabeth richer but infuriated Philip; Drake being knighted (1581) seemed an insult; pushed the countries towards war
What was the Treaty of Joinville (1584)? :: Philip II and the French Catholic League agreed to cooperate against Protestants – England feared a Catholic alliance
Why did Elizabeth intervene in the Netherlands in 1585? :: William of Orange was assassinated (1584); Antwerp fell to Parma (1585); fear Spain would win and then invade England
How many troops did the Treaty of Nonsuch send? :: About 7 400 under Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester
What was Robert Dudley's role in the Netherlands? :: Led the English army 1585–87; angered Elizabeth by accepting the title Governor-General (1586); poor results; returned 1587
What did Drake destroy at Cadiz apart from ships? :: Barrel staves (seasoned wood) – the Armada later had to use unseasoned barrels, so food and water rotted
C: Drake's raid on Cadiz in {{c1::1587}} became known as {{c2::'singeing the King of Spain's beard'}}.
What was Philip II's Armada plan? :: The Armada (about 130 ships) under the Duke of Medina Sidonia would sail up the Channel, meet the Duke of Parma's army in the Netherlands and escort it to invade Kent
Why did Philip launch the Armada in 1588? :: Religion (Pope's blessing); execution of Mary; English help to the Dutch; Drake's raids; to stop English attacks on trade
Who commanded the English fleet against the Armada? :: Lord Howard of Effingham, with Drake as vice-admiral
Describe the course of the Armada in 1588. :: Sighted off Cornwall in July; skirmishes up the Channel in crescent formation; anchored at Calais; fireships at night; Battle of Gravelines; wind blew them into the North Sea; sailed home round Scotland and Ireland
Why were English ships better than Spanish ships? :: Race-built galleons were faster and more manoeuvrable; long-range culverins; four-wheeled gun carriages allowed faster reloading
What mistakes did Spain make with the Armada? :: Medina Sidonia had little naval experience; no deep-water port to meet Parma; poor communication with Parma; rotten supplies; cannons designed for boarding
What were the consequences of the defeat of the Armada? :: Protestantism and Elizabeth's rule secure; boost to national pride and her image (Armada Portrait); England's naval reputation grew; but war with Spain continued until 1604
What did Elizabeth say at Tilbury (1588)? :: 'I have the body of a weak and feeble woman, but I have the heart and stomach of a king' – boosted morale
Explain why the Armada was defeated. :: English ships and guns were faster and better ;; Fireships at Calais broke the crescent and led to Gravelines ;; Spanish planning failed – no link-up with Parma; storms wrecked ships on the way home
Explain why relations between England and Spain worsened 1569–85. :: Religion – Philip backed Catholic plots (Ridolfi, Throckmorton) ;; Trade – privateers like Drake attacked Spanish treasure ;; Netherlands – English support for the Dutch rebels led to the Treaty of Nonsuch
Explain why Mary Queen of Scots was executed in 1587. :: The Babington Plot gave proof she backed murdering Elizabeth ;; The Bond of Association made it hard to protect her ;; Pressure from Parliament and Council; the threat from Spain made removing a Catholic heir more urgent
## Key topic 3: Society in the Age of Exploration 1558–88
Why did education expand under Elizabeth? :: Humanism valued learning; Protestants wanted people to read the Bible; growing need for educated men in government, law and trade
What was taught in petty schools? :: Basic reading and writing in English (using a hornbook), sometimes simple arithmetic – for young children, often run by local clergy or educated women
What was taught in grammar schools? :: Latin (and some Greek), classical authors, religion – boys from about 7 to 14 from gentry, yeomen and merchant families; long days, strict discipline (birching)
What was education like for girls? :: Mostly at home – noble girls learned music, languages, needlework, dancing and household management; few went to school; universities closed to them
How were the children of nobles educated? :: At home by private tutors; boys might then go to university or the Inns of Court (to study law)
Why did most poor children receive little education? :: They had to work from a young age and families couldn't afford fees
Which sports were popular with the rich? :: Hunting, hawking, real tennis, fencing, archery
Which sports were popular with ordinary people? :: Football (violent, few rules), wrestling, archery practice, blood sports like cockfighting and bear-baiting
What pastimes were popular in Elizabethan England? :: Music, dancing, card and dice games, reading (rich), plays, festivals and holidays (e.g. May Day, Christmas)
C: The first purpose-built playhouse in London, {{c1::The Theatre}}, was built by James Burbage in {{c2::1576}}.
Why were theatres built outside the City of London? :: City authorities (influenced by Puritans) disapproved – feared crime, disorder and plague spreading in crowds
Why did acting companies need noble patrons? :: Under the 1572 Vagabonds Act, actors without a patron could be treated as vagabonds (e.g. Leicester's Men had Dudley as patron)
Why did Puritans oppose the theatre? :: Thought plays immoral, drew people from church, encouraged idleness and crime
What was the theatre like for audiences? :: All classes attended – poor stood in the yard (groundlings) for a penny, wealthy sat in covered galleries
C: England's population rose from about {{c1::3 million}} in the 1550s to about {{c2::4 million}} by 1600.
How did enclosure cause poverty? :: Landowners fenced off land for sheep farming, which needed fewer workers, so labourers lost jobs and homes
What was rack-renting? :: Landlords raising rents sharply – tenant farmers could not pay and were evicted
How did bad harvests affect the poor? :: Food prices rose sharply, causing hunger and pushing people into vagrancy (e.g. 1586–88)
Why did the decline of the cloth trade cause poverty? :: Problems with the Antwerp market hit exports, so cloth workers lost jobs
Why were vagabonds feared? :: Seen as linked to crime, spreading disease and causing rebellion; moved around the country
Who were the deserving and undeserving poor? :: Deserving – old, sick, disabled, young, who could not work ;; Undeserving (sturdy beggars) – able-bodied who would not work
What was the 1563 Act for the Relief of the Poor? :: JPs could raise money from people in their parish for the poor – and punish those who refused to pay
What did the 1572 Vagabonds Act do? :: Vagabonds over 14 whipped and burned through the right ear; repeat offences could bring death ;; Introduced a compulsory national poor rate to help the deserving poor
What did the 1576 Poor Relief Act do? :: JPs had to provide materials (wool, flax) so the able-bodied poor could work for relief; those refusing sent to houses of correction
What were houses of correction? :: Places where the able-bodied poor who refused to work were made to work and punished (e.g. Bridewell in London)
How did towns deal with poverty? :: Ipswich and Norwich ran censuses of the poor and compulsory poor rates (Norwich census 1570); Ipswich set up a hospital for the poor; London used Bridewell
How did attitudes to the poor change under Elizabeth? :: Growing acceptance that some poverty was not the person's fault and that the community should help through compulsory rates, alongside harsh punishment of vagabonds
Explain why poverty increased in Elizabethan England. :: Population growth and inflation – more people, higher prices, lower real wages ;; Enclosure and rack-renting drove labourers off the land ;; Bad harvests and the decline of the cloth trade
What new technology helped exploration? :: Better ships (galleons), the astrolabe and quadrant for latitude, the magnetic compass, more accurate maps (Mercator projection 1569)
Why did the English want to expand trade? :: The cloth trade with Antwerp declined; new markets and goods (spices, silk) were needed; investors formed companies like the Muscovy Company (1555)
Why was privateering a factor in exploration? :: The chance of capturing Spanish treasure attracted investors (including Elizabeth) and sailors
C: Drake left Plymouth in {{c1::December 1577}} with five ships and returned in {{c2::September 1580}}.
What did Drake do during the circumnavigation? :: Sailed through the Strait of Magellan; raided Spanish ports on the Pacific coast; captured the treasure ship Cacafuego; claimed 'Nova Albion' (California); traded for spices in the Moluccas
What was the significance of Drake's circumnavigation? :: Huge treasure for investors and the Queen; showed Spain was vulnerable; boosted English pride and exploration; worsened relations with Spain
Where was Drake knighted? :: On the Golden Hind at Deptford in April 1581, by a French diplomat on Elizabeth's orders
C: Raleigh received a patent to colonise in {{c1::1584}}; the first colony landed on Roanoke Island in {{c2::1585}}.
Why was the colony named Virginia? :: After Elizabeth, the 'Virgin Queen'
What was the significance of Raleigh? :: Organised and paid for the Virginia expeditions; encouraged colonisation and investment ;; Elizabeth would not let him sail himself
Who led the 1585 Roanoke colony? :: Ralph Lane (governor) with Sir Richard Grenville commanding the fleet – about 100 men
What happened to the 1585 colony? :: Relations with Native Americans broke down (Lane killed chief Wingina); supplies ran low; colonists went home with Drake in 1586

# Paper 2 Superpower relations and the Cold War 1941–91
What were the Grand Alliance conferences? :: Tehran (1943), Yalta (Feb 1945), Potsdam (July 1945)
What was agreed at Yalta (1945)? :: Germany divided into four zones; free elections in Eastern Europe; USSR to join war against Japan; United Nations set up; Poland in Soviet sphere
What changed at Potsdam (1945)? :: Truman replaced Roosevelt, Attlee replaced Churchill; disagreements over Germany and reparations; US had the atomic bomb
What were the Long and Novikov Telegrams (1946)? :: Kennan (Long) – USSR wanted to spread communism, US should contain it ;; Novikov – US wanted world domination
What was the Iron Curtain? :: Churchill's 1946 phrase for the division of Europe between communist east and capitalist west
What was the Truman Doctrine (1947)? :: US would support countries threatened by communism (containment) – first applied to Greece and Turkey
What was Marshall Aid? :: US economic aid ($13 billion) to rebuild Europe and stop the spread of communism ('dollar imperialism' to the USSR)
How did Stalin respond to Marshall Aid? :: Cominform (1947) to control communist parties; Comecon (1949) as an economic alternative
What was the Berlin Blockade (1948–49)? :: Stalin cut off road, rail and canal access to West Berlin; the West responded with the Berlin Airlift; ended May 1949
What were the consequences of the Berlin Blockade? :: Germany split into FRG (West) and GDR (East) 1949; NATO formed 1949; Warsaw Pact 1955
What was the arms race? :: Competition to build more nuclear weapons: US A-bomb 1945, USSR 1949, H-bombs 1952/53, ICBMs; Sputnik 1957
Why did Hungary rise up in 1956? :: Hatred of Rákosi's rule, Khrushchev's destalinisation speech; Nagy promised reforms and leaving the Warsaw Pact
What happened in the Hungarian Uprising? :: Soviet tanks invaded Budapest November 1956; thousands of Hungarians killed (older textbooks say ~20,000; modern estimates about 2,500–3,000); Nagy executed; Kádár installed; West did not intervene
Why was the Berlin Wall built (1961)? :: To stop the 'brain drain' of skilled East Germans fleeing to the West (around 3 million by 1961)
What were the consequences of the Berlin Wall? :: Ended refugee crisis; symbol of the Cold War; Kennedy's 'Ich bin ein Berliner' speech (1963); reduced tension over Berlin
What was the Bay of Pigs invasion (1961)? :: CIA-trained Cuban exiles invaded Cuba to overthrow Castro; failed; pushed Castro closer to the USSR
Describe the Cuban Missile Crisis (October 1962). :: US U-2 spy planes found Soviet missile sites in Cuba; Kennedy imposed a naval blockade; 13 days; Khrushchev removed missiles in exchange for no US invasion of Cuba and (secretly) removal of US Jupiter missiles from Turkey
What were the consequences of the Cuban Missile Crisis? :: Hotline (1963), Limited Test Ban Treaty (1963), Outer Space Treaty (1967), Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968) – start of détente
What was the Prague Spring (1968)? :: Dubček's reforms in Czechoslovakia ('socialism with a human face') – free speech, less censorship; Warsaw Pact invaded August 1968
What was the Brezhnev Doctrine? :: USSR would intervene in any communist country that threatened the Warsaw Pact
What was détente? :: Easing of tension in the 1970s: SALT 1 (1972), Apollo–Soyuz (1975), Helsinki Agreement (1975)
Why did détente end? :: Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (1979); Carter Doctrine; US boycott of Moscow Olympics (1980); SALT 2 not ratified
What was Reagan's 'second Cold War'? :: Called USSR the 'evil empire'; increased arms spending; Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI/'Star Wars', 1983)
What were Gorbachev's 'new thinking' policies? :: Perestroika (economic restructuring), glasnost (openness), Sinatra Doctrine (let Eastern Europe choose its own path)
Which summits and treaties improved relations in the late 1980s? :: Geneva (1985), Reykjavik (1986), Washington – INF Treaty (1987), Malta (1989)
When did the Berlin Wall fall and when did the USSR collapse? :: Wall fell November 1989; Germany reunified 1990; Warsaw Pact dissolved 1991; USSR collapsed December 1991
## Key topic 1: Origins of the Cold War 1941–58
What was the Grand Alliance? :: The wartime alliance of Britain (Churchill), the USA (Roosevelt) and the USSR (Stalin) against Nazi Germany
What was agreed at Tehran (1943)? :: Britain and the USA would open a second front in France (D-Day); USSR would join the war against Japan after Germany's defeat; the USSR could keep part of eastern Poland
C: The Yalta Conference took place in {{c1::February 1945}}; the Potsdam Conference in {{c2::July–August 1945}}.
What was agreed at Potsdam? :: Germany demilitarised and denazified; war criminals tried (Nuremberg); Poland's western border on the Oder–Neisse line; each power took reparations from its own zone (USSR also got some industrial equipment from western zones)
Why did tension grow at Potsdam? :: New leaders (Truman more anti-communist); Soviet troops occupied Eastern Europe; arguments over reparations and Poland; Truman told Stalin about the atomic bomb
What did the atomic bomb do to relations? :: Dropped on Hiroshima (6 Aug 1945) and Nagasaki (9 Aug) – Stalin felt threatened and determined to get his own bomb
What were the ideological differences between the USA and USSR? :: USA – capitalism, democracy, free elections, private ownership, individual freedom ;; USSR – communism, one-party state, state ownership, equality, censorship
Who wrote the Long Telegram and when? :: George Kennan, US diplomat in Moscow, February 1946
Who wrote the Novikov Telegram and when? :: Nikolai Novikov, Soviet ambassador to the USA, September 1946
What was the significance of the Long and Novikov Telegrams? :: Each side became convinced the other was aggressive – the US adopted containment; the USSR strengthened its hold on Eastern Europe
C: Churchill made his 'Iron Curtain' speech at Fulton, Missouri, in {{c1::March 1946}}.
Why did Stalin want satellite states in Eastern Europe? :: A buffer zone for security after invasions through Eastern Europe (about 20 million Soviet dead in WW2); to spread communism
How did Stalin create satellite states? :: Communists took over through rigged elections, arresting opponents, 'salami tactics', backed by the Red Army – e.g. Poland (1947), Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia (1948)
Which communist country broke away from Stalin's control? :: Yugoslavia under Tito (1948)
Why did Truman announce the Truman Doctrine in March 1947? :: Britain could no longer afford to support Greece against communist rebels; Turkey under Soviet pressure; US gave $400 million
When was the Marshall Plan announced and who benefited? :: June 1947 by US Secretary of State George Marshall; 16 Western European countries received aid 1948–52 – Stalin banned Eastern European states from accepting
How did Stalin view the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan? :: As 'dollar imperialism' – the US using money to dominate Europe and threaten the USSR
What was Cominform? :: Communist Information Bureau (1947) – coordinated communist parties and kept satellite states loyal to Moscow
What was Comecon? :: Council for Mutual Economic Assistance (1949) – Soviet alternative to Marshall Aid for Eastern Europe
Why was Germany a source of tension after 1945? :: USA wanted a strong German economy for trade and against communism; USSR wanted Germany weak and reparations
What were Bizonia and Trizonia? :: Bizonia – British and US zones merged (1947) ;; Trizonia – French zone added (1948)
What triggered the Berlin Blockade? :: Western powers introduced the new Deutschmark in their zones (June 1948) – Stalin feared a strong West Germany
C: The Berlin Blockade lasted from {{c1::June 1948}} to {{c2::May 1949}}.
How large was the Berlin Airlift? :: About 2.3 million tonnes of supplies flown in on over 270 000 flights
Why was the Berlin Airlift a success for the West? :: Kept West Berlin supplied without war; Stalin backed down; propaganda victory showing US commitment
C: The FRG (West Germany) was created in {{c1::May 1949}} and the GDR (East Germany) in {{c2::October 1949}}.
C: NATO was formed in {{c1::April 1949}} and the Warsaw Pact in {{c2::May 1955}}.
What was NATO? :: North Atlantic Treaty Organisation – a military alliance of the USA, Canada and Western European countries; an attack on one was an attack on all
Why was the Warsaw Pact formed? :: Soviet response to West Germany joining NATO (1955) – a military alliance of the USSR and its satellites under Soviet command
C: The USSR tested its first atomic bomb in {{c1::1949}} and launched {{c2::Sputnik}} in 1957.
What is MAD? :: Mutually Assured Destruction – both sides had enough nuclear weapons to destroy each other, so neither would start a nuclear war
What was the significance of the arms race? :: Increased fear and tension; huge cost; but MAD acted as a deterrent and prevented direct war
Why did Sputnik and the ICBM alarm the USA? :: Showed the USSR could launch missiles that could hit the USA – fears of a 'missile gap'
Who was Rákosi? :: Hungary's hard-line Stalinist leader – secret police (AVH), terror, poverty; removed in 1956
C: The Hungarian Uprising began on {{c1::23 October 1956}}; Soviet tanks crushed it from {{c2::4 November 1956}}.
What did Nagy announce that led to the Soviet invasion? :: Hungary would leave the Warsaw Pact (1 November 1956) and hold free elections
How did the West react to the Hungarian Uprising? :: Condemned it at the UN, accepted refugees (about 200 000 fled) ;; No military help – Hungary was in the Soviet sphere, risk of nuclear war, Suez Crisis distracted Britain and France
What were the consequences of the Hungarian Uprising? :: Soviet control reinforced; showed the US would not intervene in the Soviet sphere; Khrushchev showed he was tough; Nagy executed (1958)
Explain why the Grand Alliance broke down 1945–47. :: Ideological differences resurfaced once Hitler was defeated ;; Disputes over Germany and Poland; Soviet control of Eastern Europe ;; New leaders (Truman), the atomic bomb and the telegrams increased mistrust
Explain the importance of the Berlin Blockade for the development of the Cold War. :: First major crisis – Germany divided into FRG and GDR (1949) ;; Led to the formation of NATO (1949) ;; Showed US commitment to containment in Europe
## Key topic 2: Cold War crises 1958–70
Why was the refugee problem in Berlin a crisis for the USSR? :: Skilled young East Germans fled through West Berlin – embarrassing for communism and damaging the GDR economy
C: Khrushchev issued his Berlin ultimatum in {{c1::November 1958}}, demanding Western troops leave Berlin within {{c2::six months}}.
What happened at the Geneva Summit (1959)? :: Foreign ministers met to discuss Berlin – no agreement, but talks continued
What happened at Camp David (1959)? :: Eisenhower and Khrushchev met in the USA; Khrushchev dropped the ultimatum; agreed to meet in Paris
What happened at the Paris Summit (1960)? :: Collapsed because the USSR had shot down a US U-2 spy plane (pilot Gary Powers) on 1 May 1960 and Eisenhower refused to apologise
What happened at the Vienna Summit (1961)? :: Khrushchev bullied the young Kennedy and renewed the Berlin ultimatum; Kennedy increased US defence spending
C: Construction of the Berlin Wall began on {{c1::13 August 1961}}.
What happened at Checkpoint Charlie in October 1961? :: US and Soviet tanks faced each other for 16 hours before both withdrew
C: Kennedy made his 'Ich bin ein Berliner' speech in West Berlin in {{c1::June 1963}}.
Why did Kennedy accept the Berlin Wall? :: It avoided war – 'a wall is a hell of a lot better than a war'; West Berlin stayed free
What were the international consequences of the Berlin Wall? :: Ended the refugee crisis and the Berlin problem; became a propaganda symbol of communist oppression; Berlin was no longer a flashpoint
C: Fidel Castro took power in Cuba in {{c1::January 1959}}.
Why did Cuba become a problem for the USA? :: Castro nationalised US businesses; the US imposed a trade embargo; Cuba turned to the USSR (sold sugar, received aid); only 90 miles from Florida
C: The Bay of Pigs invasion took place in {{c1::April 1961}}.
What were the consequences of the Bay of Pigs? :: Humiliated Kennedy; Castro turned fully to the USSR and declared Cuba communist; encouraged Khrushchev to put missiles in Cuba
Why did Khrushchev put missiles in Cuba? :: Protect Cuba from US invasion; close the missile gap (US Jupiter missiles in Turkey); test Kennedy; bargaining chip (e.g. over Berlin)
C: A U-2 photographed missile sites in Cuba on {{c1::14 October 1962}}; Kennedy announced a blockade on {{c2::22 October 1962}}.
What was ExComm? :: Kennedy's executive committee of advisers during the Cuban Missile Crisis – debated options (air strike, invasion, blockade)
Why did Kennedy choose a blockade (quarantine)? :: Gave Khrushchev time to back down without humiliation; less risky than an air strike or invasion which could start nuclear war
What was 'Black Saturday' (27 October 1962)? :: A U-2 was shot down over Cuba and Khrushchev sent a second letter demanding US missiles leave Turkey – the most dangerous day
How did the Cuban Missile Crisis end? :: Robert Kennedy met Soviet ambassador Dobrynin; on 28 October Khrushchev agreed to remove the missiles in return for a US pledge not to invade Cuba (Turkey missiles removed secretly later)
C: The Washington–Moscow hotline was set up in {{c1::1963}}.
What did the Limited Test Ban Treaty (1963) do? :: Banned nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and underwater
What were the consequences of the Cuban Missile Crisis for the leaders? :: Kennedy's reputation rose ;; Khrushchev seemed weak (deal on Turkey was secret) and was removed in 1964 ;; Castro kept power
Explain the importance of the Cuban Missile Crisis for relations between the superpowers. :: Brought the world closest to nuclear war, so both sides wanted to reduce risk ;; Led to the hotline and arms control treaties (LTBT 1963, OST 1967, NPT 1968) ;; Began a move towards détente
Who did Dubček replace in Czechoslovakia in January 1968? :: Antonín Novotný, an unpopular hard-line communist
What was in Dubček's Action Programme (April 1968)? :: More freedom of speech and press, less secret police power, freedom to travel, some private business, more trade with the West – but stay in the Warsaw Pact
Why did Brezhnev invade Czechoslovakia? :: Feared reforms would spread to other satellites; worried Czechoslovakia would drift to the West and weaken the Warsaw Pact
C: Warsaw Pact troops invaded Czechoslovakia on {{c1::20–21 August 1968}}.
What happened after the invasion of Czechoslovakia? :: Dubček taken to Moscow and replaced by Gustáv Husák (1969); reforms reversed; student Jan Palach burned himself in protest (1969)
How did the West react to the Prague Spring invasion? :: Condemned it but did nothing – the US was busy in Vietnam and accepted Czechoslovakia was in the Soviet sphere; Yugoslavia, Romania and Western communist parties criticised it
Explain why the Berlin Wall was built in 1961. :: The refugee 'brain drain' was damaging East Germany ;; Khrushchev failed to force the West out of Berlin through ultimatums and summits ;; A wall solved the problem without war
Write a narrative account of the Cuban Missile Crisis – key stages. :: Cuban Revolution (1959) and US hostility → Bay of Pigs (1961) → Soviet missiles found (Oct 1962) → blockade → letters and Black Saturday → deal on 28 Oct → hotline and treaties
## Key topic 3: The end of the Cold War 1970–91
Why did both superpowers want détente in the 1970s? :: Arms race too expensive; fear of nuclear war after Cuba; US weakened by Vietnam; USSR wanted Western trade and technology
C: SALT 1 was signed by Nixon and Brezhnev in {{c1::1972}}.
What did SALT 1 agree? :: Interim Treaty froze numbers of ICBMs and submarine missile launchers for 5 years; ABM Treaty limited anti-ballistic missile sites; Basic Principles Agreement
C: The Helsinki Agreement was signed by 35 countries in {{c1::1975}}.
What were the three 'baskets' of the Helsinki Agreement? :: 1 – Security: recognised Europe's borders (including East Germany) ;; 2 – Cooperation in trade, science and technology ;; 3 – Human rights (freedom of speech, movement)
Why was the human rights basket a problem for the USSR? :: The USSR did not keep it, so the West could criticise Soviet abuses, which damaged trust
C: SALT 2 was signed by Carter and Brezhnev in {{c1::1979}} but never ratified by the US Senate.
What did SALT 2 propose? :: Limits on strategic nuclear launchers (about 2 250 each) and restrictions on new missiles
C: The USSR invaded Afghanistan in {{c1::December 1979}}.
Why did the USSR invade Afghanistan? :: To support a friendly communist government; fear of Islamic fundamentalism (after the 1979 Iranian Revolution) spreading to Soviet Muslim republics; fear of US influence
What was the Carter Doctrine (1980)? :: The US would use military force to defend its interests in the Persian Gulf; also armed the Mujahideen, stopped grain sales, withdrew SALT 2 from the Senate
What were the Olympic boycotts? :: USA and around 60 countries boycotted the Moscow Olympics (1980); the USSR and most of the Eastern bloc boycotted Los Angeles (1984)
What were the consequences of the invasion of Afghanistan? :: Ended détente; long, costly war for the USSR (withdrew 1989); 'Second Cold War'; US backed the Mujahideen
Why did Reagan take a hard line against the USSR? :: Strongly anti-communist; believed détente had helped the USSR; wanted to win the Cold War by outspending the USSR
C: Reagan called the USSR an {{c1::'evil empire'}} and announced SDI in {{c2::1983}}.
What was SDI ('Star Wars')? :: Strategic Defense Initiative – a planned system of satellites with lasers to destroy Soviet missiles in space, making the USA safe from attack
Why was SDI important? :: Threatened to end MAD and break the spirit of the Outer Space Treaty; the USSR could not afford to compete – pressure for change
What was the Reagan Doctrine? :: US support for anti-communist groups around the world (e.g. Contras in Nicaragua, Mujahideen in Afghanistan)
Who led the USSR between Brezhnev and Gorbachev? :: Andropov (1982–84) and Chernenko (1984–85) – both old and ill; Gorbachev took over in March 1985
Why did Gorbachev introduce 'new thinking'? :: Soviet economy was failing; spending on the arms race and Afghanistan was unaffordable; people had low living standards; he wanted to reform communism
What happened at the Geneva Summit (1985)? :: Reagan and Gorbachev's first meeting – no agreements but they built a good relationship
What happened at the Reykjavik Summit (1986)? :: Nearly agreed to scrap nuclear weapons but failed because Reagan would not give up SDI
C: The INF Treaty was signed in Washington in {{c1::December 1987}}.
What did the INF Treaty do? :: Eliminated all intermediate-range nuclear missiles (500–5 500 km) in Europe – the first treaty to reduce nuclear weapons
What did Gorbachev announce at the UN in December 1988? :: Cuts of 500 000 Soviet troops and that Eastern European countries could choose their own path (the end of the Brezhnev Doctrine)
C: The Malta Summit between Bush and Gorbachev in {{c1::December 1989}} is often seen as the end of the Cold War.
How did Gorbachev's new thinking affect Eastern Europe? :: He would not use force to keep communist governments in power, so people protested and communist regimes collapsed in 1989
What happened in Poland in 1989? :: Solidarity (led by Lech Wałęsa) won free elections – first non-communist government in the Eastern bloc
How did Hungary contribute to the fall of the Berlin Wall? :: Opened its border with Austria (1989), letting thousands of East Germans escape to the West
C: The Berlin Wall was opened on {{c1::9 November 1989}}; Germany reunified on {{c2::3 October 1990}}.
What other revolutions happened in Eastern Europe in 1989? :: Czechoslovakia's Velvet Revolution (Václav Havel), Romania (Ceaușescu executed), Bulgaria
Why was the fall of the Berlin Wall significant? :: Symbol of the end of communist control of Eastern Europe; led to German reunification
What happened in the August 1991 coup? :: Communist hard-liners tried to overthrow Gorbachev; it failed when Boris Yeltsin led resistance – weakened Gorbachev further
C: The Warsaw Pact was dissolved in {{c1::July 1991}}; Gorbachev resigned on {{c2::25 December 1991}} and the USSR ceased to exist.
Explain why the Soviet Union collapsed in 1991. :: Economic weakness worsened by the arms race and Afghanistan ;; Glasnost allowed criticism and nationalism in the republics (e.g. Baltic states) ;; Loss of Eastern Europe in 1989 and the failed 1991 coup destroyed communist authority
Explain the importance of the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan for relations between the superpowers. :: Ended détente – SALT 2 not ratified ;; Carter Doctrine and Olympic boycotts increased hostility ;; Drained the USSR, contributing to Gorbachev's reforms

# Paper 3 Weimar and Nazi Germany 1918–39
What problems faced the Weimar Republic in 1919? :: Treaty of Versailles, political extremism (left and right), economic problems, weak constitution (Article 48, proportional representation)
What were the terms of the Treaty of Versailles? :: Land lost (13%, Alsace-Lorraine, colonies), army limited to 100 000, no air force, Rhineland demilitarised, reparations (£6.6 billion), War Guilt Clause (Article 231)
What was the 'stab in the back' myth? :: The belief that the army had not been defeated but betrayed by politicians ('November Criminals') who signed the armistice
What were the weaknesses of the Weimar constitution? :: Proportional representation – many small parties, coalitions, weak government ;; Article 48 – president could rule by decree
What was the Spartacist Revolt (1919)? :: Communist uprising in Berlin led by Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht; crushed by the Freikorps
What was the Kapp Putsch (1920)? :: Right-wing Freikorps under Wolfgang Kapp took over Berlin; failed due to a general strike by workers
Why did hyperinflation happen in 1923? :: Germany missed reparations; France and Belgium occupied the Ruhr; workers went on strike (passive resistance); government printed money
What were the effects of hyperinflation? :: Savings worthless (middle classes hit hard); prices soared (loaf of bread 200 billion marks by Nov 1923); people with debts/mortgages benefited
How did Stresemann bring recovery (1924–29)? :: Rentenmark (1923); Dawes Plan (1924) – US loans, reparations reduced; Young Plan (1929); Locarno Pact (1925); League of Nations (1926); Kellogg–Briand Pact (1928)
What were the limitations of recovery under Stresemann? :: Reliance on US loans; farmers struggled; extremist parties still had support; unemployment remained
What were cultural changes in Weimar Germany? :: Freedom of expression: Bauhaus architecture, Expressionist art, cinema (Metropolis), cabaret; new women (more freedom, vote, work)
What were the early aims of the Nazi Party (Twenty-Five Point Programme, 1920)? :: Abolish Versailles, union of all Germans, Lebensraum, only Germans as citizens (anti-Semitism), strong central government
What was the Munich Putsch (1923)? :: Hitler's attempt to seize power in Bavaria, Beer Hall; failed; Hitler tried and sentenced to 5 years (served 9 months), wrote Mein Kampf
How did the Nazis change tactics after the Munich Putsch? :: Use democracy (elections) to gain power; Bamberg Conference (1926); Hitler Youth; reorganised party nationally; propaganda
How did the Depression (1929) help the Nazis? :: Unemployment rose to 6 million; Weimar seemed weak; people turned to extremists – Nazis became largest party in July 1932 (37%)
Why did Hitler become Chancellor in January 1933? :: Backstairs intrigue – von Papen and Hindenburg thought they could control him; Nazi popularity; fear of communism
What was the Reichstag Fire (February 1933)? :: Fire blamed on communist van der Lubbe; Hitler used it to get Hindenburg to pass the Decree for the Protection of People and State – arrests of communists
What was the Enabling Act (March 1933)? :: Allowed Hitler to pass laws without the Reichstag for four years – effectively a dictatorship
What was the Night of the Long Knives (June 1934)? :: Hitler had Ernst Röhm and other SA leaders (and opponents) murdered by the SS – secured army support
How did Hitler become Führer? :: Hindenburg died August 1934; Hitler combined roles of Chancellor and President; army swore oath of loyalty to him
What was the police state in Nazi Germany? :: SS (Himmler), Gestapo (secret police), SD, concentration camps, informers, courts controlled by Nazis
How did the Nazis use propaganda? :: Ministry of Propaganda under Goebbels: radio (cheap People's Receivers), rallies (Nuremberg), newspapers, films, 1936 Berlin Olympics, censorship
How did the Nazis deal with the churches? :: Concordat with the Catholic Church (1933) – later broken; Reich Church (Protestant) under Müller; Pastor Niemöller's Confessing Church opposed – sent to camps
How did the Nazis treat young people? :: Hitler Youth and League of German Maidens (compulsory 1939); school curriculum changed (race studies, PE, history); teachers in Nazi Teachers' League
What were Nazi policies towards women? :: Kinder, Küche, Kirche (children, kitchen, church); Law for Encouragement of Marriage (loans); Mother's Cross medals; discouraged from working
How did the Nazis reduce unemployment? :: National Labour Service (RAD), public works (autobahns), rearmament, conscription (1935), invisible unemployment (women and Jews not counted)
What were Strength Through Joy and Beauty of Labour? :: Part of the German Labour Front (DAF): KdF – cheap holidays/leisure; SdA – improved workplaces
How were Jews persecuted 1933–39? :: Boycott of Jewish shops (1933); Nuremberg Laws (1935) – removed citizenship, banned marriage with Germans; Kristallnacht (November 1938) – synagogues and shops destroyed, 20 000+ sent to camps
Which other groups were persecuted by the Nazis? :: Roma and Sinti, homosexuals, disabled people (sterilisation law 1933), Slavs, 'asocials', Jehovah's Witnesses
Who opposed the Nazis in the 1930s? :: Some churches (Niemöller, Galen later), youth groups (Swing Youth, Edelweiss Pirates)
## Key topic 1: The Weimar Republic 1918–29
What was the legacy of the First World War for Germany? :: About 2 million soldiers dead; British naval blockade caused food shortages and starvation; huge debts; bitterness at defeat
C: Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicated on {{c1::9 November 1918}}; the armistice was signed on {{c2::11 November 1918}}.
What revolution happened in Germany in 1918? :: Sailors mutinied at Kiel; workers' and soldiers' councils took over towns; the Kaiser fled to the Netherlands and a republic was declared
Who led the new republic in 1918–19? :: Friedrich Ebert (SPD) – became Chancellor in November 1918 and the first President in February 1919
Why did the new government meet in Weimar? :: Berlin was too dangerous because of unrest (Spartacists) – the National Assembly met in Weimar in February 1919
How was the Weimar President chosen and what were his powers? :: Elected every 7 years ;; Chose the Chancellor, commanded the army, could dissolve the Reichstag and rule by decree under Article 48
How was the Reichstag elected? :: Every 4 years by proportional representation; all men and women over 20 could vote
What was the Reichsrat? :: The second chamber representing the 17 states (Länder) – could delay laws passed by the Reichstag
What were the strengths of the Weimar constitution? :: Democratic – votes for women and men over 20; basic rights (free speech, religion); checks and balances between President, Reichstag and courts
Why was Article 48 a weakness? :: The President could bypass the Reichstag and rule by decree in an emergency – later used to undermine democracy (1930–33)
Why was proportional representation a weakness? :: Many small parties won seats so no party had a majority; coalitions often collapsed – unstable government
Why were the army, judges and civil servants a problem for Weimar? :: Many were the Kaiser's men, conservative and disloyal to the republic – judges were lenient to right-wing extremists
C: The Treaty of Versailles was signed on {{c1::28 June 1919}}.
Why did Germans call the Treaty of Versailles a 'Diktat'? :: Germany was not allowed to negotiate – it was forced to sign
Which territories did Germany lose at Versailles? :: Alsace-Lorraine to France; West Prussia and Posen (Polish Corridor) to Poland; all colonies; Saar run by the League for 15 years; Danzig a free city; union with Austria (Anschluss) banned
What military terms did Versailles impose? :: Army limited to 100 000; no conscription; no tanks, air force or submarines; navy limited to 6 battleships; Rhineland demilitarised
Who were the 'November Criminals'? :: The politicians who signed the armistice (and later the treaty) – blamed for betraying Germany
Who was the Spartacist League? :: A communist group led by Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht that wanted a revolution like Russia's (1917)
C: The Spartacist Revolt took place in Berlin in {{c1::January 1919}}.
Who were the Freikorps? :: Right-wing ex-soldiers who kept their weapons – used by Ebert to crush the Spartacists, but hostile to the republic
C: The Kapp Putsch took place in {{c1::March 1920}}.
Why did the Kapp Putsch happen? :: Freikorps were angry at the Treaty of Versailles and at being disbanded – wanted to restore the Kaiser's style of rule
What role did the army play in the Kapp Putsch? :: Refused to fire on the Freikorps – the government had to flee Berlin
What political murders took place in the early 1920s? :: Matthias Erzberger (1921, signed the armistice) and Walther Rathenau (1922, Foreign Minister) killed by right-wing groups
Why did France occupy the Ruhr in January 1923? :: Germany failed to pay reparations (missed deliveries of coal and timber)
What was passive resistance in the Ruhr? :: Workers went on strike and refused to cooperate with the French – the government paid their wages by printing money
How much did German money collapse by November 1923? :: One US dollar was worth over 4 trillion marks
Who benefited from hyperinflation? :: People with debts or mortgages (paid off cheaply), businessmen who borrowed, some foreigners
Who suffered most from hyperinflation? :: People with savings and fixed incomes (pensioners, middle classes); workers whose wages couldn't keep up; food shortages
Why did hyperinflation damage the Weimar Republic? :: The middle classes lost faith in the republic; extremist parties gained support; contributed to the Munich Putsch
Explain why the Weimar Republic was unpopular 1919–23. :: Signed the hated Treaty of Versailles – stab in the back myth ;; Weak, unstable coalition government and attacks from left and right ;; Hyperinflation (1923) ruined savings
When was Stresemann Chancellor and Foreign Minister? :: Chancellor August–November 1923; Foreign Minister 1923 until his death in October 1929
What were Stresemann's first steps in 1923? :: Called off passive resistance (September 1923); began paying reparations again; introduced the Rentenmark (November 1923)
How did the Rentenmark work? :: A new temporary currency backed by German land and property – replaced by the Reichsmark (1924); ended hyperinflation
What did the Dawes Plan (1924) do? :: Reparations reduced to more affordable annual payments for a time; US loans of 800 million marks; French troops left the Ruhr (1925)
What did the Young Plan (1929) do? :: Reduced total reparations from £6.6 billion to about £2 billion with 59 years to pay
Why was the Young Plan opposed? :: Nationalists (Hugenberg, Hitler) said Germany should pay nothing – the campaign against it gave the Nazis national publicity
What did the Locarno Pact (1925) agree? :: Germany accepted its western borders with France and Belgium; Rhineland stayed demilitarised – Germany treated as an equal
C: Germany joined the League of Nations in {{c1::1926}} as a permanent member of the Council.
What was the Kellogg–Briand Pact (1928)? :: Germany and over 60 other countries agreed not to use war to settle disputes
Why did Stresemann win the Nobel Peace Prize (1926)? :: For improving relations with France and the Locarno Pact (shared with Briand)
What evidence shows recovery 1924–29? :: Industrial output passed pre-war levels by 1928; extremist parties lost votes (Nazis 2.6% in 1928); international acceptance
Why was the recovery 1924–29 fragile? :: Depended on short-term US loans that could be recalled; farmers faced falling prices; unemployment never disappeared; extremists still opposed the republic
How did wages change 1924–29? :: Real wages rose (by 1928 among the highest in Europe) – working class better off
How did housing improve 1924–29? :: Government and local councils built over 2 million new homes (1924–31), funded by a rent tax
What was the Unemployment Insurance Act (1927)? :: Workers paid contributions to receive benefits when unemployed – covered about 17 million workers
C: Hindenburg was elected President in {{c1::1925}}.
How did the position of women change in politics in Weimar Germany? :: Women over 20 gained the vote (1919); around 10% of Reichstag deputies in 1919 were women; equality written into the constitution
How did the position of women change in work in Weimar Germany? :: More women in the professions (teachers, doctors, lawyers), civil service, offices and shops – but often lower pay; many expected to leave work on marriage
Who was the 'new woman'? :: Young independent women with short hair and make-up, who smoked, went out alone, dressed fashionably – criticised by conservatives
What was the Bauhaus? :: School of design founded by Walter Gropius in Weimar (1919), moved to Dessau (1925) – functional buildings and furniture using steel, glass and concrete
Name two Weimar artists and their style. :: Otto Dix and George Grosz – New Objectivity (Neue Sachlichkeit): realistic, critical paintings showing poverty, war wounds and corruption
Name two famous Weimar films. :: 'The Cabinet of Dr Caligari' (1920) and Fritz Lang's 'Metropolis' (1927); star Marlene Dietrich
Why did some Germans dislike Weimar culture? :: Conservatives and nationalists saw it as immoral and decadent, especially in Berlin's cabaret and nightlife
Explain why Germany recovered 1924–29. :: Stresemann's Rentenmark ended hyperinflation ;; Dawes Plan and US loans boosted industry ;; International agreements (Locarno, League) restored confidence
## Key topic 2: Hitler's rise to power 1919–33
Describe Hitler's early life up to 1919. :: Born 1889 in Austria; failed to get into art school in Vienna; volunteered for the Bavarian army in WW1, awarded the Iron Cross; after the war worked as an army political agent
Who founded the German Workers' Party (DAP) and when did Hitler join? :: Anton Drexler founded it in January 1919; Hitler joined in September 1919 after being sent to spy on it
C: The DAP was renamed the National Socialist German Workers' Party (NSDAP) in {{c1::February 1920}}; Hitler became leader in {{c2::July 1921}}.
What were the early features of the Nazi Party? :: Swastika symbol, salute, newspaper (Völkischer Beobachter, bought 1920), mass meetings with Hitler's speeches, the SA
What was the SA? :: Sturmabteilung (stormtroopers, 'Brownshirts') set up in 1921, led by Ernst Röhm – protected Nazi meetings and attacked opponents
Why did the Nazi Party grow in its early years (1920–23)? :: Hitler's powerful speeches; simple message blaming Versailles, Jews and communists; anger at Weimar; mainly in Bavaria
C: The Munich Putsch took place on {{c1::8–9 November 1923}}.
Why did Hitler attempt the Munich Putsch? :: Hyperinflation and the Ruhr crisis made Weimar unpopular; Stresemann called off passive resistance; inspired by Mussolini's March on Rome (1922); hoped the Bavarian leaders would join
What happened in the Munich Putsch? :: Hitler took over a Bavarian government meeting in the Bürgerbräukeller beer hall; next day marched with Ludendorff into Munich; police opened fire and 16 Nazis died; Hitler arrested
Why did the Munich Putsch fail? :: Bavarian leaders (Kahr, Lossow, Seisser) withdrew support; the army and police stayed loyal; poor planning
What were the consequences of the Munich Putsch? :: Party banned; Hitler jailed in Landsberg; trial gave national publicity; wrote Mein Kampf; decided to win power legally through elections
What ideas did Hitler set out in Mein Kampf? :: Aryan racial superiority, anti-Semitism, Lebensraum (living space in the east), destroying Versailles, hatred of communism, the leader principle
Why did the Nazis have limited support 1924–28? :: Stresemann's recovery made people more content; Hitler in prison and banned from public speaking; party divided; Nazis won only 2.6% in 1928
What was decided at the Bamberg Conference (1926)? :: Hitler overcame Gregor Strasser's socialist wing and established the Führerprinzip (leader principle) – his total control of the party
How was the Nazi Party reorganised after 1925? :: Divided into regions (Gaue) each led by a Gauleiter; Hitler Youth (1926); SS (1925) as Hitler's bodyguard; Goebbels in charge of propaganda in Berlin
C: The Wall Street Crash happened in {{c1::October 1929}}.
Why did the Wall Street Crash hit Germany so hard? :: American banks recalled loans; German businesses collapsed; exports fell; unemployment soared
How did unemployment affect German society 1929–32? :: Poverty, homelessness, hunger; young people and the middle classes lost hope; people turned to extremist parties
Why did the Weimar governments fail to deal with unemployment 1929–32? :: Coalition collapsed (1930) over unemployment benefit; Brüning cut benefits, wages and spending and raised taxes ('hunger chancellor'); relied on Article 48 rather than the Reichstag
Who was Brüning? :: Centre Party Chancellor (March 1930–May 1932) who ruled by presidential decree; his austerity worsened the Depression
How did Communist support grow 1928–32? :: KPD vote rose from about 11% (1928) to about 17% (November 1932), winning 100 seats – frightened the middle classes and businessmen
How many Reichstag seats did the Nazis win 1928–32? :: 1928: 12 ;; September 1930: 107 ;; July 1932: 230 (largest party) ;; November 1932: 196
What was Hitler's appeal? :: Seen as a strong, decisive leader who could restore Germany; powerful speaker; offered simple solutions; flew around Germany to campaign in 1932
How did Nazi propaganda win support 1929–32? :: Goebbels used posters, rallies, films, radio and newspapers (Hugenberg's press); simple slogans ('Work and Bread'); targeted different groups
Which groups did the Nazis promise something to? :: Workers – jobs ;; Farmers – help with debt ;; Middle classes and businessmen – protection from communism ;; Nationalists – destroy Versailles
How did the SA help the Nazis win support? :: Gave an image of order and discipline; fought communists in the streets; disrupted opponents' meetings; gave unemployed men food and uniform
What happened in the 1932 presidential election? :: Hindenburg won (about 53%) but Hitler came second (about 37%) – showed his national appeal
Why did Brüning fall in May 1932? :: Hindenburg lost confidence in him (he had banned the SA and failed to cure unemployment) – von Schleicher plotted against him
Who was von Papen? :: Chancellor June–November 1932 with little Reichstag support; lifted the SA ban; called July 1932 election; later persuaded Hindenburg to appoint Hitler
What happened in August 1932? :: Hitler demanded to be Chancellor; Hindenburg refused and offered him Vice-Chancellor, which he rejected
Who was von Schleicher? :: General and Chancellor December 1932–January 1933; tried to split the Nazis by offering Gregor Strasser a post; failed and lost Hindenburg's support
C: Hitler was appointed Chancellor on {{c1::30 January 1933}} with {{c2::von Papen}} as Vice-Chancellor.
How many Nazis were in Hitler's first cabinet? :: Three – Hitler, Frick and Göring – so Papen believed they could control him
Explain the role of Hindenburg and von Papen in Hitler becoming Chancellor. :: Papen wanted revenge on Schleicher and power, so did a deal with Hitler ;; Hindenburg disliked Hitler but was persuaded he could be controlled with few Nazis in cabinet ;; Both feared communism and wanted a majority government
Explain why the Nazis gained support 1929–32. :: Depression and 6 million unemployed made Weimar seem weak ;; Hitler's leadership and Goebbels' propaganda promised something for everyone ;; Fear of communism – SA seen as protection
## Key topic 3: Nazi control and dictatorship 1933–39
C: The Reichstag Fire happened on {{c1::27 February 1933}}.
What did the Decree for the Protection of the People and the State do? :: Suspended civil rights (free speech, press, assembly); allowed arrest without trial – about 4 000 communists arrested
What did the Nazis win in the March 1933 election? :: About 44% of the vote (288 seats) – not a majority on their own
C: The Enabling Act was passed on {{c1::23 March 1933}} by {{c2::444 votes to 94}}.
How did Hitler get the Enabling Act passed? :: Communist deputies arrested or barred; SA surrounded the Kroll Opera House; the Centre Party was promised the Church would be protected; only the SPD voted against
C: Trade unions were banned on {{c1::2 May 1933}} and replaced by the German Labour Front.
C: The Law against the Formation of New Parties of {{c1::July 1933}} made Germany a one-party state.
How did Hitler take control of local government? :: State parliaments abolished (January 1934); Nazi Reich governors ran the states
Why was Röhm and the SA a threat to Hitler by 1934? :: SA had about 3 million members; Röhm wanted the SA to absorb the army and a 'second revolution'; army generals and businessmen were alarmed – Hitler needed the army
C: The Night of the Long Knives took place on {{c1::30 June 1934}}.
Who was killed in the Night of the Long Knives besides Röhm? :: SA leaders and other opponents including von Schleicher, Gregor Strasser and von Kahr
What were the consequences of the Night of the Long Knives? :: Removed the SA threat; won army support; SS became more powerful; showed Hitler would kill opponents – terror
C: Hindenburg died on {{c1::2 August 1934}}; the army then swore an oath of loyalty to {{c2::Hitler personally}}.
Explain why Hitler was able to create a dictatorship 1933–34. :: Reichstag Fire decree let him arrest opponents ;; Enabling Act gave him power to make laws ;; Night of the Long Knives and the army oath removed rivals and secured the army
Who led the SS and what did it do? :: Heinrich Himmler (from 1929) ;; Elite black-uniformed force; arrested, imprisoned and killed opponents; ran concentration camps (Death's Head units)
What was the SD? :: Sicherheitsdienst – the SS intelligence service led by Reinhard Heydrich; spied on opponents and party members
What was the Gestapo? :: Secret state police set up by Göring (1933), later under Himmler and Heydrich; could arrest without trial; relied heavily on informers
What were block wardens? :: Local Nazi officials who watched households in their block and reported disloyalty
C: The first concentration camp was set up at {{c1::Dachau}} in {{c2::March 1933}}.
Who was sent to concentration camps in the 1930s? :: Political opponents (communists, socialists), trade unionists, church leaders, Jews, homosexuals, 'asocials' – often held in 'protective custody' without trial
How did the Nazis control judges and the courts? :: Judges had to join the National Socialist League for the Maintenance of Law; disloyal judges sacked; the People's Court (1934) tried treason cases; the death penalty applied to more crimes
What was the People's Court? :: Set up in 1934 to try treason; judges were loyal Nazis and there was no jury – most defendants were found guilty
C: The Nazis signed a Concordat with the Pope in {{c1::July 1933}}.
What did the Concordat agree? :: Nazis would not interfere with the Catholic Church; the Church would stay out of politics
How did the Nazis break the Concordat? :: Priests harassed and arrested; Catholic schools closed; Catholic Youth banned
What was 'Mit brennender Sorge' (1937)? :: The Pope's letter ('With burning anxiety') criticising Nazi treatment of the Church – read in Catholic churches
Who were the 'German Christians'? :: Protestants who supported the Nazis, led by Ludwig Müller – became Reich Bishop of the Reich Church (1933)
What was the Confessing Church? :: Protestant church set up (1934) by Pastor Niemöller and Dietrich Bonhoeffer against Nazi control of religion – in 1937 over 800 pastors were arrested
Who was Joseph Goebbels? :: Head of the Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda (set up March 1933); controlled media and culture
What was the Reich Chamber of Culture? :: Set up in 1933 – artists, writers, musicians, journalists and film-makers had to be members, so the Nazis controlled culture
C: The Nazis burned books by Jewish and communist authors in Berlin in {{c1::May 1933}}.
How did the Nazis control newspapers? :: Banned opposition papers; editors had to follow Nazi instructions (Editors' Law 1933); news came from the state press agency
How did the Nazis use radio? :: Cheap 'People's Receivers' (by 1939 about 70% of households had a radio); loudspeakers in streets and workplaces; Hitler's speeches broadcast
Name a famous Nazi propaganda film and its director. :: 'Triumph of the Will' (1935) about the 1934 Nuremberg Rally, directed by Leni Riefenstahl
What were the Nuremberg Rallies? :: Huge annual Nazi Party rallies with marches, speeches, searchlights – showed power and unity
How were the 1936 Berlin Olympics used for propaganda? :: Showed off Germany's efficiency and Aryan strength; Germany won the most medals; anti-Jewish signs temporarily removed ;; But black American Jesse Owens won 4 gold medals
What was 'degenerate art'? :: Modern art (e.g. Expressionism) the Nazis banned and mocked in a 1937 exhibition in Munich
What art did the Nazis approve of? :: Realistic, heroic images of Aryan families, workers, soldiers and peasants
What was Nazi architecture like? :: Huge, classical-style buildings of stone (Albert Speer), e.g. the Nuremberg rally grounds – showed power; Bauhaus was closed (1933)
How did the Nazis control music and literature? :: Jazz banned as 'black' music; Wagner promoted; books by Jewish, communist or anti-war writers banned (e.g. 'All Quiet on the Western Front')
Why did many Germans support the Nazi regime? :: Jobs and economic recovery; order restored; pride in rearmament and foreign successes (Rhineland 1936); effective propaganda; fear of the police state
Who was Pastor Martin Niemöller? :: A WW1 U-boat commander who at first supported the Nazis but opposed Nazi control of the Protestant Church; set up the Pastors' Emergency League and the Confessing Church; in concentration camps 1937–45
Who were the Swing Youth? :: Mainly middle-class young people who listened and danced to American jazz and swing, dressed in Western fashion and rejected the Hitler Youth
Who were the Edelweiss Pirates? :: Working-class teenagers (late 1930s onwards) who wore an edelweiss badge, went hiking and camping, sang anti-Nazi songs and sometimes attacked Hitler Youth patrols
How significant was opposition to the Nazis before 1939? :: Limited – most people conformed through fear or support; Churches mostly defended their own interests; youth groups rebelled more in culture than politics
Explain why the Nazis were able to control Germany 1933–39. :: Police state – SS, Gestapo and camps spread fear ;; Propaganda and censorship under Goebbels shaped opinion ;; Popular policies (jobs, national pride) won support
## Key topic 4: Life in Nazi Germany 1933–39
What were Nazi views on women and the family? :: Women's role was to be wives and mothers producing healthy Aryan children; the family was the basis of the nation
What did the Law for the Encouragement of Marriage (1933) offer? :: A loan of 1 000 marks to newly married couples if the wife gave up work; a quarter cancelled for each child born
What was the Mother's Cross? :: Medal for mothers with many children – bronze (4–5), silver (6–7), gold (8+); awarded on Hitler's mother's birthday
What was Lebensborn? :: SS programme (1935) where unmarried 'racially pure' women could have children with SS men
How did the Nazis change women's employment? :: Women doctors, teachers and civil servants dismissed (from 1933); women banned from being judges or lawyers (1936)
Why did Nazi policy on women's work change after 1937? :: Rearmament caused labour shortages, so women were encouraged back into work; marriage loan rules relaxed
How did the Nazis want women to look? :: Natural – no make-up or trousers, hair in a bun or plaits, flat shoes; not slim (to be healthy mothers); not to smoke
What did the 1938 divorce law allow? :: A man could divorce his wife if she could not have children
Did Nazi women's policies succeed? :: Partly – marriages and births rose, but the number of working women rose after 1937 because the economy needed them
What were the Nazi aims for young people? :: Loyal to Hitler; boys fit and ready to be soldiers; girls ready to be mothers; believe in Nazi racial ideas
C: The Hitler Youth Law of {{c1::1936}} banned other youth groups; membership became compulsory in {{c2::1939}}.
What were the youth organisations for boys and girls? :: Boys – German Young People (10–14), Hitler Youth (14–18) ;; Girls – Young Girls (10–14), League of German Maidens (14–18)
Who led the Hitler Youth? :: Baldur von Schirach
What did boys do in the Hitler Youth? :: Sport, hiking and camping, military training (marching, map reading, shooting), Nazi ideas
What did girls do in the League of German Maidens? :: Sport, cooking, sewing and childcare to prepare for motherhood; learning Nazi ideas
How large was the Hitler Youth by 1939? :: About 7–8 million members (from about 2 million in 1933)
How did the Nazis control teachers? :: Teachers had to join the National Socialist Teachers' League (nearly all did by 1937) and swear loyalty to Hitler; Jewish and disloyal teachers sacked
How did the Nazis change the school curriculum? :: History – German glory and Versailles injustice ;; Biology – race science ;; Geography – Lebensraum ;; More PE (15% of time) ;; Religious education dropped ;; Girls studied domestic science
What were Adolf Hitler Schools and Napolas? :: Elite schools training future Nazi leaders – mainly physical and ideological training
Explain why the Nazis controlled young people. :: Young people were the future of the 'Thousand Year Reich' ;; Needed loyal soldiers and mothers ;; Easier to shape their beliefs through school and the Hitler Youth
C: Unemployment fell from about {{c1::6 million}} in 1933 to about {{c2::0.3 million}} in 1939.
What was the National Labour Service (RAD)? :: From 1935 young men 18–25 had to do 6 months' work (e.g. farming, building) in uniform, living in camps, for low pay
How did building autobahns reduce unemployment? :: Began 1933; employed about 125 000 men; 3 000 km built by 1938
How did rearmament reduce unemployment? :: Factories made weapons, tanks and planes; the Four-Year Plan (1936, Göring) prepared the economy for war
C: Conscription was introduced in {{c1::1935}}; the army grew to about {{c2::1.4 million}} by 1939.
What was invisible unemployment? :: Groups not counted in figures – Jews dismissed from jobs, women who left work, men in labour camps or the RAD, part-time workers counted as full-time
What was the German Labour Front (DAF)? :: Replaced trade unions (1933), led by Robert Ley; set wages and hours; strikes banned
How did working hours and wages change under the Nazis? :: Average working hours increased; wages rose slightly but food prices rose too, so real wages barely improved
What did Strength Through Joy (KdF) offer? :: Cheap holidays and cruises, theatre and concert trips, sports – rewards to keep workers happy
What was the Volkswagen scheme? :: Workers paid 5 marks a week to save for a 'people's car' – none were delivered as the factory switched to war production
What did Beauty of Labour (SdA) do? :: Improved workplaces – canteens, toilets, lighting, sports facilities – often built by workers in their own time
Did living standards improve under the Nazis? :: Mixed – jobs and leisure (KdF) for many and more consumer goods; but longer hours, no unions, fewer freedoms, food prices up
What was the Nazi idea of the Aryan master race? :: Germans (Aryans) were the superior race (Herrenvolk); others such as Jews, Slavs and Roma were 'subhuman' (Untermenschen)
What was the Volksgemeinschaft? :: The 'people's community' – a united, racially pure Germany where individuals put the nation first
Why did the Nazis persecute Slavs? :: Seen as subhuman; their land in the east was wanted for Lebensraum
How were Roma and Sinti ('gypsies') persecuted? :: Nuremberg Laws applied to them; registration (1938 decree); sent to camps; banned from marrying Germans
How were homosexuals persecuted? :: Seen as a threat to the birth rate; laws strengthened (1935); thousands sent to concentration camps (pink triangle)
How were disabled people persecuted? :: Sterilisation Law (July 1933) – hundreds of thousands forcibly sterilised; from 1939 a 'euthanasia' programme murdered disabled children and adults
C: The Nazi boycott of Jewish shops and businesses took place on {{c1::1 April 1933}}.
How did the boycott of Jewish shops work? :: SA men stood outside Jewish shops and businesses, painted Stars of David and told people not to enter – limited support from the public
What other anti-Jewish measures came in 1933? :: Jews dismissed from the civil service and teaching; banned from some professions
C: The Nuremberg Laws were passed in {{c1::September 1935}}.
What were the two Nuremberg Laws? :: Reich Citizenship Law – Jews became subjects not citizens (lost rights) ;; Law for the Protection of German Blood and Honour – banned marriage and sexual relations between Jews and Germans
What triggered Kristallnacht? :: A young Polish Jew, Herschel Grynszpan, shot a German diplomat (Ernst vom Rath) in Paris – Goebbels used it as an excuse
C: Kristallnacht happened on {{c1::9–10 November 1938}}.
What were the consequences of Kristallnacht? :: About 100 Jews killed, thousands of shops and synagogues destroyed; 20 000+ sent to camps; Jews fined 1 billion marks; Jewish children banned from schools; Jewish businesses closed
How did persecution of Jews increase 1933–39? :: Boycott (1933) → removal from jobs → Nuremberg Laws (1935) → Kristallnacht (1938) → forced emigration and exclusion from economic life (1939)
Explain why the Nazis persecuted minorities. :: Racial beliefs – Aryan superiority and anti-Semitism ;; Scapegoats for Germany's problems (Versailles, Depression) ;; Wanted a pure Volksgemeinschaft and to strengthen the race (eugenics)

# Exam technique
## Overview of the papers
What are the three Edexcel GCSE History papers and their weightings? :: Paper 1 Thematic study + historic environment (30%, 1h 15m, 52 marks) ;; Paper 2 Period study + British depth study (40%, 1h 45m, 64 marks) ;; Paper 3 Modern depth study (30%, 1h 20m, 52 marks)
On which questions are SPaG marks awarded? :: The final 16-mark essay on Paper 1 (Q5/6) and on Paper 3 (Q3d) – 4 extra marks each for spelling, punctuation, grammar and specialist terms
How should you plan your time in the exam? :: Roughly a minute per mark (plus reading time) – don't overrun on short questions; leave the most time for the 12- and 16-mark questions
What are the four levels for a 12-mark 'explain why' question? :: Level 1 (1–3), Level 2 (4–6), Level 3 (7–9), Level 4 (10–12)
What are the four levels for a 16-mark essay? :: Level 1 (1–4), Level 2 (5–8), Level 3 (9–12), Level 4 (13–16)
Why must you go beyond the stimulus points in 12- and 16-mark questions? :: Answers that only use the given bullet points are capped (at Level 3) – you must add at least one point of your own to reach Level 4
What makes an explanation 'developed'? :: Make a point, support it with specific evidence (dates, names, numbers), then explain how it links to the question ('This meant that…')
What is the PEEL structure? :: Point – Evidence – Explain – Link back to the question
What do examiners mean by 'analysis'? :: Explaining how and why factors caused or affected something, not just describing events
What is a sustained line of reasoning? :: An answer where every paragraph builds the same argument towards a clear judgement
## Paper 1: Medicine and the Western Front
What questions are in Section A (historic environment) of Paper 1? :: Q1 Describe two features (4) ;; Q2(a) How useful are Sources A and B (8) ;; Q2(b) How could you follow up Source B (4)
How is 'Describe two features of…' (4 marks) marked? :: For each of two features: 1 mark for identifying a valid feature + 1 mark for supporting detail
How do you answer 'How useful are Sources A and B for an enquiry into…?' (8 marks)? :: For each source: what its content shows about the enquiry, how its provenance (nature, origin, purpose) affects usefulness, and own contextual knowledge to test it – judge usefulness for the specific enquiry
What are the levels for the 8-mark usefulness question? :: Level 1 (1–2) simple comments ;; Level 2 (3–5) judgements on content and/or provenance with some context ;; Level 3 (6–8) judgements using content AND provenance and contextual knowledge
What common mistakes lose marks in source usefulness answers? :: Saying a source is useless because it is biased; describing content without judging it; ignoring provenance; not using own knowledge; not linking to the enquiry
What are the four parts of the follow-up question (Q2b, 4 marks)? :: Detail in the source I would follow up (1) ;; Question I would ask (1) ;; Type of source I could use (1) ;; How this might help answer my question (1)
What makes a good answer to the follow-up question? :: Pick a specific detail linked to the enquiry; ask a precise question; name a specific type of source (e.g. RAMC records); explain exactly what information it would give
What questions are in Section B (thematic study) of Paper 1? :: Q3 Explain one way in which… were similar/different (4) ;; Q4 Explain why… (12) ;; Q5 or Q6 'How far do you agree?' essay (16 + 4 SPaG)
How do you answer 'Explain one way in which… were similar/different' (4 marks)? :: State one clear similarity or difference, then support it with specific knowledge of BOTH periods (e.g. 'Both the Black Death and the Great Plague were blamed on miasma: in 1348… in 1665…')
How do you answer the 12-mark 'Explain why…' question? :: Write three developed paragraphs – the two stimulus points plus one of your own – each explaining how that factor caused the change/continuity
How do you answer the 16-mark 'How far do you agree?' essay? :: Paragraphs agreeing and disagreeing (using the stimulus points and your own knowledge), then a conclusion giving a clear judgement with a reason (e.g. which factor was most important)
What are the criteria for judgement in a 16-mark essay? :: The reason for your overall judgement – e.g. which factor had the longest-lasting effect or affected the most people
What concepts are tested in Paper 1 Section B? :: Change and continuity, causation, similarity and difference, significance, and the role of factors across 1250–present
## Paper 2: Elizabethan England (British depth study)
What questions are in the Elizabethan section of Paper 2? :: (a) Describe two features (4) ;; (b) Explain why (12) ;; (c) choice of two 'How far do you agree?' questions (16) – no SPaG
How is the Elizabethan (a) 'Describe two features' marked? :: 1 mark for each feature identified + 1 for supporting detail, up to 4
How do you answer the Elizabethan (b) 12-mark 'Explain why…'? :: Three developed paragraphs explaining causes, using the two stimulus points and at least one of your own
How do you answer the Elizabethan (c) 16-mark question? :: Choose one statement; argue for and against with specific evidence and your own points; finish with a clear, justified judgement
## Paper 2: Cold War (period study)
What questions are in the Cold War section of Paper 2? :: Q1 Explain two consequences (8) ;; Q2 Write a narrative account analysing… (8) ;; Q3 Explain the importance of two of three events/developments (16)
How is 'Explain two consequences of…' (8 marks) marked? :: 4 marks per consequence – identify the consequence and support it with specific detail explaining how it resulted from the event
How do you answer 'Write a narrative account analysing…' (8 marks)? :: Tell the events in chronological order, linking each to the next with causation ('this led to…'), using the stimulus points and your own knowledge; have a clear beginning, middle and end
What are the levels for the narrative account? :: Level 1 (1–2), Level 2 (3–5), Level 3 (6–8) – Level 3 needs a well-organised analytical narrative with clear links between events
How do you answer 'Explain the importance of…' (16 marks: 2 × 8)? :: Choose two of the three; for each explain HOW and WHY it mattered for the topic in the question (e.g. relations between the superpowers) – consequences, short and long term
What are the levels for each 8-mark 'importance' answer? :: Level 1 (1–2), Level 2 (3–5), Level 3 (6–8) – Level 3 explains importance with accurate, relevant knowledge
## Paper 3: Weimar and Nazi Germany
What questions are in Section A of Paper 3? :: Q1 Give two things you can infer from Source A (4) ;; Q2 Explain why… (12)
How is the inference question (4 marks) marked? :: For each of two inferences: 1 mark for a valid inference + 1 for a supporting detail quoted from the source
What is an inference? :: Something the source suggests but does not directly say – 'This suggests that…'
What questions are in Section B of Paper 3? :: Q3(a) How useful are Sources B and C (8) ;; (b) What is the main difference between Interpretations 1 and 2 (4) ;; (c) Suggest one reason why they differ (4) ;; (d) How far do you agree with Interpretation X (16 + 4 SPaG)
How do you answer 'What is the main difference between the views?' (4 marks)? :: Identify the overall difference in the historians' views (not small details) and support it with a brief quotation from each interpretation
How do you answer 'Suggest one reason why Interpretations 1 and 2 give different views' (4 marks)? :: Explain a reason such as: they emphasise different aspects, use different sources (e.g. Sources B and C may support each), or the extracts are partial – support with details
How do you answer 'How far do you agree with Interpretation 2?' (16 + 4 marks)? :: Explain the view of Interpretation 2; use the other interpretation and your own knowledge to agree and challenge; reach a clear judgement on how far you agree
What is the difference between a source and an interpretation? :: A source is evidence from the time (contemporary); an interpretation is a later view by a historian of what happened
